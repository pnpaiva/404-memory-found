Is MiniDisc Still Made? Sony Ended Discs in 2025

2026-04-07 | Updated 2026-09-21 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

MiniDisc is no longer manufactured. Sony sold the last MiniDisc hardware in March 2013 and announced in January 2025 that it would end production of recordable MiniDisc media the following month, with no replacement. The format now lives entirely in the second-hand market, where a clean recorder runs roughly $100 to $400.

What problem was MiniDisc built to solve?

Sony spent the late 1980s watching two formats split the job of playing music. The compact disc sounded clean and jumped to any track instantly. The cassette could be recorded at home, over and over, for almost nothing. Neither one did both.

MiniDisc was Sony's answer. It was a magneto-optical disc, rewritable thousands of times, sealed inside a plastic shell so fingers never touched the surface, holding 74 minutes of audio on a disc small enough to lose in a coat pocket. A memory buffer read ahead of the laser so the music kept playing when the player was knocked, which is the one thing portable CD players could never manage.

Sony announced the format at a press conference in May 1991. "We understand consumer needs, and we put our technology to work to meet those needs," said Norio Ohga, then Sony's president and chief executive, in the company's 1991 announcement.

The audio was compressed to fit. ATRAC, Sony's own codec, discarded the sound a listener was least likely to notice was gone. It was good work, and it was lossy, and both of those facts followed MiniDisc for the rest of its life. The first players reached Japanese shops in late 1992 and American ones that December.

How big did MiniDisc actually get in Japan?

Big enough to beat the CD player on its home turf. Japanese buyers took about 1,080,000 MiniDisc machines and 10 million blank discs in 1995 alone. By 1997, MiniDisc player sales in Japan had overtaken CD player sales, with roughly 5 million units sold there against about 1 million in the United States. Worldwide hardware sales hit 6.3 million units in 1998, alongside 60 million blank discs.

The reasons were local and specific. Japanese commuters spent hours a day on trains, where a skipping portable CD player was a daily irritation. Home recording from rented CDs was normal and legal, and MiniDisc did it faster and cleaner than a cassette deck, with track names typed onto the little screen.

The part that mattered most was content. Japanese record labels pressed albums onto pre-recorded MiniDiscs and sold them next to the CDs, on release day. That closed the loop: music in the format sold players, and players sold labels on pressing more music. No other market ever got that far.

Why did MiniDisc flop in the United States?

Two numbers explain most of it. A MiniDisc recorder cost $750 at the American launch in December 1992, and Sony sold fewer than 50,000 of them in the first twelve months. Sony aimed the marketing at young music buyers who could not possibly pay $750 for a music player.

American buyers already had an answer that worked. CDs were everywhere, and recordable CD-R blanks were heading toward pocket change. MiniDisc asked them to pay several times as much for a smaller disc that held less and compressed the audio. The only argument that could have won was a rack of pre-recorded albums in the store, and the major American labels never pressed them. They saw a proprietary Sony format, they distrusted the compression, and they had no reason to split a CD market that was printing money.

So MiniDisc stayed where it started: a format for people who recorded their own music. That is a real audience, and a small one. Sony had been on the losing side of this arithmetic before, when Betamax lost to VHS on availability rather than quality.

Did the copy protection drive away the people who wanted it most?

MiniDisc shipped with the Serial Copy Management System. A digital recording made from a CD onto a MiniDisc was allowed. A digital copy of that MiniDisc onto another MiniDisc was not. One generation, then the chain stopped.

Sony built that restriction to reassure record labels, and it mostly annoyed the enthusiasts who were buying the hardware. The people willing to spend real money on a recording format were exactly the people who wanted to move their recordings around freely.

The fixes that followed kept the restrictions. NetMD in 2001 added a USB cable and Sony's SonicStage software, which is the moment MiniDisc started arguing against itself: if the music already lived on a computer, why move it onto a proprietary disc instead of a player that took the files as they were? Hi-MD in 2004 raised the capacity and finally allowed uncompressed recording, which was the right feature several years late. By then the iPod had already settled the question of where portable music would live.

Is MiniDisc still made in 2026?

No. Sony sold the last MiniDisc players in March 2013, more than twenty years after the format launched. Blank media outlived the hardware by a decade, because Sony kept pressing recordable discs for the Japanese market long after nobody was selling new decks.

That ended too. On 23 January 2025 Sony announced it would stop producing recordable MiniDisc media, MD Data discs, MiniDV cassettes and recordable Blu-ray the following month. The company's statement left no ambiguity: "There will be no successor models." Production of blank MiniDiscs stopped in February 2025, and since Sony was the last manufacturer, the world now has a fixed and shrinking supply of blank discs.

Ownership has not changed hands. Sony still holds the MiniDisc format and trademark, which makes this different from the brands that got sold off in bankruptcy. Sony simply stopped selling anything for it. What keeps the format usable is community work rather than corporate support: Web MiniDisc Pro, a browser tool built on WebUSB, now does what SonicStage did, without installing Sony's abandoned software on a modern machine.

MiniDisc also turns up in places nobody planned. In June 2019, 18 hours of Radiohead recordings from the OK Computer era leaked after, as guitarist Jonny Greenwood put it to the press that month, "someone stole Thom's minidisk archive from around the time of OK Computer." The band released the whole archive rather than pay the ransom.

Where to find one today

Only used, and condition is most of the price. A well kept portable recorder typically runs $100 to $400 in 2026, while a unit with a dim display or a grinding transport sells for a fraction of that, because the repair costs more than the player is worth. Most pre-recorded MiniDisc albums still change hands for under $100, though sealed Japanese pressings by major artists have reached four figures at auction. Blank discs keep climbing now that production has stopped. Sellers list players, blanks and pre-recorded discs on Etsy. Test the transport and the headphone output before you commit to anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are MiniDiscs still being made in 2026?

No. Sony announced on 23 January 2025 that it would end production of recordable MiniDisc media in February 2025, and it was the last manufacturer still making them. Sony had already stopped selling MiniDisc players in March 2013, so as of 2026 no new MiniDisc hardware or blank media is being produced by anyone.

How much does a MiniDisc player cost in 2026?

A working used MiniDisc recorder generally costs $100 to $400 in 2026, depending on the model and its condition, and units with worn transports or failing screens sell for much less. No new MiniDisc players have been built since Sony ended production in March 2013, so every unit on the market is second-hand.

Does MiniDisc sound as good as a CD?

Standard MiniDisc does not, because it uses Sony's lossy ATRAC compression and permanently discards part of the audio during recording. ATRAC was tuned so that most listeners would not notice the loss on portable equipment, and Hi-MD, which Sony introduced in 2004, did allow uncompressed recording at full CD quality.
