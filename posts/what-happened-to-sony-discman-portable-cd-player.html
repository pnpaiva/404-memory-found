Is the Sony Discman Still Made? What One Costs Now

2026-05-06 | Updated 2026-09-23 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

Sony stopped making the Sony Discman around 2010, having renamed the line CD Walkman in 2000. The brand is still Sony's, but it is attached to digital Walkman players now, and no new Sony portable CD player has been manufactured in roughly fifteen years. A working 1984 D-50, the first portable CD player ever sold, trades for $100 to $400 in the collector market.

Why Sony shrank the CD player to the size of four jewel cases

Sony and Philips put the compact disc on sale in 1982, and the first players were pieces of furniture. Sony's General Audio Division answered with a physical target instead of a feature list: a machine no larger than four CD jewel cases stacked together, about 13 centimeters across and 4 centimeters thick. The engineering team was handed a block of wood cut to those dimensions and told to build a CD player that fit inside it.

The result went on sale in Japan in November 1984 as the D-50, priced at 49,800 yen, roughly $350 in the United States at the time. British buyers paid £279.99. The Victoria and Albert Museum in London, which holds one in its permanent collection, records in its catalogue entry that "At the time of its release it was the world's smallest and most affordable CD-player", and notes that its example cost the original owner close to 70 percent of a month's wages.

The official name on the paperwork was "compact disc compact player". Discman was the marketing nickname, lifted from the Walkman, and it outlived the official one by decades.

How Sony fixed the skipping, and why the fix arrived too late

A portable CD player reads data off a spinning disc with a laser, and any jolt knocks that laser off its track. That is the entire reason a generation rode in back seats with a thumb pressed on the lid. Sony's answer was Electronic Skip Protection, sold as ESP from the mid-1990s. The player read ahead of the music and parked the next few seconds of audio in RAM, then played out of that buffer while the laser found its place again.

First-generation ESP held roughly three to ten seconds and compressed the buffered audio, because memory was expensive and those players carried only 32 or 64 kilobytes of it. Later models held ten to forty-eight seconds without compression, and Sony branded its own version G-Protection. By the early 2000s a CD Walkman could survive a jog without stuttering.

The timing was awful. The complaint that had defined portable CD audio for a decade got solved at the exact moment discs stopped being how people carried music. Sony had already hedged once with the MiniDisc, a recordable format that sold well in Japan and went almost nowhere in the United States.

When did the Discman become the CD Walkman?

Sony started retiring the Discman name at home first, on Japanese models launched between October 1997 and March 1998, then dropped it everywhere in 2000. Every portable product moved under the Walkman umbrella, so players sold after that carry "CD Walkman" on the lid and in the manual.

Nobody changed how they talked. The name had gone generic the way Kleenex did, and buyers kept walking into stores asking for a Discman for another decade. Sony's own support pages and service manuals from the 2000s use the new name; the people carrying the things did not.

The rename landed near the top of the market. CD revenue in the United States peaked at the end of the 1990s, and then file sharing and the download store pulled it apart, a collapse traced in detail in our look at what Napster did to music sales. Sony kept shipping CD players through the second half of the 2000s, but by then the volume had moved to flash memory and hard drives.

Who owns the Discman name today?

Sony Group Corporation does. Discman and Walkman are Sony trademarks, and neither has been sold off or licensed to another company the way some dead electronics brands were. What changed is what the names are attached to. Sony wound down portable CD player production around 2010, and Walkman in 2026 means a digital audio player that reads files from internal storage or a memory card. No new Sony portable CD player has been manufactured in roughly fifteen years, and the company has never announced a revival.

The machine that ended the run arrived on October 23, 2001. Apple's announcement that day described a player that "packs up to 1,000 CD-quality songs" into a 6.5-ounce case, and Steve Jobs said in the same press release that "With iPod, Apple has invented a whole new category of digital music player that lets you put your entire music collection in your pocket". The first iPod cost $399, worked only with Macs and reached stores on November 10, 2001.

It was not the first device to hold music as files, as our piece on the MP3 players that came before the iPod sets out. It was the one that made a binder of discs feel like luggage.

Can you buy a new portable CD player in 2026?

Not one built by Sony. The category never fully closed, though. Budget electronics brands still ship cheap personal CD players and boomboxes, and the audio specialist FiiO sells a modern portable CD player, the DM13, with Bluetooth output and USB ripping built in. These are niche products aimed at collectors and audio hobbyists, not the mass-market gear that filled school hallways.

The discs sit in a similar spot. The RIAA's year-end report for 2025 put United States CD revenue at $312.4 million on 29.5 million units, down 11.6 percent from the year before, while vinyl took $1.04 billion from 46.8 million records. CDs still sell in real numbers. They are simply no longer the format a portable player has to support.

Where to find one today. Working players come almost entirely from the resale market, and the 1984 original is the expensive one: BGR's 2025 survey of 1980s electronics put collector prices for a working D-50 at $100 to $400, with clean, serviced units that still have the box and manual asking more. Ordinary 1990s and 2000s CD Walkman models cost far less, because Sony made millions of them and they were built to be dropped. Sellers who specialize in tested vintage audio list them on Etsy's Sony Discman search page. Check the lid switch and the battery contacts before buying, since those fail more often than the laser does.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did Sony stop making the Sony Discman?

Sony retired the Discman name in 2000 and sold the same players as the CD Walkman after that, then wound down portable CD player manufacturing around 2010. No Sony portable CD player has been built since, and the Walkman name now belongs to Sony's digital audio players.

How much is an original 1984 Sony Discman worth?

The Sony D-50, the first Discman, launched in November 1984 at 49,800 yen in Japan, about $350 in the United States. BGR reported in 2025 that collectors pay roughly $100 to $400 for a working D-50, with serviced examples that keep the original box and accessories at the top of that range.

What replaced the Sony Discman?

Hard-drive and flash MP3 players replaced the Sony Discman, led by the Apple iPod, which was announced on October 23, 2001 at $399 and held 5GB, or about 1,000 songs. Smartphones and streaming services then replaced dedicated music players entirely, and Sony's own portable CD line ended around 2010.
