Is Discovery Zone Still Around? One Playground Left

2026-09-16 | Updated 2026-10-13 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Discovery Zone is not still around as the chain people remember. It filed for Chapter 11 in March 1996, filed again in April 1999, and its last locations closed by the end of 2001, with CEC Entertainment buying the trademarks in July 1999. In 2026 a single unaffiliated indoor playground uses the Discovery Zone name, at Florence Mall in Florence, Kentucky.

Who built Discovery Zone, and what was inside a FunCenter?

Discovery Zone was founded in 1989 by Ronald Matsch, Jim Jorgensen and David Schoenstadt. Wikipedia's account of the chain puts the first center in Kansas City, Missouri, in October 1989. The company called its locations FunCenters, and the layout barely changed over the next twelve years.

A FunCenter was a warehouse of padded tubes bolted to the ceiling, a ball pit deep enough to lose a shoe in, rope bridges, foam obstacle courses and a bank of arcade games that ran on plastic tokens. Parents waited behind glass in an area the company called the Quiet Zone.

The product was a birthday party with no weather, no grass stains and no setup, sold by the session rather than by the slice. Tennis champion Billie Jean King was an early investor and a public supporter of the chain, which sold physical play to girls and boys on identical terms.

The pitch landed in the right decade. American malls were still full, two-income households were normal, and an indoor playground that charged admission at the door looked like a business with almost no cost of goods sold.

How did Blockbuster turn a regional chain into a national one?

It paid for the buildout. In April 1993, Blockbuster Entertainment invested about $10.3 million in Discovery Zone and took roughly a fifth of the company, with accounts differing on whether the stake was 20 or 21 percent.

That money turned a regional operator into a national one in about eighteen months. In July 1994, Discovery Zone bought 45 Leaps and Bounds centers from McDonald's for a reported $111 million in stock, absorbing its only serious national rival in a single move, and it bought back FunCenters its own franchisees had built.

When Viacom acquired Blockbuster in September 1994, the Discovery Zone holding came along inside the package. The Baltimore Sun reported in March 1996 that "Viacom owns a 49 percent stake in Discovery Zone through its acquisition in 1994 of Blockbuster Entertainment Group." That is how a chain of ball pits ended up on a Hollywood conglomerate's balance sheet.

The chain reached close to 300 centers in the mid-1990s. Every one of them cost real money to open, because a FunCenter was a purpose-fitted big-box space rather than a storefront, and the buildout was paid for long before the first token went into a machine.

What did the 1996 bankruptcy filing actually show?

Discovery Zone filed for Chapter 11 protection in Wilmington, Delaware on March 26, 1996. Wikipedia's account of the petition puts the debts at up to $366.8 million. The Baltimore Sun reported that about $230 million of that was bank debt and subordinated debentures run up during the buying spree.

The trajectory behind those numbers was ugly. By the Sun's account, Discovery Zone lost $24.9 million in 1994, lost $114.4 million through the first three quarters of 1995, and fell into technical default on some of its loan covenants in September 1995.

The company blamed its own expansion. It said it had grown too fast, and that it would renegotiate rents on many of its sites and close the centers it could not fix. Its chief executive, Steven R. Berrard, had resigned the week before the filing, and Donna Moore took over as the case opened.

The demand problem underneath was never fixed. A FunCenter was busy on Saturday and Sunday and close to empty on a Tuesday afternoon, which meant the rent, the staff, the insurance and the cleaning ran seven days a week against revenue that arrived on two.

Why did the second bankruptcy end the chain?

Discovery Zone emerged from Chapter 11 on July 30, 1997 under Wellspring Associates, with Blockbuster no longer in control. Wellspring put a reported $20 million into refurbishing FunCenters from 1998, shrinking the Mega Zone play structure and the toddler area to make room for laser tag, a sports challenge area and a karaoke stage, and moving arts and crafts into the old Quiet Zone.

It did not work, because the fixtures were never the problem. Discovery Zone sold novelty, and novelty does not produce repeat visits the way food does. A family might book one party a year and stay away in between.

By May 1998 the chain was down to 203 locations. It filed for Chapter 11 a second time on April 20, 1999. Two months later it stopped pretending: on June 25, 1999 it closed 106 of its 128 remaining FunCenters at once, with the families holding party reservations given no warning at all.

A company spokesman defended the timing. "The abrupt closings were necessary to prevent any further hardships already being put upon our vendors and creditors," the spokesman said in a statement CNNfn reported on June 30, 1999. Discovery Zone died a few years ahead of Toys R Us, and for a version of the same reason: fixed costs sized for demand that never arrived.

Who owns the Discovery Zone name in 2026?

The trademark went to the owner of Chuck E. Cheese. In July 1999, CEC Entertainment paid about $19 million in cash for the chain's remaining property and, in the words of its annual report to the Securities and Exchange Commission, "all furniture, fixtures, equipment and intellectual properties owned by Discovery Zone, Inc." The same filing says CEC converted 10 of those sites into Chuck E. Cheese restaurants.

A bankruptcy judge converted the case to liquidation in June 2000, and the last Discovery Zone locations closed by the end of 2001. Nobody has rebuilt the chain since, and no national operator has tried the admission-only indoor playground at that scale again.

The name did come back, without the company. On February 7, 2020 an unaffiliated operator opened a Discovery Zone at Eastgate Mall in Union Township, Ohio, outside Cincinnati, and a second at Florence Mall in Florence, Kentucky on July 23, 2021. Community retail wikis say the Ohio site closed in 2025, and the operator's own website in 2026 markets only the Florence playground.

Florence sells timed play sessions rather than memberships. The operator's information page in 2026 lists $14 for a potty-trained child, $5 for the toddler zone and $2 per adult, and shows the playground open Friday through Sunday. It is a tribute built by strangers to the original company, which is the honest answer for anyone searching for the chain they remember from 1996.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Discovery Zone still around in 2026?

The original Discovery Zone chain is not. It closed its last locations by the end of 2001 after two bankruptcies, in March 1996 and April 1999. One indoor playground does trade under the Discovery Zone name in 2026, at Florence Mall in Florence, Kentucky, but it is run by an unaffiliated operator that revived the name in 2020, not by the company founded in 1989.

When did Discovery Zone close?

Discovery Zone closed in stages. It filed for Chapter 11 on March 26, 1996, emerged in July 1997, filed a second time on April 20, 1999, and shut 106 of its 128 remaining FunCenters without warning on June 25, 1999. A bankruptcy judge converted the case to liquidation in June 2000, and the last Discovery Zone locations closed by the end of 2001.

What happened to Leaps and Bounds, the McDonald's indoor playground?

Leaps and Bounds was McDonald's own chain of indoor play centers, and Discovery Zone bought 45 of them in July 1994 for a reported $111 million in stock. The Leaps and Bounds sites were rebranded as Discovery Zone FunCenters, so they went down inside the same collapse and were gone along with the rest of the chain by the end of 2001.
