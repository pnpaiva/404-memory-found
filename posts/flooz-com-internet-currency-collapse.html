Flooz.com Explained: The Dot-Com Cash That Vanished

2026-10-09 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Flooz.com sold an online gift currency from the fall of 1999 until August 2001, when it suspended its site and filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy. It raised about $35 million in venture capital, spent $8 million of that on a Whoopi Goldberg advertising campaign, and left unused flooz balances worthless and nonrefundable. Nothing has revived Flooz.com since, and its bankruptcy case did not close until July 2012.

How did Flooz actually work?

Flooz was a gift currency rather than a bank account. A buyer charged a dollar amount to a credit card, typed in a recipient, and Flooz.com emailed that person a balance to spend at the online stores in its network.

Mental Floss described the mechanics plainly: a shopper could charge $50 worth of flooz, email it to a friend, and the friend could spend it at a participating merchant. What the friend could not do was turn it back into dollars.

That one-way valve was the whole product. Flooz.com collected real money up front and issued a credit that only worked inside its own merchant list, which made the company responsible for keeping that list long enough to be useful.

It worked, briefly. Flooz.com had opened about 125,000 accounts by January 2000, a few months after its debut in the fall of 1999. For a currency invented by a startup, that was real traction.

The weakness was structural. A dollar is worth a dollar everywhere. A flooz was worth a dollar only at roughly 30 websites, and only for as long as the company behind it kept paying its bills.

What did the Whoopi Goldberg campaign cost?

Flooz.com spent $8 million on the advertising campaign that introduced the currency, with Whoopi Goldberg fronting television, radio and print.

Set that against the money the company had. Flooz.com raised about $35 million in venture capital across its life, so the launch campaign alone accounted for close to a quarter of everything investors ever put in.

The logic was not unreasonable. A new currency is worthless until people recognize it, and recognition in 1999 was bought on television. Goldberg gave an abstract product a face, and a fifteen-second spot explained it better than a press release could.

The problem was the second half of the equation. Awareness tells people flooz exists. It does not tell them where to spend it. Flooz.com was buying national attention for a currency accepted at a few dozen stores, and the gap between those two numbers never closed.

By the time the money ran low, the company was carrying a consumer brand far larger than the commerce underneath it.

Which stores took flooz, and why did they stop?

At its peak the currency was usable at about 30 retail websites, and the names on the list were real ones. Barnes & Noble, J. Crew and Tower Records all took flooz at checkout.

For a merchant the arrangement was cheap. The retailer picked up a customer holding credit that could only be spent somewhere inside the network, and Flooz.com handled the billing.

It ended without warning. On August 8, 2001 Flooz.com suspended its website and told the merchants in its network that it had stopped processing transactions. Shoppers who clicked through to pay with flooz found nothing on the other end.

That was the moment the currency stopped being a currency. A balance is spendable only while somebody accepts it, and a merchant network that can be switched off in an afternoon is not the same thing as money in a wallet.

Forrester analyst James Crawford was blunt about what the product had always been, telling the E-Commerce Times in 2001 that flooz was "essentially glorified loyalty points masquerading as cash."

How a credit-card ring in Russia drained the cash

While the merchant network was thinning, something worse was moving through the payment pipe. Over roughly three months in 2001, Flooz.com unknowingly sold about $300,000 of its currency to a ring of credit-card thieves operating out of Russia and the Philippines.

The method suited the product. Stolen card numbers bought flooz, the flooz moved by email, and the value came out the other side as merchandise. The FBI alerted the company to the scheme.

The fatal blow came from Flooz.com's own payment processor rather than from the thieves. Once Flooz.com charges began showing up on the statements of cardholders whose numbers had been stolen, the processor stopped sending Flooz.com its daily reimbursements and froze accounts until it was holding about $1 million of the company's money.

The Deseret News reported in 2001 that the freeze "created an untenable cash flow situation." A business that collects cash today and settles with merchants later cannot survive having its incoming cash held back.

Robert Levitan, the chief executive, blamed both the fraud and the dot-com slump for the decision to stop.

Is Flooz.com still around in 2026?

No. Flooz.com has been dead since August 2001, and nothing has revived it.

The sequence sits in the public record. Operations were suspended on August 8, 2001. The shutdown was reported as final on August 27, 2001. A Chapter 7 petition for Flooz.Com, Inc. was filed on August 31, 2001 in the Southern District of New York, case number 1:01-bk-14833, before Judge Robert E. Gerber.

Chapter 7 means liquidation rather than reorganization: assets are sold and creditors are paid if anything is left over. David R. Kittay was appointed trustee, and the meeting of creditors was set for December 5, 2001.

The case outlived the company by more than a decade. The docket shows it reopened on December 22, 2010 and terminated on July 27, 2012, with a clerk's letter about unclaimed dividends following in June 2013.

For anyone still holding flooz, the practical answer arrived in 2001 and has not changed. Unused balances became worthless and were not refundable.

Why no buyer wanted the company

Between the August 8 suspension and the final shutdown, Flooz.com tried to sell itself. The notice on its site said the company had been in merger discussions with a number of companies but could not find a suitable partner.

The official explanation leaned on the market rather than the fraud. Flooz.com said it had been "adversely affected by dramatic changes in capital markets and the general slowdown in the economy," which was true of nearly every dot-com that year and said nothing about the frozen $1 million.

The timing did not help. Flooz.com was shopping a switched-off consumer currency in the middle of a collapse that had already taken Pets.com, at a moment when no acquirer wanted a brand whose customers had just watched their balances go to zero.

There was also not much left to buy. The merchant contracts were worthless once the site stopped processing, the technology was unremarkable, and the one asset with any value was a list of people who had reason to be angry.

Flooz.com raised about $35 million, spent $8 million of it on a television campaign, and left its creditors to a Chapter 7 liquidation that took almost eleven years to close.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happened to Flooz.com?

Flooz.com, the online gift currency that debuted in the fall of 1999, suspended its website on August 8, 2001 and shut down for good later that month, after the FBI alerted it to a credit-card fraud ring and its payment processor froze about $1 million of its funds. A Chapter 7 bankruptcy petition for Flooz.Com, Inc. was filed on August 31, 2001 in the Southern District of New York.

Was Whoopi Goldberg really the face of Flooz?

Yes. Whoopi Goldberg fronted the $8 million advertising campaign that introduced Flooz.com in 1999, appearing in television, radio and print ads for the online gift currency. Flooz.com raised about $35 million in venture capital in total, so that one campaign consumed close to a quarter of it.

Did Flooz customers get their money back?

No. When Flooz.com shut down in August 2001, unused flooz balances became worthless and were not refundable, and what was left of the company went into a Chapter 7 liquidation in the Southern District of New York. That case, number 1:01-bk-14833, was not terminated until July 27, 2012.
