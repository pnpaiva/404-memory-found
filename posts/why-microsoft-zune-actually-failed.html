Is the Microsoft Zune Still Around? No, but It Still Works

By Dana Reyes · Updated November 8, 2026 · First published March 24, 2026 · 6 min read

The Microsoft Zune is not still around as a product. Microsoft stopped making Zune hardware in 2011 and switched off the Zune online services on November 15, 2015, which left the players with no store and no subscription. The devices still work as offline music players in 2026, used ones list for roughly $90 to $150, and a small community is still writing new software for them.

What was the Microsoft Zune, and when did it launch?

Microsoft put the first Zune in United States stores on November 14, 2006, at $249.99 for a 30 GB player, aimed squarely at the 30 GB iPod.

The hardware was competitive. The Zune 30 had a three inch screen, an FM tuner and Wi-Fi, none of which the iPod offered. The Wi-Fi powered a feature Microsoft pitched as wireless sharing: send a track to another Zune owner nearby, and they could play it three times within three days before it locked.

Behind the player sat Zune Marketplace and a subscription called Zune Pass, $14.99 a month for unlimited access to the catalog. In 2006, renting music instead of buying it was a strange idea. It is now how most people listen.

Distribution was the first warning sign. The Zune shipped in the United States only, and Canada did not get players in stores until June 13, 2008. The United Kingdom, Australia and the rest of Europe never got the hardware at all, while Apple sold iPods in nearly every country on earth.

Why did the Zune lose to the iPod?

Pricing was the first error. A challenger facing an entrenched leader has to be much cheaper or much better. At $249.99 the Zune was neither, and switching meant abandoning a music library already bought from iTunes and locked to Apple's copy protection.

The second problem was everything built around the iPod: the speaker docks, the car adapters, the headphones, the software already sitting on people's computers. The iPod was not the first MP3 player, but by 2006 it had five years of accessories and habit behind it, and the Zune had none of that.

Then there was timing. Apple introduced the iPhone in January 2007, eight weeks after the Zune reached stores. Every standalone music player on the market, Apple's own included, was suddenly living on borrowed time, and Microsoft had just committed to a multi-year plan for one.

Robbie Bach, who ran Microsoft's Entertainment and Devices division, said the same thing in 2012. "The portable music market is gone and it was already leaving when we started," he told an audience in Seattle, in remarks reported by Complete Music Update.

How badly did the Zune actually sell?

Microsoft never published a lifetime sales total for the Zune, which is usually an answer in itself. The figures that did surface were brutal.

In May 2008, roughly 18 months after launch, Microsoft said it had sold two million Zunes since the product went on sale, a number TechCrunch and Engadget both reported at the time. That total covered two hardware generations. Apple was clearing more iPods than that in a single quarter.

Retailers noticed. GameStop pulled the Zune from its shelves later in May 2008 and said plainly that the player had no appeal for its customers.

The strange part is that the best Zune came last. The Zune HD, which reached stores in September 2009 at $219.99 for 16 GB, had an OLED touchscreen, a web browser and an interface reviewers preferred to the software Apple was shipping. It landed in a market the iPhone and the iPod touch had already absorbed. Good hardware arriving years late is a familiar Microsoft pattern, visible again in the original Xbox, which burned billions before the Xbox 360 made the category pay.

Who owns the Zune brand now, and does the hardware still work?

Microsoft owns it, and nothing is being made. Microsoft stopped developing Zune hardware in 2011 and discontinued the players that October, pointing customers toward Windows Phone instead. The media brand was folded into Xbox Music in 2012, Xbox Music became Groove Music, and Microsoft closed the Groove subscription at the end of 2017 and moved its remaining music subscribers to Spotify.

The hard cutoff for Zune owners came on November 15, 2015, when Microsoft switched off the Zune services. Zune Music Pass subscriptions had already been converted to Groove Music Pass. From that day there was no Zune store, no Zune streaming and no subscription attached to the device.

A Zune still works as an offline player. Load MP3s over USB with the old Zune desktop software and it plays, the FM tuner still tunes, and the battery is the part that reliably fails first. What does not work is anything that had to reach Microsoft: Marketplace, Zune Pass and wireless sync to the store.

Where to find one today. Zunes trade steadily second hand, and condition drives everything. Working 30 GB players listed across resale sites in 2026 run about $90 to $150, refurbished units with a new battery or drive about $170 to $190, and Zune HD 16 GB models about $75 to $100. Buy one with a replaced battery or budget for the swap, and check the vintage Zune listings on Etsy for players sold with their original cables and packaging.

What can a Zune do in 2026?

More than Microsoft ever intended. In October 2026 a developer released sm64-zune, a port of Super Mario 64 that runs on the Zune HD, built on the sm64ex community decompilation and an unofficial development kit called OpenZDK.

It is native code on the player's Tegra chip rather than emulation, with audio, save support and the Nintendo 64 controller drawn on the touchscreen. It needs firmware 4.5 and a copy of the game the project does not supply.

Hackaday covered the release on October 9, 2026 and noted that "there's still a fairly active userbase for it," which is a fair summary of the Zune in 2026: a dead product with a living community attached. That community is the reason the players keep working at all, because it archives the desktop software and the modding tools Microsoft stopped hosting years ago.

Nothing about this comes from Microsoft. There has been no Zune hardware since 2011, no Zune service since 2015, and no sign the company intends to revive either.

What did the Zune get right before anyone else?

Robbie Bach gave the honest post mortem in 2012. "We just weren't brave enough, honestly, and we ended up chasing Apple," he said in remarks reported by Complete Music Update, calling the Zune "a chasing product" that gave nobody a reason to go out and buy it.

Chasing or not, the Zune was early on several things. Zune Pass sold unlimited listening for a flat $14.99 a month in 2006, years before that became the normal way to pay for music. Wireless track sharing anticipated the tap to send features every phone has now. The social layer, mocked at the time under the slogan "Welcome to the Social," is standard in every streaming app in 2026.

The most durable piece was the design. The typography first, chrome light interface Microsoft built for the Zune software and the Zune HD became the Metro design language that Windows Phone launched with in 2010, and its fingerprints are still on Windows and Xbox menus.

None of it saved the product, because Microsoft was selling a device and Apple was selling a habit. Sony's MiniDisc hit a version of the same wall a decade earlier: careful engineering attached to a format the rest of the market had no reason to adopt.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Microsoft Zune still around in 2026?

No. Microsoft stopped developing Zune hardware in 2011, discontinued the players in October 2011, and switched off the Zune online services on November 15, 2015. Zune players people already own still work as offline music players in 2026, and a homebrew community still writes software for them, but there is no store, no subscription and no support from Microsoft.

Why did the Microsoft Zune fail?

The Microsoft Zune failed because it launched in November 2006 at $249.99, matching the iPod on price without beating the ecosystem around it, and because Apple introduced the iPhone eight weeks later and began erasing the standalone music player market. Robbie Bach, who ran the Microsoft division responsible, called it a chasing product in 2012. Microsoft had sold only two million Zunes by May 2008.

How much is a Microsoft Zune worth today?

Microsoft Zune players are cheap collectibles rather than valuable ones. Working 30 GB models list for about $90 to $150 on 2026 resale sites, refurbished units with a new battery or hard drive for about $170 to $190, and Zune HD 16 GB players for about $75 to $100. A working battery and the original accessories account for most of the difference.
