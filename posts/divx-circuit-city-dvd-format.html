DIVX Explained: Circuit City's $337 Million Flop

2026-09-23 | By Theo Lindqvist | 6 min read

DIVX was a pay-per-view disc format from Digital Video Express, a venture 75 percent owned by Circuit City. It launched in June 1998, was discontinued on June 16, 1999, and cost Circuit City $337 million. No DIVX disc has played since the authorization servers went dark on June 30, 2001.

Quick facts
Launched
June 1998, Digital Video Express (Circuit City owned about 75 percent)
Price then
$4.49 a disc and about $499 for the first player, roughly $9 and $1,030 in 2026 dollars
Units sold
About 87,000 players and 535,000 discs in the last quarter of 1998
What replaced it
Open DVD, then DVD by mail and streaming
Status today
Dead. Discontinued June 16, 1999; no disc has played since June 30, 2001

How did a DIVX disc actually work?

A DIVX disc looked like an ordinary DVD and cost about $4.49 at Circuit City in 1998, roughly $9 in 2026 dollars. You never returned it. The moment you first played it, a 48 hour clock started, and once that clock ran out the disc stopped playing.

Watching it again meant paying a continuation fee for another two days. Anyone who wanted a film permanently could convert the disc to a DIVX Silver version for an extra charge and then play it without limit.

The enforcement lived inside the hardware. Every DIVX player shipped with a built in modem and had to be connected to a phone line, because it dialed a Digital Video Express account server to register purchases and authorize playback, much the way a satellite box cleared a pay-per-view order.

That was also the part customers disliked. A machine in the living room that phoned a company twice a day and knew exactly which movies had been watched was a difficult sell in 1998, and privacy critics said so at volume.

Who built DIVX, and why did Circuit City back it?

Digital Video Express was formed in 1997 as a limited partnership between Circuit City and Ziffren, Brittenham, Branca and Fischer, a Los Angeles entertainment law firm. Circuit City held roughly 75 percent of it, which is why the whole venture landed on one retailer's balance sheet.

The reasoning came from the film business. Studios had watched the video rental chains build an industry on tapes the studios sold once, and DIVX promised a disc that paid the rights holder on every viewing rather than on every purchase.

For Circuit City the appeal was simpler. The chain would own the format, sell the players, sell the discs, and collect the fees afterward. Salespeople had a direct incentive to push the machines on the floor.

Testing began in June 1998 in the San Francisco and Richmond, Virginia markets, across 45 stores run by Circuit City and The Good Guys. The national rollout followed on September 25, 1998, with a single Zenith player model and a catalog still measured in the low hundreds of titles.

How did DIVX sell in its one holiday season?

The only Christmas DIVX ever got was 1998, and the numbers were not catastrophic so much as thin. Roughly 87,000 players and 535,000 discs across about 300 titles moved in the final quarter of 1998, with about 375,000 of those discs selling in December alone.

Set against that, the standard DVD format launched in the United States in 1997 was already running away with the market, backed by every studio and every major retailer rather than two chains and a law firm.

The player was the bottleneck. The first DIVX machine, the Zenith Inteq DVX2100, listed around $499, close to $1,030 in 2026 dollars, and about $100 more than a plain DVD player that would play any disc sold anywhere. Thomson later added RCA and ProScan models.

Sales fell away sharply once the holidays ended. By early 1999 the third party retailers that had agreed to stock DIVX were backing out, leaving Circuit City carrying a national format almost by itself.

Why did the studios and the other retailers walk away?

Several studios signed on early, including Disney, DreamWorks, Paramount, Universal and 20th Century Fox, but signing on and committing were different things. A studio could release a title on open DVD and reach every household with a player. A DIVX release reached only the households that had bought a specific machine from a specific chain.

Retailers did the same arithmetic and reached a harsher answer. Stocking DIVX meant selling hardware and discs that pushed customers back toward a competitor's account system, and it meant a public argument with an internet audience that had turned against the format.

The backlash was real and it was loud. Anti-DIVX banners spread across enthusiast sites through 1998 and early 1999, and the format became a reputational liability for anyone who carried it.

On June 16, 1999, Digital Video Express announced it was shutting down. Richard Sharp, chairman of Digital Video Express and Circuit City Stores, told Variety that year: "Unfortunately, we have been unable to obtain adequate support from studios and other retailers." He added: "Despite the significant consumer enthusiasm, we cannot create a viable business without support in these essential areas."

Is DIVX still around, and do the discs play in 2026?

No. DIVX is completely dead, and it has been dead longer than it was alive. The format shipped in June 1998, stopped being sold in June 1999, and the last day any DIVX disc could be authorized was June 30, 2001, after which the authorization servers were switched off for good.

That shutdown is why a DIVX disc in 2026 is not a rare movie but a piece of plastic. There is no offline mode and no unlock, because the playback permission was never on the disc. It lived on a server that no longer exists. A DIVX Silver disc bought for unlimited viewing stopped working on the same date as every other one.

The players did survive the shutdown in one narrow sense. A DIVX machine is still a working DVD player, so the hardware kept a second life long after the service behind it closed.

Where to find one today

DIVX discs and player boxes now circulate as collectors' artifacts rather than media, and sellers list them that way, since nothing on them will play. A disc that cost $4.49 new in 1998 is worth whatever a collector will pay for an object that documents a failed format. Listings turn up under DIVX and Digital Video Express on Etsy.

What did the DIVX failure cost Circuit City?

The headline figure is $114 million. That is the after-tax charge Circuit City recorded on the disposal of the DIVX business, on top of a $16.2 million after-tax loss from DIVX operations in the same fiscal year. The company's Form 10-K described the charge as covering "commitments under licensing agreements with movie distributors, the write-down of assets to net realizable value, lease termination cost, employee severance and benefit cost".

Counting the money already sunk into the venture before the shutdown, Variety put the total hit to Circuit City at $337 million in 1999. Digital Video Express also paid a $100 cash rebate to every customer who had bought a DIVX player before June 16, 1999.

Open DVD took the whole market, and rental moved on without a special disc: first to DVD by mail, then to streaming. The industry had already settled formats this way twice, with Betamax and VHS in the 1980s and later with HD DVD against Blu-ray.

The strangest afterlife belongs to the name. Jerome Rota called his video codec DivX as a deliberate dig at Circuit City's format, complete with a winking emoticon. The codec outlived the thing it was mocking by decades.

Frequently Asked Questions

What was DIVX and why did it fail?

DIVX was a pay-per-view disc format launched by Digital Video Express, a venture 75 percent owned by Circuit City, in June 1998. A disc cost about $4.49 and played for 48 hours before charging again. It failed because studios and rival retailers never backed it, and Digital Video Express shut it down on June 16, 1999.

Do DIVX discs still work in 2026?

No DIVX disc has played since June 30, 2001, when Digital Video Express switched off the authorization servers that granted playback permission. Because that permission was never stored on the disc itself, DIVX discs cannot be revived, and the ones sold today in 2026 are collectors' artifacts rather than watchable movies.

Is the DivX codec the same thing as Circuit City's DIVX?

They are unrelated. Circuit City's DIVX was a physical disc format that ran from 1998 to 1999. The DivX video codec was written separately by Jerome Rota, who named it after the failed disc format as a joke, and it is still in use long after Digital Video Express closed in 1999.
