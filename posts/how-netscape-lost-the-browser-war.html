Why Netscape Navigator Lost to Internet Explorer

2026-03-22 | Updated 2026-11-03 | By Theo Lindqvist | 6 min read

Netscape Navigator lost the browser war because Microsoft made Internet Explorer free and shipped it inside Windows, which ran on more than 90 percent of new PCs. Justice Department figures filed in court show Netscape falling from about 76 percent of browser usage in January 1997 to about 19 percent by March 2000. No Netscape browser has been supported since March 1, 2008, and the brand now sits with AOL, which Bending Spoons bought from Yahoo on January 2, 2026.

What made Netscape Navigator the default browser of 1995?

Netscape Communications shipped Netscape Navigator in 1994, months after Marc Andreessen left the University of Illinois team behind NCSA Mosaic. Navigator drew images inline while a page was still loading, which made the web feel like something to use rather than something to study.

The business model is the part that mattered later. Netscape gave Navigator away to individuals and educators and charged companies for it, so the installed base grew far faster than the revenue. By the middle of 1995 the browser was how most people reached the web, and Netscape had started describing itself as a platform, a layer above Windows that would reduce the operating system to a detail.

Saying that out loud turned a software company into a target. Microsoft shipped Windows 95 with no browser in the box, and watched a company barely a year old set the standards for the network everyone was about to join.

How did Bill Gates decide to go after Netscape?

On May 26, 1995, Gates sent Microsoft's executive staff a memo titled "The Internet Tidal Wave." The Justice Department quoted it in its filings in United States v. Microsoft, including the line that named the problem: Netscape's browser was "dominant, with 70% usage share", a share Gates believed would let Netscape decide which "network extensions will catch on."

Microsoft licensed Mosaic code from Spyglass and shipped Internet Explorer 1.0 in August 1995 inside the Windows 95 Plus! pack. It was thin and almost nobody used it. Internet Explorer 2.0 was better and still lost. Internet Explorer 3.0, released in 1996, was the first version that competed on features rather than price.

The decision that mattered was not technical. Netscape charged companies for Navigator. Microsoft made Internet Explorer free to every user, business licenses included, and never charged again. One product carried a price and the other did not, and that price was what paid Navigator's engineers.

Why did bundling Internet Explorer with Windows work?

Windows ran on more than 90 percent of new Intel-compatible personal computers through the second half of the 1990s, a share the court later described as large, stable and protected by a high barrier to entry. A browser that shipped inside Windows reached the buyer before any download could.

Contracts finished the job. Microsoft signed computer manufacturers, internet service providers and content companies to carry or promote Internet Explorer, and the court found that manufacturers who favored Navigator put their Windows terms at risk. AOL, then the largest online service in America, agreed in 1996 to build its client on Internet Explorer instead of Navigator in exchange for a place on the Windows desktop.

Netscape's answer was to ask people to go and fetch a browser on purpose. In 1995 that was normal. By 1998, with Windows 98 shipping the browser wired into the shell, it was a favor most buyers had no reason to do.

How fast did Navigator's share actually collapse?

The Justice Department filed a chart in the case built from AdKnowledge monthly usage data. It shows Netscape's share of browser usage falling from about 76 percent in January 1997 to about 19 percent by March 2000. Microsoft's share rose from 20 percent to roughly 69 percent over the same three years.

The lines crossed in 1998, the year Internet Explorer passed Navigator and the year Netscape agreed to sell itself to AOL. The government also noted that the chart understated Internet Explorer, because AOL subscribers used Internet Explorer heavily and their traffic was not fully reflected in the hit counts.

Three years is the whole story. Navigator did not lose on a bad release or a missed quarter. It lost the default position, and in browsers the default is the market.

What did the court say Microsoft actually did?

The United States sued Microsoft in May 1998. On November 5, 1999, Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson issued his findings of fact and wrote that "viewed together, three main facts indicate that Microsoft enjoys monopoly power," citing a share of Intel-compatible PC operating systems that was extremely large and stable and shielded by what he called the applications barrier to entry.

The findings held that Microsoft used that power against a list of threats that included Netscape Navigator, Java, Apple and RealNetworks, and that the company's own messages showed manufacturers being pressured over which browser they promoted. On April 3, 2000, Jackson ruled that Microsoft had violated Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act, and on June 7, 2000, he ordered it split in two.

None of it reached Navigator in time. The D.C. Circuit upheld the monopoly-maintenance finding in 2001, threw out the breakup and removed Jackson from the case, and the settlement that followed imposed rules on conduct rather than structure. The browser the case was about had already lost.

Who owns Netscape Navigator today?

Nobody sells it or updates it. AOL bought Netscape Communications in 1998, kept Netscape-branded releases going for most of a decade, and then ended support for all Netscape browsers and client products on March 1, 2008. The cutoff had first been set for February 1 that year and was pushed back a month. There has been no supported Netscape Navigator since.

The name travels with AOL. Yahoo sold AOL to the Italian software group Bending Spoons, a sale that Bending Spoons' own filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission says was completed on January 2, 2026. That makes Bending Spoons the owner of the Netscape brand today. For what is left of the name and the portal, see whether Netscape still exists.

The code did better than the company. Netscape published the Communicator source under an open license in 1998 as the Mozilla project. The team eventually threw that codebase out and rebuilt from scratch, and the rebuild became Firefox.

What is left of the first browser war in 2026?

Internet Explorer is gone as well. Microsoft retired Internet Explorer 11 on June 15, 2022, writing that "Internet Explorer (IE) is officially retired and out of support as of today, June 15, 2022," and then permanently disabled the desktop application through a Microsoft Edge update on February 14, 2023.

Its replacement tells the story. Microsoft Edge is built on Chromium, the open-source engine behind Google Chrome, and carries an Internet Explorer mode for old corporate web applications. The company that won the browser war by controlling the engine now ships someone else's.

Firefox is the only widely used browser still running an engine descended from the code Netscape released in 1998. The rest of Netscape's work is everywhere and nobody notices it. JavaScript, SSL and the HTTP cookie all came out of the company that lost, and every browser opening this page implements all three.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who won the first browser war?

Microsoft won the first browser war. Internet Explorer passed Netscape Navigator in usage during 1998, and Justice Department figures filed in United States v. Microsoft show Netscape falling from about 76 percent of browser usage in January 1997 to about 19 percent by March 2000. AOL ended support for all Netscape browsers on March 1, 2008.

Is Internet Explorer still available in 2026?

No. Microsoft retired Internet Explorer 11 on June 15, 2022 and then permanently disabled the desktop application through a Microsoft Edge update on February 14, 2023. The only way to open a page in Internet Explorer in 2026 is Internet Explorer mode inside Microsoft Edge.

Did Microsoft break the law to beat Netscape Navigator?

A federal court said it did. Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson found on November 5, 1999 that Microsoft held monopoly power in PC operating systems, and ruled on April 3, 2000 that it had violated the Sherman Act, in part by tying Internet Explorer to Windows at Netscape Navigator's expense. The D.C. Circuit upheld the monopoly-maintenance finding in 2001 but cancelled the ordered breakup.
