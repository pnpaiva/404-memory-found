How Many Payphones Are Left? The FCC Stopped Counting

2026-09-17 | Updated 2026-10-15 | By Theo Lindqvist | 7 min read

Nobody can give an exact number of payphones left in the United States, because the Federal Communications Commission stopped requiring payphone audits in 2018 and ended its data series. Its last full count put the total under 100,000 at the end of 2016, down from more than 2.1 million at the 1999 peak. Thousands still work in 2026, most of them owned by private operators such as Pacific Telemanagement Services, which said in 2018 that it ran 20,000 phones.

What did the FCC's last payphone count find?

The Federal Communications Commission counted payphones for years, and those numbers are still the only national trend line anyone has.

At the 1999 peak there were more than 2.1 million payphones in service in the United States. By the end of 2016 the figure was under 100,000, a fall of about 95 percent.

The collapse was close to uniform. Most states lost more than 90 percent of their payphones between 2000 and 2016, according to the analysis of FCC data that Spokeo published through Stacker. New York State went from 171,098 phones in 2000 to 20,716 in 2016, an 87.9 percent decline, and that still left it holding about a fifth of every payphone counted in the country.

Hawaii was the outlier, down only about 60 percent. Hawaiian Telcom told the Honolulu Civil Beat that national trends may simply take longer to arrive there, and other telecom analysts pointed at visitors who find a coin call cheaper than international roaming.

Those two facts explain most of the map. Payphones lasted longest where cell coverage was bad and where travelers turned up without a working plan.

Why nobody can give you a 2026 number

Congress opened the payphone business to competition in the Telecommunications Act of 1996. The law stopped the Bell companies from favoring their own phones and set up a system that paid independent operators for calls which never touched a coin slot, such as toll-free and calling-card calls.

That compensation system needed policing, so the FCC required carriers to have the call tracking systems behind those payments audited every year by an independent third party.

By 2018 the audits cost more than the calls were worth. The Commission adopted FCC 18-21, Modernization of Payphone Compensation Rules, on February 22, 2018, and the Federal Register published it on March 15, 2018. The order dropped the annual audit and its reporting requirement, on the reasoning that those rules were no longer needed to make sure payphone providers received the compensation they were due. Carriers still have to track calls and pay operators.

The side effect is that the federal government stopped generating payphone data. Anyone who quotes an exact national figure for 2026 is estimating from the 2016 baseline, from what operators disclose, or from counting phones by hand.

Are there still working payphones in 2026?

Yes, and one company accounts for most of them. Pacific Telemanagement Services, based in San Ramon, California, picked up the phones the Bell companies dropped. AT&T said in December 2007 that it was leaving the business and would sell or remove 65,000 payphones, in prisons and in public places, across its original 13 states by the end of 2008. In 2011 Pacific Telemanagement Services agreed to buy nearly all of Verizon's remaining 50,000 payphones, most of them in the Northeast.

How many it runs today depends on who you ask. Tom Keane, the company's president, said in 2018 that it operated 20,000 phones nationwide. Later accounts put the number near 25,000, while a company profile has claimed about 45,000 across 48 states. Nobody audits the figure anymore.

Keane has been consistent about why any of them still earn money. “People tend to forget about pay phones, until their cellphone doesn't get a signal, until there's a natural disaster,” he said in a 2011 Wall Street Journal report that NPR's Planet Money quoted. He was just as blunt about the trend in the same piece: “We have an erosion rate that doesn't have us thinking we'll be in the pay phone business for long.”

That is where the survivors sit. Rural highways with no signal, correctional facilities where inmates cannot hold a handset of their own, transit stations and hospital lobbies. A payphone stopped being street furniture around 2010 and became emergency equipment instead.

How New York removed 6,000 street phones

New York City started replacing its sidewalk payphones in 2015 under the franchise that became LinkNYC. Over the next seven years roughly 6,000 phones came out and about 2,000 Wi-Fi kiosks went in.

On May 23, 2022 a crane lifted the last free-standing public payphone off Seventh Avenue near West 50th Street, a block from Times Square. It went to the Museum of the City of New York for Analog City, an exhibit about the city before computers.

Matthew Fraser, then the commissioner of the city's Office of Technology and Innovation, marked the moment: “As a native New Yorker, saying goodbye to the last street payphone is bittersweet.” He compared the change to the shift from horse and buggy to automobile to airplane.

The title was disputed within days. Four full-length glass booths still stand on West End Avenue on the Upper West Side, and payphones inside private buildings were never part of the city's franchise count. New York removed its last public street phone in 2022, not its last phone.

What did a payphone call cost before the coins stopped?

Coin prices climbed as the customer base shrank, and 50 cents became the standard local rate in the early 2000s.

Verizon, then the largest payphone operator in the country, began converting its 430,000 payphones across 33 states in September 2001 so that a local call took 50 cents instead of 35 cents, with directory assistance moving to the same price. The company blamed wireless competition: payphone use had fallen 23 percent in two years, so each remaining call had to carry more of the cost. Qwest had already gone to 50 cents in May 2001, and SBC Communications and BellSouth announced the same price. New York came last, with 110,000 phones converted later that year.

What emptied the coin boxes is no mystery. Cheap handsets did it, first with phones like the Nokia 3310 from 2000 and then the Motorola Razr V3 from 2004, which put a dial tone in the pocket of people who had never owned a landline. Can free payphones bring the booth back?

A small movement has been reinstalling payphones that take no money at all. PhilTel, a volunteer collective in Philadelphia, pulls out the coin mechanism, fits a circuit board and a router, and routes calls over VoIP using Asterisk. Its first phone went live at Iffy Books near Chinatown on December 17, 2022, and calls to most North American numbers are free. Progress is slow: PhilTel's own site said in October 2025 that the group was working on installing its second phone.

The idea came from Futel, which an engineer named Karl Anderson started in Portland, Oregon in 2014 and which has run about a dozen free phones across Oregon, Washington and Michigan.

Set against the collapse, these are rounding errors. The Payphone Project counted 4,800 payphones in Philadelphia in the 1990s, and one of PhilTel's organizers estimated about 50 still working in the city in 2022. The revival matters for a different reason. It shows that a payphone now has to be donated infrastructure, because coins no longer cover the line rental and the maintenance visit.

Where to find one today

Payphones have a second life as decor and as restoration projects. Single-slot Western Electric units, booth signs, coin doors, receivers and instruction cards all turn up in the vintage telephone listings on Etsy.

Condition drives the price, and the gap between a gutted shell and a unit with a working coin mechanism and its keys is wide. Read the listing carefully: reproductions built to look like 1950s phones sit in the same search results as genuine Bell System hardware, and a phone with no key is a phone you cannot open.
