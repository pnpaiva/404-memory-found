Is Columbia House Still Around? It Closed in 2026

2026-09-13 | Updated 2026-10-04 | By Theo Lindqvist | 5 min read

Columbia House is no longer around. The mail-order club stopped accepting new orders after September 15, 2026, 71 years after it launched as the Columbia Record Club in 1955, and existing orders were honored through the wind-down. Its music club had already ended around 2010, leaving a DVD and Blu-ray operation that was down to roughly a dozen titles by 2026.

What ended on September 15, 2026

Columbia House stopped taking new orders after September 15, 2026, which closed a mail-order business that had run for 71 years. The announcement was one line on the company's own website: "After 9/15/26, The Columbia House will no longer be accepting new orders."

There was no press release, no farewell campaign and no final catalog. Collectors spotted the notice over a weekend in August 2026, it spread, and then the company deleted it. A customer service representative confirmed to CNN by phone that the shutdown was happening anyway, after the public notice had already come down.

What closed was not the music club. Columbia House had stopped selling music about fifteen years earlier and spent the years after that mailing DVDs and Blu-rays to a shrinking list of members. By 2026 the online catalog was down to roughly a dozen discs, as Gizmodo noted when the news broke.

Existing memberships and outstanding purchases were honored through the wind-down, so nobody with a disc on order was cut off on the fifteenth. The sign-up form was the part that went away first, which is a quiet ending for a company that once mailed tens of millions of Americans a reply card every month.

How a penny bought 12 CDs and still made money

The offer was real. New members picked eight, ten or twelve titles for one cent, sent no money up front, and agreed to buy a set number of albums later at what the fine print called regular club prices, plus shipping and handling.

The giveaway was the cheap part. Mental Floss reported that a free disc cost Columbia House around $1.50 to produce, and that artists were not paid royalties on the records the club gave away, which is why a dozen albums for a cent was survivable arithmetic rather than a stunt.

The money came afterward. Every month the club shipped a Selection of the Month unless the member mailed a card back refusing it before a deadline. Miss the deadline and the album arrived at full club price, with shipping charged per disc.

Chris Wilcha, who worked in marketing at Columbia House in the 1990s, described the model to The A.V. Club in 2015: "The whole business was premised on this concept called negative option." On that arithmetic, Mental Floss estimated the club made as much as $7.50 on each album it shipped. The penny was the advertisement. The albums nobody meant to order were the business, which is why one generation remembers the club as a gift and another remembers it as a trap.

Why 16 million members walked away

Columbia House began in 1955 as the Columbia Record Club, an experiment by Columbia Records in selling music to Americans who lived nowhere near a record store. It took the Columbia House name in the 1970s and became a fixture of late-night television advertising.

The CD boom made it enormous. At its 1996 peak the club had about 16 million members and roughly $1.4 billion in annual sales, the figures that ran in every account of the 2026 shutdown.

Then the reasons to join disappeared one at a time. Big-box chains undercut club prices on new releases. Napster made the catalog free in 1999, paid downloads made a monthly reply card look absurd, and the Sony Discman gave way to the iPod. The same decade emptied the stores that sold those discs by the thousand, Tower Records among them.

Columbia House answered by dropping the product and keeping the machine. The music club ended around 2010, the shipping and billing operation stayed, and discs that people still bought by mail, which by then meant movies, went into the envelopes instead.

Who owns Columbia House today?

Nobody is running it any more. The brand's last operator was Edge Line Ventures, the company through which John Lippman took what was left of Columbia House after its parent filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in 2015. Sony Music Entertainment kept the Columbia House trademark, which it has held since the years when the club was a Columbia Records sales channel.

Lippman's announced plan in 2015 was a relaunch as a vinyl subscription service, timed to the record revival. The vinyl club never arrived. What he ran instead was the DVD and Blu-ray business, quietly, for another decade, and that is the operation that stopped in September 2026.

Gizmodo made the point in 2026 that the format was never what kept the company alive: "The format wasn't the secret sauce, and neither was the content. It was the business model." A club that could bill a member who forgot to say no did not need a hit record or a hit movie. It needed a list of people who would keep forgetting, and after 71 years that list ran out.

What the club left behind

Columbia House pressed its own editions of other labels' albums for members, which is why so many American record collections hold copies that do not quite match the store version. Those pressings still circulate, along with the catalogs, the order forms and the sheets of stamps members peeled off to pick their twelve titles.

Where to find one today: club-edition LPs and CDs, 1970s and 1980s catalogs and unopened mailers turn up in the vintage ephemera listings on Etsy. Club pressings were made in enormous quantities, so they are among the cheaper artifacts of the era, and condition matters far more than rarity.

The club's longer legacy is the billing. The monthly shipment you have to decline, the free trial that renews, the box that ships unless you remember to say no: that is negative option billing, and Columbia House ran it on American teenagers for four decades before subscription software made it ordinary.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Columbia House still around in 2026?

No. Columbia House stopped accepting new orders after September 15, 2026, ending 71 years that began with the Columbia Record Club in 1955. Existing memberships and outstanding purchases were honored while the business wound down, but no new sign-ups were taken after that date.

When did Columbia House stop selling CDs?

Columbia House ended its music club around 2010, roughly fifteen years before the company closed, and sold only DVDs and Blu-rays after that. The music side had peaked in 1996 with about 16 million members and roughly $1.4 billion in annual sales, then lost members through the download and streaming years.

Who owned Columbia House at the end?

Columbia House was run by Edge Line Ventures, John Lippman's company, which took the brand on after the parent company's Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2015. Sony Music Entertainment holds the Columbia House trademark. The vinyl subscription club Lippman announced for 2016 never arrived, and the DVD operation he kept running is the business that closed in September 2026.
