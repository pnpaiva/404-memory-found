Who Owns the 3DO Now? Throwback Entertainment Does

2026-05-07 | Updated 2026-09-24 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

The 3DO trademark, the hardware designs and the 3do.com domain are owned by Throwback Entertainment, a Toronto publisher, and no 3DO console has been manufactured since 1996. The 3DO Interactive Multiplayer launched on October 4, 1993 at $699.99 and sold roughly 2 million units before Panasonic and the other licensees dropped it. A 2026 attempt to relaunch the brand fell apart within a week.

Why did the 3DO cost $699.99 at launch?

The 3DO Interactive Multiplayer reached American stores on October 4, 1993 at $699.99, which Wikipedia's inflation table puts at about $1,586 in March 2026 dollars. That number was not a miscalculation. It was the direct result of how the company was built.

Trip Hawkins founded Electronic Arts in 1982 and left in 1991 to start The 3DO Company around a different model. Instead of manufacturing a console, 3DO would design the standard, license it to consumer electronics firms, and take a royalty on every game sold. Panasonic, Goldstar, Sanyo, Creative Labs and Samsung all shipped 3DO machines. The 3DO Company shipped none.

That structure made a cheap console impossible. Sega, Nintendo and later Sony could sell hardware at or below cost because they earned it back on software. Panasonic had no software royalty to recover, so the FZ-1 had to carry its manufacturing cost, Panasonic's margin, the 3DO royalty and retail markup all at the same time.

Price cuts came quickly and never went far enough. Six months in, the FZ-1 dropped to $499. Goldstar's cheaper machine arrived at $399. A Sega Genesis or a Super Nintendo still cost a fraction of either.

Did the PlayStation kill the 3DO?

The hardware was not the problem. The FZ-1 ran a 32-bit ARM60 processor at 12.5 MHz with 2 MB of RAM, 1 MB of video RAM and a double-speed CD-ROM drive, which was a serious specification for a living room machine in 1993. Time magazine named the 3DO its 1993 Product of the Year.

Then the fifth generation arrived at prices the 3DO could not answer. The Sega Saturn reached North America in May 1995 at $399. Sony put the PlayStation on shelves in September 1995 at $299. The Atari Jaguar was already flailing in the same window.

The sales gap that followed was not close. The Saturn sold 9.26 million units worldwide and is usually filed as a failure. The PlayStation sold about 102 million. The 3DO platform, across every manufacturer that built one, sold around 2 million units before it was discontinued in 1996.

Software made it worse. The CD-ROM drive invited full-motion video, and much of the launch-era library was grainy live action rather than games. Road Rash and Crash 'N Burn were real, but a few strong titles could not carry a $699.99 console against Nintendo and Sega at their peak.

What happened to The 3DO Company after the console died?

The 3DO Company had a successor ready in concept. The M2 was a next-generation design meant to outrun both the Saturn and the PlayStation, and developers were already getting hardware. In October 1995 the company agreed to sell Matsushita exclusive rights to the M2 technology for a $100 million licensing fee plus royalties, with the deal completed in January 1996.

Matsushita never shipped it as a console. The company looked at using the technology in DVD players, car navigation systems and set-top boxes, then cancelled the M2 in 1997. Prototype M2 units remain among the most valuable unreleased hardware in gaming.

With the hardware money banked, Hawkins rebuilt 3DO as a third-party publisher behind Army Men, High Heat Major League Baseball and Might and Magic. It did not hold. The company reported a net loss of more than $10 million in the nine months to December 2002 and filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on May 28, 2003.

The catalog was then carved up at auction. Microsoft took High Heat Baseball, Take-Two Interactive took Army Men, Ubisoft took Might and Magic and Heroes of Might and Magic, and Namco took Street Racing Syndicate.

Who owns the 3DO name today?

The console trademark did not go to any of those publishers. It sits with Throwback Entertainment, a video game publisher based in Toronto, Ontario, which also holds the hardware designs and the 3do.com domain.

That ownership was tested in public in 2026. In late June, a relaunched Empire Interactive, run by British-Turkish entrepreneur Isik Sekercigil, announced on LinkedIn that it had acquired the trademark and select intellectual property of The 3DO Company and intended to bring the console back.

Throwback Entertainment called the claim inaccurate. "The rights to the 3DO trademark and associated designs, as well as the 3DO.com domain, are owned by Throwback Entertainment," the company told Time Extension in 2026, adding that the assets had not been sold, transferred or licensed and were not for sale. Limited Run Games chief executive Josh Fairhurst, who had first offered to license 3DO titles and then checked, said publicly: "I'm currently of the opinion that this is not legitimate."

Empire Interactive withdrew in early July 2026, saying it "did not anticipate such fragmentation of rights" and that it would avoid prolonged legal disputes and work on its own games instead. The fragmentation is real: Ziggurat Interactive bought more than 30 games once published by The 3DO Company from Prism Entertainment in 2020, so the software, the trademark and the company name are in different hands.

Can you still play 3DO games in 2026?

Yes, and emulation is the path of least resistance. The 3DO library is small, well documented and supported by mature emulators, and the handful of titles worth playing, including Road Rash, Star Control II and the original Need for Speed, run fine on a modern PC. Nothing about that requires Throwback Entertainment's permission for discs you already own.

On original hardware, the second-generation Panasonic FZ-10 is the more practical buy. It is a top loader, which removes the FZ-1's motorized tray from the list of things that can fail after 30 years. Laser drives in either model are the usual point of failure, so a console sold as tested is worth the premium. The same calculus applies to playing Sega Genesis games today.

Where to find one today. Retro buying guides put a working 3DO console in the rough range of $100 to $200, with Goldstar units cheaper than Panasonic ones and boxed complete systems running far above that. Stock moves, so the specialist retro shops are the sane starting point rather than a listing that disappears. Stone Age Gamer carries consoles, controllers and accessories of this era.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much did the 3DO cost in 1993?

The Panasonic FZ-1 model of the 3DO Interactive Multiplayer launched in North America on October 4, 1993 at $699.99, equivalent to roughly $1,586 in March 2026. The price fell to $499 after about six months on sale, and Goldstar's competing 3DO model launched at $399.

How many 3DO consoles were sold?

The 3DO platform sold about 2 million units worldwide across every licensee that built one, between the October 1993 launch and its discontinuation in 1996. For scale, the Sega Saturn sold 9.26 million units and the Sony PlayStation sold about 102 million.

Why did the 3DO fail?

The 3DO failed because its licensing model made it structurally expensive. Because Panasonic, Goldstar and Sanyo built the hardware and needed a profit on each unit, the 3DO could not be sold at a loss the way Sega, Nintendo and Sony sold theirs, so it launched at $699.99 in 1993 against machines costing a fraction of that, with a thin game library and no exclusive to force the purchase.
