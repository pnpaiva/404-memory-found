How MySpace Lost to Facebook and Sold for $35M

2026-03-25 | Updated 2026-11-12 | By Dana Reyes | 7 min read

MySpace lost to Facebook in May 2009, when comScore counted 70.28 million US unique visitors for Facebook against 70.26 million for MySpace, three years after News Corporation tied the site's revenue to a $900 million Google advertising agreement that paid on traffic. News Corporation sold MySpace for $35 million in June 2011, six years after paying $580 million for it. MySpace still exists in 2026 as a music site owned by Viant Technology, whose owners promise a relaunch but have set no date.

What made MySpace the biggest social network in America?

MySpace was built in 2003 by Tom Anderson and Chris DeWolfe, two employees of the internet marketing company eUniverse, later renamed Intermix Media. It was not the first social network. It was the first one that handed the page layout to the user.

Every profile was an editable HTML window. People pasted code into a text box and got tiled backgrounds, autoplaying songs, custom cursors and a Top 8 friends list that ran real social politics in American high schools. The pages were often unreadable. They were also unmistakably personal, which is the part no mainstream platform has matched since.

Music was the second engine. Bands set up profiles, uploaded songs and reached listeners without a label in between, and a generation of American teenagers found new acts on profile pages rather than on the radio.

The lead that bought was enormous. comScore counted 73.7 million US unique visitors on MySpace in May 2008, against 35.6 million for Facebook, a gap of more than two to one in the market that mattered most to advertisers.

How did the News Corporation deal change the site?

Rupert Murdoch's News Corporation agreed in July 2005 to buy Intermix Media, the owner of MySpace, for $580 million. The reasoning was straightforward: the largest audience on the web, bought by a company that knew how to sell advertising against an audience.

A year later, News Corporation put that audience under contract. In August 2006 its internet arm, Fox Interactive Media, made Google the exclusive search and keyword advertising provider across its sites, MySpace included. The agreement filed with the SEC sets out the terms: Google owed "guaranteed minimum revenue share payments to Fox Interactive Media of $900 million based on Fox achieving certain traffic and other commitments," paid between the first quarter of 2007 and the second quarter of 2010.

Read that clause as a MySpace product manager in 2007 and the job description writes itself. The money was not attached to whether anyone enjoyed the site. It was attached to traffic and to the volume of pages served. Ads multiplied, pages that were already slow under custom HTML got slower, and any change that cut the number of clicks in a session worked against the number the company had promised Wall Street.

The site kept growing for a while anyway, which is the trap. Page views held up long enough to make the strategy look sound, and by the time the audience numbers turned, the product work that would have kept people there had been deferred for three years.

How close was the Facebook crossover in 2009?

Much closer than the story usually suggests. Twelve months after MySpace's two-to-one lead, the gap was gone. comScore put Facebook at 70.28 million US unique visitors in May 2009 against 70.26 million for MySpace, a margin of roughly 23,000 people, after Facebook added 2.8 million American visitors in a single month and MySpace lost about 700,000.

Facebook had offered the opposite bargain all along. Every profile looked the same: no autoplay, no glitter, no layout a teenager could break with one unclosed tag. Pages loaded quickly and predictably, which mattered more each month as MySpace profiles grew heavier with custom code and advertising. The News Feed did the rest, collecting updates in one place instead of making users walk profile to profile. MySpace never shipped an equivalent that users kept.

The two sites also grew in opposite directions. Facebook started closed, on college campuses where a university email address was the entry ticket, then opened outward from a base of real names. MySpace was fighting spam accounts, fake profiles and a reputation problem with American parents that never attached itself to Facebook.

Network effects reward the leader and punish it just as fast when the direction flips. Once a user's friends had moved, staying on MySpace meant talking to an empty room. The same reversal had already taken Friendster apart a few years earlier, with MySpace as the beneficiary.

What did the collapse cost News Corporation?

On June 29, 2011, News Corporation sold MySpace to the advertising targeting firm Specific Media for $35 million, mostly in stock. That was well under the $100 million News Corporation had been asking for the site, and a fraction of the $580 million it paid six years earlier. Justin Timberlake joined the buying group as an investor and a public face for the relaunch.

The sale price is the number everyone remembers, and it understates the damage. News Corporation also spent six years of management attention, a run of executive turnover, and the traffic commitments it had written into the Google agreement, on a property that ended up worth less than a mid-size office building.

The music paid a price too. In March 2019 MySpace confirmed that user uploads from 2003 to 2015 were gone, an estimated 50 million songs from about 14 million artists. "Due to a server migration, files were corrupted and unable to be transferred over to our updated site," the company said in its statement, which added that there was no way to recover the lost data. An independent music archive disappeared while the industry was still working out what the file-sharing era had done to it, a story covered in our look at what Napster did to the music business.

Who owns MySpace today?

Viant Technology, the advertising technology company run by brothers Tim and Chris Vanderhook. Ownership traces straight back to the 2011 sale: Specific Media grew into Viant, and Viant has held MySpace ever since. Myspace.com still resolves and still works as a music and discovery site rather than a social network, and the companion piece on what is left of the site walks through the current state in detail.

The owners say they are not finished with it. In Tommy Avallone's documentary Myspace, which ran the festival circuit in 2026, Tim Vanderhook said: "We are stewards of the Myspace brand at this point, and we are going to relaunch Myspace." He added that they are "waiting for the right time to do it." No date, no product and no feature list has been announced.

They have tried once already. The 2013 rebuild, with Timberlake attached, dropped private messaging and profile customization and never held an audience. Chris Vanderhook put the bill at "a little over $150 million," as Entrepreneur reported in 2026, and said advertisers left as Facebook grew. Tom Anderson, the automatic first friend on every new account in the 2000s, is not part of the next plan.

What the collapse taught the platforms that came after

MySpace's real legacy is not a feature. It is the proof that a dominant social network can lose its audience in about twelve months of visible decline, from a two-to-one lead in May 2008 to the crossover in May 2009, and that the loss is close to irreversible once it starts.

The lesson companies took from it was mostly defensive: standardize the layout, keep the feed fast, and buy or copy anything growing. The lesson users took was different. Interest in handmade personal pages never went away, which is why a deliberate MySpace clone like SpaceHey exists at all, and why archived pages from the GeoCities era still draw visitors.

The other lesson holds for everything that followed MySpace onto the web. A site that starts optimizing for the number it promised an advertiser, rather than for the reason people showed up, gets to keep that number for about as long as it takes users to find the next place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did MySpace lose to Facebook?

MySpace lost to Facebook because it optimized for advertising volume instead of the experience. After News Corporation bought MySpace's owner for $580 million in 2005 and signed a $900 million search and advertising agreement with Google in 2006 that depended on traffic commitments, the site filled with ads and slowed down, while Facebook offered fast, standardized profiles and the News Feed. Facebook passed MySpace among American users in May 2009.

When did Facebook overtake MySpace?

Facebook overtook MySpace in the United States in May 2009, when comScore counted 70.28 million US unique visitors for Facebook against 70.26 million for MySpace. A year earlier, in May 2008, MySpace had led 73.7 million to 35.6 million, so the reversal took about twelve months. News Corporation sold MySpace two years after the crossover, in June 2011.

Who owns MySpace now?

MySpace is owned by Viant Technology, the advertising company run by Tim and Chris Vanderhook, who bought the site as Specific Media for $35 million in June 2011. Myspace.com is still online in 2026 as a music and discovery site, and the Vanderhooks said in a 2026 documentary that they intend to relaunch it, without naming a date. Neither founder, Tom Anderson nor Chris DeWolfe, is involved.
