Is Nortel Still Around in 2026? Who Owns It Now

2026-10-19 | Updated 2026-11-06 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Nortel Networks was worth more than a third of Canada's main stock index in 2000 and filed for creditor protection in Delaware and Ontario on January 14, 2009. It failed because it sold equipment to telephone carriers that stopped buying, and because four years of accounting restatements destroyed what credibility was left. Nortel does not exist in 2026: Ericsson, Avaya and Ciena absorbed its divisions, and RPX Corporation holds most of the patents that sold for about $4.5 billion in 2011.

What did Nortel Networks actually sell?

Nortel Networks grew out of the Northern Electric and Manufacturing Company, set up in 1895 to build telephone equipment in Canada. It traded as Northern Telecom from 1976 and took the Nortel name in the 1990s.

Almost nothing it made was something a shopper could buy. Its DMS central-office switches routed calls for the phone companies themselves. Its Meridian and Norstar systems ran the phone sitting on an office desk. Its optical division lit long-haul fiber, and its wireless unit built CDMA networks for carriers.

That made Nortel a supplier to the companies building out the internet rather than a brand with customers of its own. It was a good place to be in 1999 and a terrible one in 2002, because when the carriers stopped buying there was no retail business underneath to cushion the fall.

The same wave of telecom money built and then buried a lot of ambitious hardware, from the satellite network behind Iridium to the cable modems of Excite@Home. Nortel was the largest of the equipment makers caught in it.

How big was Nortel at the top of the market?

Big enough to distort a national stock index. The Canadian Encyclopedia puts Nortel at more than a third of the value of the TSE 300 at its height in 2000, and ranks it the ninth most valuable corporation in the world at that point. Anyone holding a Canadian index fund was holding mostly one telecom equipment maker.

The share price peaked in the summer of 2000 and then fell for two straight years. Pension funds and retail investors who had bought the index without reading it took the loss with the company.

The headcount tells the same story in people. Nortel employed about 94,000 worldwide at its peak, and the cuts started within weeks of the share price turning. Round after round of restructuring through 2001 and 2002 took the workforce down by more than half.

What makes the collapse unusual is that it ran for nine years. Nortel did not go from the top of the index to bankruptcy court in one crash the way the dot-com retailers did. It shrank, restated, reorganized, hired new chief executives and shrank again, and only ran out of road in 2009.

What did the accounting restatements do to the company?

Between 2003 and 2005 Nortel restated its results for 2000, 2001, 2002 and the first half of 2003, more than once. For a company already asking investors to trust a turnaround, restating four years of numbers was close to fatal to its credibility.

In April 2004 the board fired chief executive Frank Dunn, chief financial officer Douglas Beatty and controller Michael Gollogly. Prosecutors in Ontario later charged all three with fraud, alleging the books had been managed to trigger cash and stock bonuses for senior management.

The case took almost a decade and ended in acquittal. On January 14, 2013, Justice Frank Marrocco of the Ontario Superior Court found that the gaps between the original numbers and the restated ones came from differences of accounting judgment rather than fraud, and the Crown did not appeal.

The verdict arrived four years too late to matter commercially. By then the company had been in bankruptcy for four years and its businesses had already been sold. The restatements did their damage in 2004, in the same stretch that accounting scandals were taking down WorldCom and others in telecom.

What happened on January 14, 2009?

Nortel Networks filed for creditor protection on both sides of the border on the same day. Its US units filed for Chapter 11 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware. The Canadian parent and its Canadian subsidiaries went to the Ontario Superior Court of Justice under the Companies' Creditors Arrangement Act, and the European arms were placed into administration in London.

Chief executive Mike Zafirovski framed it as a reset rather than a surrender, saying the company "must be put on a sound financial footing once and for all," as The Register reported on the day of the filing in 2009.

It did not work out that way. Within months the plan stopped being a restructuring and became a liquidation, because buyers wanted the individual business lines and nobody wanted the whole company. Nortel never came out of bankruptcy as an operating business. The filing of January 2009 was the last corporate decision it made as one.

Who bought the pieces after the filing?

The sales ran through 2009 and 2010, one division at a time, each approved by the courts in Delaware and Ontario.

Ericsson took the CDMA and LTE wireless business for $1.13 billion. Avaya bought the enterprise phone business, Meridian and Norstar included, for $900 million in cash, with a further $15 million set aside to keep staff in place. Ciena took the optical networking and carrier Ethernet unit for $769 million, made up of $530 million in cash and $239 million in convertible notes.

Shareholders got nothing. Equity in a company that had been the most valuable in Canada was wiped out, and Canadian pensioners spent years in court over a plan that was not fully funded when the company stopped existing.

What was left at the end was the patents, and they turned out to be worth more than any operating division in the estate.

Who owns Nortel's patents today?

Most of them belong to RPX Corporation. Getting there took two sales and three years.

Google opened the 2011 auction with a stalking-horse bid of $900 million and lost. A consortium called Rockstar, made up of Apple, EMC, Ericsson, Microsoft, Research In Motion and Sony, paid about $4.5 billion for more than 6,000 patents and applications, roughly five times Google's opening number.

Google's general counsel Kent Walker called the result "disappointing for anyone who believes that open innovation benefits users and promotes creativity and competition," in a statement reported by PCWorld in 2011.

The portfolio did not stay put. Rockstar's members kept about 2,000 patents for themselves and placed roughly 4,000 in the consortium, and in December 2014 RPX Corporation agreed to buy that block for about $900 million through its RPX Clearinghouse subsidiary, which also ended Rockstar's remaining lawsuits. Patents that a smartphone war was fought over in 2011 changed hands for a fifth of the price three years later.

That chain is why former Nortel patents still surface in disputes with no connection to Canada or to telephone switches. Research In Motion, now BlackBerry, was one of the original buyers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Nortel still around in 2026?

No. Nortel Networks filed for creditor protection in Canada and the United States on January 14, 2009, sold its businesses off piece by piece and never resumed operating. Nothing ships under the Nortel name in 2026: the wireless division went to Ericsson, the enterprise phone business to Avaya and the optical business to Ciena.

Who owns Nortel's patents now?

Most of Nortel's former patents are held by RPX Corporation. The Rockstar consortium of Apple, EMC, Ericsson, Microsoft, Research In Motion and Sony bought more than 6,000 Nortel patents for about $4.5 billion in 2011, kept roughly 2,000 among its members, and sold the remaining block of about 4,000 to RPX for about $900 million in December 2014.

Why did Nortel go bankrupt?

Nortel Networks sold equipment to telephone and internet carriers, so when carrier spending collapsed after 2000 it had no consumer business to fall back on. Years of accounting restatements, the firing of three senior executives in April 2004 and continuous restructuring left it short of cash, and Nortel filed for bankruptcy protection on January 14, 2009.
