Is Flickr Still Around in 2026? SmugMug Owns It

2026-04-10 | Updated 2026-09-22 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Flickr is still online in 2026. SmugMug, a privately held photo-hosting company, bought it from Verizon in April 2018 and still runs it as a separate site. A free Flickr account holds 1,000 photos and videos, and Flickr Pro costs 11 dollars a month or 82 dollars a year.

How a failed video game turned into Flickr

Flickr started as a feature inside a game nobody ever got to play. Stewart Butterfield, Caterina Fake and Jason Classon founded Ludicorp in Vancouver in the summer of 2002 to build Game Neverending, a browser-based multiplayer world.

The game let players collect objects and leave notes for each other. That sharing layer drew more attention than the game wrapped around it, and on February 10, 2004 the team launched the photo half on its own as Flickr.

The launch shape mattered. Flickr opened to a small invited group of people who cared about organizing images rather than to a mass audience. Those users tagged their own photos, and the tags became a way to move sideways through millions of pictures instead of scrolling down a single feed.

Creative Commons licensing arrived alongside the tags. A photographer could mark a file for reuse with attribution or keep full copyright, and that choice travelled with the image. Flickr also published an open API, so outside developers built uploaders, screensavers and printing tools the company never had to write itself. By 2005 the site was where serious amateurs put their work.

What did Yahoo pay for Flickr in 2005?

Yahoo bought Ludicorp, and with it Flickr, on March 20, 2005, roughly a year after launch. Yahoo never published the price. Reporting at the time and since puts it between 22 and 25 million dollars, with some accounts as high as 35 million, and no filing has ever settled it.

The strategy was search. Yahoo ran one of the web's largest search engines, and Flickr was quietly assembling an index of photographs described by the people who took them. Attaching that metadata to image search looked like a cheap way to get ahead of Google.

What Yahoo actually bought was a community with tools around it. The tags, the groups and the API worked because the people running the product answered to photographers. Inside Yahoo, they answered to a portal strategy instead.

Users felt the change first at the login screen. Yahoo folded Flickr accounts into Yahoo IDs, tying the site to an authentication system people already resented and marking Flickr as a Yahoo property rather than its own place. Flickr did not get its own login back until March 2019, fourteen years after the deal closed.

Why both founders quit Yahoo in 2008

Butterfield and Fake lasted three years. Both left in the summer of 2008, during a wider exodus of senior staff from Yahoo. Fake went in June, Butterfield in July.

Butterfield's resignation letter, addressed to Yahoo executive Brad Garlinghouse and published by Gawker in 2008, was written in character as a tin miner leaving a century-old mining concern. The joke carried a real complaint: he wrote that "my ability to contribute has dwindled to near-nothing."

The Globe and Mail, profiling Butterfield in 2014, described three years in which the Flickr team constantly had to fight for resources to improve and expand the service. That is how an acquired product usually dies. There is no shutdown notice, just a team that never grows and a roadmap that has to clear somebody else's committee.

The timing could not have been worse. Apple shipped the first iPhone in 2007, and on October 6, 2010 Kevin Systrom and Mike Krieger launched Instagram as an iPhone app. Flickr was built around uploading from a desktop computer, and the people who might have rebuilt it for phones had already resigned.

Did the free terabyte in 2013 fix anything?

Marissa Mayer became Yahoo's chief executive in July 2012 and put Flickr on the short list of properties worth reviving. On May 20, 2013 Yahoo relaunched the site with a new design and handed every user one terabyte of free storage, a number no rival was offering.

Uploads jumped and the press paid attention for a few months. What did not change was everything around the storage. The API stopped being a priority, the community tools stayed roughly where they had been in 2010, and the mobile apps trailed Instagram's.

Storage also turned out to be the wrong thing to give away. It drew people who wanted a free hard drive rather than an audience, and it made the paid tier look pointless. Flickr's own verdict, published on the company blog in November 2018, was plain: "Reducing the free storage offering ensures that we run Flickr on subscriptions, which guarantees that our focus is always on how to make your experience better."

By 2015 the numbers had flattened again and Yahoo was being taken apart. Verizon completed its purchase of Yahoo's operating business for 4.48 billion dollars on June 13, 2017, the day Mayer resigned. Flickr came along as a line item, the same way Tumblr did.

Who owns Flickr today?

SmugMug owns Flickr. The company, a paid photo-hosting service running since 2002, announced its intent to buy the site from Verizon on April 20, 2018, for a price neither side disclosed. SmugMug is privately held, and in 2026 it still runs Flickr and SmugMug as separate products.

Don MacAskill, SmugMug's chief executive, said in the announcement that year: "Uniting the SmugMug and Flickr brands will make the whole photography community stronger and better connected."

The clean-up was real engineering. In May 2019 SmugMug moved Flickr off Yahoo's data centers, a migration covering more than 100 million accounts and billions of photos and videos, with the final maintenance window on May 22, 2019.

It has not been easy money. In December 2019 MacAskill emailed Flickr Pro subscribers asking them to recruit more paying users, writing that the site "still needs your help. It's still losing money." Fewer than one percent of accounts were paying at that point. In December 2022 SmugMug launched the Flickr Foundation, a nonprofit set up to keep the archive readable for 100 years. It runs Flickr Commons, the program started with the Library of Congress in 2008, which now carries close to two million photographs from more than 100 institutions.

What a Flickr account costs in 2026

A free Flickr account holds 1,000 photos and videos. The cap was announced on November 1, 2018 and took effect on January 8, 2019; from February 5, 2019 accounts still over it had their oldest files deleted down to the limit. Images already carrying a Creative Commons license before November 1, 2018 were exempt, and Flickr said 97 percent of free accounts were under 1,000 items anyway.

Flickr Pro costs 11 dollars a month or 82 dollars a year in 2026, with a two-year plan at 148 dollars. Pro removes the cap, keeps full-resolution files, strips ads from your photostream for you and for visitors, and unlocks the detailed view statistics.

Flickr says it supports more than 100 million registered photographers, though it has not published audited user figures in years. Similarweb measured roughly 32.6 million visits to flickr.com in January 2026.

If you left photos there a decade ago, they are almost certainly still sitting in your photostream. Free tiers do get worse over time, as anyone who used Photobucket can confirm, but nothing in the Yahoo, Verizon or SmugMug handovers wiped the Flickr archive.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Flickr still around in 2026?

Yes. Flickr is still online in 2026, still accepting uploads, and has been owned by SmugMug since April 2018. A free Flickr account holds 1,000 photos and videos, and Flickr Pro costs 11 dollars a month or 82 dollars a year.

Who owns Flickr now?

SmugMug owns Flickr. The privately held photo-hosting company, founded in 2002, bought Flickr from Verizon in April 2018 for an undisclosed sum. Verizon had picked Flickr up in June 2017 as part of its 4.48 billion dollar purchase of Yahoo's operating business, and Yahoo had owned Flickr since March 2005.

Are old Flickr photos still there?

In almost every case, yes. The Flickr archive survived the Yahoo, Verizon and SmugMug handovers intact. The single mass deletion came in February 2019, when free accounts holding more than 1,000 items lost their oldest files, and photos licensed under Creative Commons before November 1, 2018 were spared even then.
