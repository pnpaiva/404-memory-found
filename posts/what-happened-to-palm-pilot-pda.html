Is the Palm Pilot Still Made? What It Costs in 2026

By Marcus Vale · Updated November 5, 2026 · First published March 23, 2026 · 6 min read

The Palm Pilot is not made any more. Palm Computing launched the first one, the Pilot 1000, in March 1996 at $299, sold about a million units in 18 months, and released its last new handhelds, the Palm TX and the $99 Palm Z22, in October 2005. The Palm name has belonged to the Chinese manufacturer TCL since 2014 and sits unused, while the company's software side survives as webOS inside LG television sets.

How did a block of wood become the Palm Pilot?

Jeff Hawkins founded Palm Computing in 1992 and built the product backwards, starting with the shape. He cut a piece of scrap lumber to shirt-pocket size, glued a paper face on it showing where a screen and buttons would go, carried a chopstick for a stylus, and used the thing in meetings for months. The Computer History Museum holds that wooden model in its collection.

"I walked around answering phone calls with this block of wood, and of course it didn't do anything," Hawkins told the PalmSource conference in 1999, in remarks reported by Wired News. He added: "I did it to see if it worked. I decided it worked pretty well."

The block produced one rule: do four things well instead of everything badly. Calendar, address book, to-do list, memo pad. The Pilot 1000 shipped in March 1996 with 128 KB of memory, a monochrome touchscreen and a cradle that synced to a PC, at $299. Palm Computing was a division of U.S. Robotics by then, and the Computer History Museum puts sales at about a million units in the product's first 18 months. Where the Apple Newton promised a computer in your pocket and failed at it, Palm promised a better pocket notebook, and that was the version people bought.

Why was Palm worth $53 billion for a single day?

The ownership moved faster than the products did. U.S. Robotics bought Palm Computing in 1995. 3Com bought U.S. Robotics in 1997. Hawkins and Donna Dubinsky left in 1998 to found Handspring, which built Palm OS machines called the Visor and competed with the company they had started.

3Com said in September 1999 that it would spin the handheld business out as Palm, Inc. The shares were priced at $38 on March 1, 2000, and started trading the next day. They touched $165 before closing at $95.06, which valued Palm at about $53.4 billion on paper. 3Com, which still held roughly 94 percent of Palm, finished that day worth closer to $28 billion.

Finance professors still teach the arithmetic, because 3Com's own share price implied that the Palm stake it retained was worth more than all of 3Com put together. Palm never grew into the number. Within two years it had split itself into a hardware company and a software company, then absorbed Handspring in 2003 and renamed the hardware half palmOne.

What did the Xerox lawsuit do to Graffiti?

Palm's text entry system, Graffiti, asked users to learn a simplified alphabet of one stroke per letter. Xerox said it owned the idea. Its patent, U.S. 5,596,656, covered a method called Unistrokes developed at its research labs, and Xerox sued 3Com, U.S. Robotics and Palm Computing over it in 1997.

A district court first granted summary judgment of non-infringement in 2000, a federal appeals court sent that ruling back, and Graffiti was found to infringe in 2001. The case turned again in May 2004, when Judge Michael Telesca of the Western District of New York threw the patent out. The Register reported that he found it "anticipated and rendered obvious by two pieces of 'prior art'", a 1983 paper by D. J. Burr and a 1984 Japanese patent filed by Tadahiro Nagayama.

Palm won, seven years after the first filing. By then the fight was over a way of entering text that on-screen keyboards had already made beside the point, on devices that were themselves being replaced.

What actually killed the PDA?

Arithmetic, mostly. Gartner counted about 13 million handhelds shipped worldwide in 2001, then 11.5 million in 2003, a 5.3 percent fall on the year, and by late 2003 its analysts were recording the eighth quarterly decline in a row. Gartner blamed smartphones for squeezing the cheap end of the market.

Buyers had not stopped wanting a pocket computer. They had started buying phones that did the same work and rang. Handspring's Treo 600 reached carriers in late 2003, after Handspring had merged into Palm, and it put the calendar, the contacts and the email behind a phone number. BlackBerry had already done that for corporate email and had the mail servers to back it up.

Palm read the room and left the room. The Palm Z22 arrived on October 12, 2005 at $99, with a color screen and about three ounces of weight, alongside the $299 Palm TX. They were the last new handheld organizers Palm designed. Chief executive Ed Colligan later confirmed the company was done with traditional handhelds, and everything Palm launched afterward was a phone.

Did Palm see the iPhone coming?

Not clearly. Colligan, one of the three people behind the first Pilot, was asked at a Churchill Club breakfast in November 2006 whether Apple could walk into the phone business. Macworld and other outlets reported his answer that month as "PC guys are not going to just figure this out. They're not going to just walk in." Daring Fireball published a closer transcription in 2022 showing he phrased it less flatly and was talking about the PC industry as a whole, but the reading stuck. Apple announced the iPhone about two months afterward.

Palm's real answer came two and a half years late. The Palm Pre launched on Sprint in June 2009 with webOS, a new operating system whose card-based multitasking and merged notifications were better than what shipped on phones at the time and were copied widely afterward. The problem was everything around it: one carrier, a small app catalog, and a company with no money left to advertise against Apple and Google.

Who owns the Palm Pilot name in 2026?

TCL, and it is not using it. Hewlett-Packard bought Palm for $1.2 billion in 2010, stopped webOS hardware in 2011, sold the webOS software to LG Electronics in February 2013, and sold the Palm trademark in October 2014 to a holding company tied to the Chinese manufacturer TCL. A licensee, Palm Ventures Group, put out a small Android companion phone under the name in 2018 and a set of earbuds in 2021. BGR reported in 2026 that the brand remains dormant and still sits with TCL. Our separate piece on whether Palm is still a company traces the paperwork in detail.

The software outlived the hardware by a wide margin. LG put webOS into television sets from 2014 and its 2026 sets run a version called webOS 26, which means the operating system Palm built for a failed phone now runs in living rooms where nobody connects it to Palm at all. Hawkins left the brand behind entirely and went into brain science at Numenta, the company he co-founded after Palm.

Where to find one today: working Palm III, Palm V and Palm VIIx units turn up steadily on Etsy. Untested units with no charger list from about $13 to $15, tested working ones with a stylus commonly ask around $40, and boxed or sealed examples run from $65 upward. Check two things before buying. The rechargeable models often have a dead internal battery, and the cradle and stylus are easy to lose and awkward to replace.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did the Palm Pilot stop being made?

Palm released its last new handheld organizers, the $299 Palm TX and the $99 Palm Z22, on October 12, 2005, and every device it launched after that was a phone. The Palm TX stayed on sale until about 2009, so a Palm Pilot bought new after 2005 was old stock rather than a current product.

How much did a Palm Pilot cost when it came out?

The Pilot 1000 launched in March 1996 at $299 with 128 KB of memory, from Palm Computing, then a division of U.S. Robotics. That was a 1996 price for a device that held a calendar, a contact list, a to-do list and notes, and nothing else.

Why did PDAs disappear?

PDAs disappeared because phones absorbed them. Gartner counted about 13 million handhelds shipped in 2001 and 11.5 million in 2003, and blamed smartphones such as the Treo 600, which arrived in late 2003, and the BlackBerry line for taking the low end. After the iPhone arrived in 2007, a separate pocket organizer had no job left to do.
