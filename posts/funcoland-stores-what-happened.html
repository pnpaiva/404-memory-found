What Happened to FuncoLand? Who Owns the Name Now

By Marcus Vale · Published November 17, 2026 · 5 min read

FuncoLand is gone as a store. Barnes & Noble outbid Electronics Boutique and bought its parent, Funco Inc., for $161.5 million in June 2000, Funco renamed itself GameStop, Inc. that December, and the FuncoLand signs came down over the following years. GameStop, Inc. still holds the FUNCOLAND trademark, and the chain's old in-house newsletter, Game Informer, outlived every store.

Why two chains fought over a strip-mall game store

FuncoLand was not dying when it was sold. Funco Inc., the Minneapolis company that ran the stores, reported net sales of about $197.9 million for the first nine months of fiscal 2000, up roughly 31 percent from about $151.1 million in the same period a year earlier. It closed that stretch with 401 stores, after opening 90 and closing one.

Funco credited the jump to operating more stores and to the Sega Dreamcast, which went on sale in the United States in September 1999.

What put the company in play was the share price, not the shelves. Funco missed its fourth-quarter earnings estimates and the stock fell. CNN/Money reported in April 2000 that the fight for the chain was set off by "a severe drop in the company's stock price earlier this year when it missed fourth-quarter earnings estimates." A 400-store used-game network had become cheap.

Who bought FuncoLand, and for how much?

Electronics Boutique moved first, agreeing in April 2000 to buy Funco at $17.50 a share. Barnes & Noble, which had taken over the Babbage's chain in October 1999, bid against it. Electronics Boutique raised its offer to $21 a share, about $135 million, and on April 26, 2000 Barnes & Noble went to $24.75 a share, valuing Funco at $161.5 million.

Funco let the auction run. "Our objective is to maximize shareholder value, so we will take our time here and see what happens," company president Stanley Bodine told CNN/Money in April 2000.

Electronics Boutique stopped raising in early May 2000 and Barnes & Noble completed the purchase the following month. GameStop's later annual report puts the sequence in one line: "In June 2000, Barnes & Noble acquired Funco and thereafter, Babbage's became a wholly-owned subsidiary of Funco."

The logic on both sides of the auction was the trade counter. A used game bought across the counter and resold carries a margin a new release cannot, and every trade hands the customer store credit that brings them back. Electronics Boutique and Barnes & Noble were not bidding for 401 leases in strip malls. They were bidding for the habit those leases had built, at a moment when the stock market had marked it down.

That last part is what most retellings get backwards. Funco was not swallowed by GameStop. Funco was the company that survived the paperwork, and in December 2000 it renamed itself GameStop, Inc. The chain behind FuncoLand legally became the chain that replaced it.

When did the FuncoLand signs come down?

Slowly. A new name on the corporate letterhead in December 2000 did not reach the strip malls for years, because conversions were scheduled market by market as leases, stock and staff training allowed.

GameStop's annual report for the fiscal year that ended February 1, 2003 was still counting four nameplates: "As of February 1, 2003, we operated 1,231 stores, in 49 states, Puerto Rico and Guam, under the names GameStop, Babbage's, Software Etc. and FuncoLand."

Accounts of the last FuncoLand storefront differ, with secondary histories putting the end of the name between 2003 and 2005. What is documented in the filings is that FuncoLand was still above doors in February 2003, two and a half years after the sale, and that the conversion worked: GameStop is still in business while all three of the mall chains it absorbed are gone as retail names.

What the monthly price sheets actually were

The thing people remember is not the carpet or the demo kiosks. It is the price sheet: a newspaper-style broadsheet stacked at the counter, listing what the store would pay for a used game and what it would charge for it, title by title and system by system.

The sheet was the business model. A customer brought in a stack of cartridges, a clerk read down the column, and the trade value came off a printed page instead of a negotiation. Sheets carried a date and were replaced as the market moved, which is why a single page preserves what a given month of 1996 or 2000 paid for a specific game.

Those sheets, with store signage and employee shirts, are what turns up when people hunt FuncoLand material now, and scanned pages circulate on collector forums as a record of 1990s trade values. It is the same pattern that makes old game magazines collectible: the value sits in the complete, dated artifact rather than in the information printed on it.

Where to find one today: FuncoLand paper and store ephemera surface in vintage and handmade marketplaces rather than game shops, and Etsy's FuncoLand listings are the usual place to start looking.

What is left of FuncoLand in 2026?

No stores, one live trademark and one magazine.

The FUNCOLAND word mark, registration number 2085805, was registered on August 5, 1997 and appears in trademark records as registered and renewed, with a status date of January 17, 2018. The owner on the record is GameStop, Inc. An earlier FUNCOLAND registration, number 1796604, filed in February 1993, is shown the same way. The side marks did not last: FUNCOLAND NATIONAL VIDEO GAME CHAMPIONSHIPS was cancelled in 2004 and FUNCOLAND FUN CLUB in 2005.

The owner is a smaller company than the one that did the converting. GameStop reported 2,206 stores worldwide as of January 31, 2026, split between 1,598 in the United States, 308 in Europe and 300 in Australia, down from 3,203 a year earlier.

The part of FuncoLand that outlived every store is a magazine. Game Informer started in August 1991 as an in-house newsletter published by FuncoLand for its own customers, passed to GameStop with the rest of the company in 2000, and has changed hands again since. A chain that vanished from strip malls still has a byline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who owns FuncoLand now?

GameStop, Inc. owns the FuncoLand name. Barnes & Noble bought the parent company, Funco Inc., for $161.5 million in June 2000, Funco renamed itself GameStop, Inc. that December, and trademark records still list GameStop, Inc. as the owner of the FUNCOLAND registration. No FuncoLand stores remain.

Is FuncoLand coming back?

Nothing points to it. GameStop has held the FuncoLand trademark since the December 2000 renaming and has not opened a store under the name in the 25 years since, while shrinking its own footprint to 2,206 stores worldwide as of January 31, 2026.

Did FuncoLand start Game Informer?

Yes. Game Informer began in August 1991 as an in-house newsletter that FuncoLand published for its customers, and it went to GameStop when Funco Inc. was renamed in December 2000. The magazine has outlived every FuncoLand store.
