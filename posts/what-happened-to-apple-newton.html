Why the Apple Newton Failed and What It Costs Now

2026-04-01 | Updated 2026-09-25 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

The Apple Newton was Apple's handheld computer line, launched on August 2, 1993 at $699 and canceled by Steve Jobs on February 27, 1998. It failed because the handwriting recognition did not work at launch, the price never came down, and a $299 PalmPilot did the same job reliably from the first day. The Newton is gone for good: there has been no Newton device and no Newton OS release since 1998, and Apple Inc. still owns the name.

Has Apple been profitable ever since the Newton died?

The Apple Newton was Apple's product, and the question people type into Google is whether the company has been in the black every year since it pulled the plug. Almost. There is one break in the run.

Apple lost $1.05 billion in fiscal 1997. In fiscal 1998, the year the Newton was canceled, it reported net income of $309 million. Fiscal 1999 brought $601 million and fiscal 2000 brought $786 million.

Then fiscal 2001 broke the streak with a net loss of $25 million. That year is the part left out whenever someone says Apple has been profitable since 1998.

The turnaround itself was real. Apple spent the middle of the 1990s burning through cash it did not have, and fiscal 1998 was also the year of the first iMac. The Newton was cut in the same twelve months the company returned to profit.

Why did the Apple Newton fail?

John Sculley, then Apple's chief executive, coined the phrase personal digital assistant at the Consumer Electronics Show on January 7, 1992. He showed a Newton prototype at the summer show in Chicago on May 29, 1992 and promised "nothing less than a revolution."

The first MessagePad reached buyers on August 2, 1993 at $699. The pitch was that it would read ordinary handwriting with no training and no special alphabet, using a recognition engine Apple licensed from ParaGraph International.

It did not. The recognizer guessed at words, and it guessed wrong often enough that the failure became the product's public identity inside a month. Garry Trudeau put it in Doonesbury on August 27, 1993, in a strip where "Catching on?" came back as "Egg freckles." The phrase followed the Newton for the rest of its life.

Sales never recovered from the reviews. Apple moved about 50,000 MessagePads by the end of November 1993 and presented the number as a strong start. It was not enough to carry a new platform, and the price never came down to meet the market: the last model, the MessagePad 2100, launched on November 7, 1997 at $999.

Is the Apple Newton still around in 2026?

No. Steve Jobs canceled the Newton hardware and the Newton OS platform on February 27, 1998, a few months after returning to run the company. The announcement took the MessagePad 2100 and the eMate 300, the keyboard model Apple had introduced for schools on March 7, 1997 at $799, off the price list together.

The reason was stated plainly at the time. "This decision is consistent with our strategy to focus all of our software development resources on extending the Macintosh operating system," Jobs said in the announcement of February 27, 1998 reported by TidBITS. He added that "to realize our ambitious plans we must focus all of our efforts in one direction."

There has been no Newton device and no Newton OS release since. Apple Inc. still exists and still owns the name, but it did not sell another pocket computer until the iPhone, and nothing in the current lineup carries the Newton brand.

Where to find one today. Complete MessagePads, the ones with the box, stylus, power adapter and software, are what hold value: RR Auction sold a fully accessorized MessagePad 2100 for $541 in 2023 against a pre-sale estimate of $200. Tested working units with no box and no accessories go for a fraction of that, and vintage sellers list them on Etsy. A corroded battery pack is the most common thing wrong with a listing.

What the Apple Newton got right before Apple cut it

The hardware was not the weak part. The original MessagePad ran a 20 MHz ARM 610 with 640 KB of RAM behind a 336 by 240 monochrome screen, which was real computing power for something that fit in a jacket pocket in 1993.

The recognition problem was fixed, just too late to matter. Newton OS 2.0, released in March 1996, dropped the licensed engine for one Apple wrote itself. It read printed letters instead of trying to decode running handwriting, and it worked well enough that owners defended it for years afterward.

The later hardware was better still. The MessagePad 2000 arrived in March 1997 with a much faster StrongARM processor, and the 2100 followed that November. By then the Doonesbury strip was three and a half years old and the reputation was set.

The software ideas outlived the platform. Newton kept data in shared stores that any application could read, so a note with a name and a time in it could offer to become a calendar entry on its own. Phone software rebuilt that idea more than a decade later.

How a $299 PalmPilot took the market Apple defined

Palm Computing's answer was to stop trying to read natural handwriting at all. Its Graffiti system made the user learn a simplified stroke alphabet, one motion per letter, inside a fixed box on the screen. It asked less of the machine and more of the owner, and it was reliable from the first day.

Price finished the argument. The Pilot 1000 shipped in March 1996 at $299, with the 512 KB Pilot 5000 at $369. Apple was asking $699 for the original MessagePad and $999 for the 2100.

So the category Apple had named and spent four years defining went to a cheaper device that did fewer things and did not embarrass its owner in a meeting. Palm took the handheld market the Newton had been built for, and Apple was out of it by February 1998.

The ARM stake that outlasted the MessagePad

The Newton needed a processor that could run all day on batteries, and nothing on the market managed it, so Apple went in with Acorn Computers and VLSI Technology in 1990 on the chip venture that became ARM. The MessagePad was the first Apple product to ship with the result.

The stake turned out to be worth more than the product it was bought for. In fiscal 1999 Apple sold about 163 million ARM shares for net proceeds of roughly $245 million and a gain before taxes of about $230 million. In fiscal 2000 it sold roughly $372 million more.

Put those two years together and the shares Apple bought to get a chip for a 1993 handheld returned more than the company's entire fiscal 1998 profit of $309 million. The instruction set Apple went shopping for because a pocket computer had to last a day on batteries now runs every iPhone and every Mac built on Apple silicon.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an Apple Newton worth today?

Complete examples do best. RR Auction sold an Apple Newton MessagePad 2100 with its box, power adapter, stylus, software, battery packs, cables and keyboard for $541 in 2023, against a pre-sale estimate of $200. A loose MessagePad with no box and no accessories sells for a fraction of that.

Why did Steve Jobs kill the Apple Newton?

Steve Jobs canceled the Apple Newton platform on February 27, 1998, months after returning to a company that had lost $1.05 billion in fiscal 1997. He said the decision was "consistent with our strategy to focus all of our software development resources on extending the Macintosh operating system," and a slow-selling four-figure MessagePad 2100 was an easy cut.

Has Apple been profitable every year since 1998?

Not quite. Apple reported net income of $309 million in fiscal 1998, $601 million in fiscal 1999 and $786 million in fiscal 2000, then a net loss of $25 million in fiscal 2001. The Apple Newton was canceled in February 1998, in the first of those profitable years.
