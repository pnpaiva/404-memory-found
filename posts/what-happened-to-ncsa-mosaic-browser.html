Is NCSA Mosaic Still Around? The Browser in 2026

2026-05-08 | Updated 2026-09-24 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

NCSA Mosaic is no longer a working browser. The National Center for Supercomputing Applications shipped the last version, Mosaic 3.0, on January 7, 1997 and stopped development the same day. The University of Illinois still holds the Mosaic name and copyright, and archived builds are free to download, but they cannot render the 2026 web.

Quick facts

January 23, 1993, by NCSA at the University of Illinois

Netscape Navigator, then Internet Explorer, then Chrome

What did Mosaic do that earlier browsers could not?

The web already existed when Mosaic arrived. Tim Berners-Lee had built the first server and the first browser at CERN by late 1990, and by 1992 a small set of academic browsers was in circulation, including ViolaWWW.

None of them reached an audience outside research labs. They were text only, or they ran on one flavor of Unix workstation, or they looked like a terminal session. Nothing about them suggested the web could be a medium for anyone who was not a physicist.

Mosaic changed four things at once. It drew images inside the page rather than popping them into a separate viewer. It put the address in a visible bar at the top with a back button that worked. It made bookmarks a normal feature instead of a text file. And it shipped free for Macintosh and Microsoft Windows, not only Unix, which is the part that mattered most.

NCSA's Mike Folk summed up the thinking on the center's own Mosaic page: "these [other browsers] don't do pictures. We're all about pictures." That was the whole pitch, and it was enough.

Who built Mosaic, and what did NCSA get out of it?

The National Center for Supercomputing Applications opened at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign in 1986 with National Science Foundation money and a plain mandate: make supercomputers usable by researchers in other fields. Building friendly front ends was the job description.

Marc Andreessen, an undergraduate working as a student programmer, and Eric Bina, a staff engineer, started on a graphical browser for the X Window System in late 1992. Bina wrote most of the parsing and rendering. Andreessen drove the interface and did the talking on mailing lists. The first release was announced on January 23, 1993, version 1.0 followed in April, and the Macintosh and Windows ports landed later the same year.

Then the numbers stopped looking academic. After the public release in November 1993, more than 40,000 people downloaded a copy in the first month, and by the spring of 1994 over a million copies were in use. A research center funded to write scientific tools suddenly had a consumer product, a support queue, and no budget for either. Andreessen had already graduated and left for California.

Why did one email about the IMG tag change the web?

On February 25, 1993, Andreessen posted a short message to the WWW-Talk mailing list. "I'd like to propose a new, optional HTML tag: IMG," it began, with a required SRC argument naming an image file to pull over the network and draw where the tag sat.

It was not really a proposal. Bina had already implemented it, and Andreessen said so in the same message: the feature was working in X Mosaic and NCSA would be using it either way. Berners-Lee favored a more general mechanism and a slower standards process. Mosaic shipped IMG anyway, and IMG was later written into the HTML 2.0 specification.

Everything visual about the web descends from that decision. Personal home pages worked because people could post photographs. News sites worked because layouts needed pictures. The first banner ad, sold by HotWired in 1994, was a 468 by 60 pixel image, which is to say it was an IMG tag with an invoice attached. The standards process caught up afterward, as it usually does.

Who owns NCSA Mosaic today?

The University of Illinois does. The university held the copyright on the code and the Mosaic trademark from the start, and it still holds both in 2026. Nobody sells the browser and nobody supports it.

The commercial rights took a longer route. In 1994 NCSA licensed Mosaic to Spyglass, Inc. of Champaign, Illinois, granting the right to modify, distribute and sublicense the browser along with the Mosaic name. The university was protective of that name: when Andreessen and Jim Clark incorporated Mosaic Communications Corporation on April 4, 1994, Illinois objected, and on November 14, 1994 the company became Netscape Communications Corporation.

Spyglass itself did not last either. OpenTV acquired the company in a stock deal that closed in July 2000, valued at roughly $2.5 billion on a fully diluted basis using OpenTV's share price on March 24, 2000. OpenTV was taken over completely by the Kudelski Group in 2009 and now sits inside Kudelski's NAGRA division as a pay-television brand. The Mosaic lineage ended in set-top boxes.

How did Mosaic code end up inside Internet Explorer?

The usual shorthand is that Internet Explorer was Mosaic with a new label. The truth is narrower. Spyglass licensed the technology and the trademarks from NCSA and then wrote its own browser. Eric Sink, who led the Spyglass Mosaic project, was blunt about it in his 2003 account, Memoirs From the Browser Wars: "we never used any of the code. We wrote our browser implementations completely from scratch."

Microsoft licensed Spyglass Mosaic in 1995 for $2 million plus a royalty on every copy sold, and shipped Internet Explorer 1.0 later that year in the Plus! pack for Windows 95. Then it bundled the browser with Windows at no charge. No copies sold meant no royalty owed, and Spyglass watched its licensing revenue go to zero while its licensee took the market.

The dispute ended in January 1997. Spyglass told the Securities and Exchange Commission in a 1997 Form 10-Q that it had amended the arrangement, "converting Microsoft's existing license for the Spyglass Mosaic browser technology into a fully paid-up license in consideration of an additional $8,000,000 payment from Microsoft." Netscape, which had taken the market from Mosaic in the first place, lost it to Internet Explorer over the following three years.

Can you download and run Mosaic in 2026?

Yes, with effort and low expectations. Builds of Mosaic 2.x and 3.0 for Windows and Unix are archived on the Internet Archive, and a Mosaic 2.7 source tree is mirrored on GitHub. Getting one running means a virtual machine, an emulator, or old hardware.

What you get is a museum piece. Mosaic predates CSS, JavaScript and every modern encryption standard, so most sites in 2026 either refuse the connection or arrive as a column of unstyled text. It is a good way to see what 1993 looked like and a bad way to check email.

The browser that outlived it is every browser. Address bar along the top, back button on the left, forward next to it, bookmarks in a menu, images inline with the words: open Chrome, Safari or Edge in 2026 and the layout is the one two people settled on in a basement office in Illinois before either of them had a business plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

When was NCSA Mosaic released and when did it shut down?

NCSA Mosaic was first released on January 23, 1993, for the X Window System, with version 1.0 following in April 1993 and Macintosh and Windows versions later that year. The National Center for Supercomputing Applications shipped the final version, Mosaic 3.0, on January 7, 1997, and ended development the same day.

Who created NCSA Mosaic?

NCSA Mosaic was built at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, starting in late 1992. Marc Andreessen, then an undergraduate student programmer, led the project and the interface, while Eric Bina wrote most of the parsing and rendering code. Andreessen left in 1993 and co-founded the company that was renamed Netscape Communications Corporation in November 1994.

Was NCSA Mosaic the first web browser?

No. Tim Berners-Lee wrote the first web browser, WorldWideWeb, at CERN in 1990, and several others followed before NCSA Mosaic appeared in January 1993. Mosaic was the first browser to draw images inline with text and the first to ship free on Macintosh and Windows, which is why it is remembered as the browser that carried the web to a general audience.
