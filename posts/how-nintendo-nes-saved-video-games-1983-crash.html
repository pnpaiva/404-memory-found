How the NES Saved Video Games After the 1983 Crash

2026-05-10 | Updated 2026-09-24 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

The Nintendo Entertainment System pulled the American console business out of a crash that cut the market from about $3.2 billion in 1983 to roughly $100 million in 1985. Nintendo did it by carrying the retail risk itself, locking third-party publishers into a quality program, and shipping Super Mario Bros. with the hardware. The NES itself was discontinued in North America in 1995, but Nintendo still sells its games: the Nintendo Switch Online classic library held 87 NES titles in September 2026.

What did the 1983 crash actually do to the market?

American home video game revenue peaked at about $3.2 billion in 1983. Two years later the same market was worth roughly $100 million. That is a fall of almost 97 percent, and it hit an industry barely a decade old.

The causes stacked on top of each other. Consoles multiplied, cartridge publishers multiplied faster, and nobody was checking quality. Atari had no licensing control over its own 2600 library, so anyone could ship anything onto it. Retailers ended up holding inventory they could not move.

Then came the discounting. Cartridges that had listed around $35 were cleared for a few dollars, which taught buyers to wait for the markdown and killed full-price sales for everyone. Stores drew the obvious conclusion and cut the category.

By 1984 the toy chains and department stores had written home video games off as a craze that had already peaked. Shelf space went to other products, and buyers stopped taking pitches. That was the retail environment Nintendo walked into in 1985, and it explains nearly every strange decision that followed.

Why did Nintendo rename the Famicom for America?

Nintendo's Japanese console, the Family Computer or Famicom, launched on July 15, 1983 at 14,800 yen, with home ports of Donkey Kong, Donkey Kong Jr. and Popeye. It sold well from the start.

The hardware came from a team under engineer Masayuki Uemura, working to instructions from Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi that were commercial as much as technical. Yamauchi wanted cartridges rather than built-in games, and he wanted a lead nobody could close. "He told me to make a machine that wouldn't have any competitors for three years," Uemura recalled in Nintendo's Iwata Asks interview series in 2010.

Selling that machine in America meant disguising what it was. Nintendo of America turned the red and white Famicom into a gray and black box with a front-loading slot that worked like a VCR, because VCRs were a growing category in 1985 and video games were not. The console became the Nintendo Entertainment System, with the middle word doing the work. Cartridges became Game Paks. The R.O.B. robot and the Zapper light gun were added so the system could be sold as a toy, a category buyers were still willing to stock.

What happened in the New York test launch of 1985?

Nintendo did not launch nationally. It launched in one city. The NES went on sale in New York on October 18, 1985 with a library of 18 games and an initial shipment of about 100,000 systems.

The terms mattered more than the hardware. Nintendo gave retailers a buyback guarantee: if a system had not sold after 90 days, the store could send it back at no cost. Nintendo staff built the displays, ran the demo kiosks and kept the inventory risk on Nintendo's own balance sheet. Stores were being asked to give up shelf space, not money.

It worked well enough to continue. Nintendo said in 1986 that it had sold nearly 90,000 units in nine weeks during the New York test. The rollout widened through 1986 until the NES was national, and the numbers compounded from there. By 1990, 30 percent of American households owned an NES, against 23 percent that owned a personal computer of any kind.

How did Super Mario Bros. and Nintendo Power keep it selling?

Hardware got the NES onto shelves. Software kept it there. Super Mario Bros. reached Japan on September 13, 1985, was part of the first wave of NES titles in North America, and became the pack-in that defined the system once Nintendo paired it with Duck Hunt in the Action Set bundle. It sold more than 40 million copies on the NES and held the record for the best-selling video game in the world for roughly two decades.

Super Mario Bros. 3 followed in North America in 1990 and sold more than 17 million copies on its own, with no hardware bundle attached to the number.

Nintendo built a media operation around the console as well. Nintendo Power launched with a July and August 1988 issue printed in 3.6 million copies, and a year of six issues cost $15 by subscription against $3.50 an issue on the newsstand. Previews, maps and tips all came from the platform holder, which meant Nintendo effectively ran the enthusiast press covering its own system. Peripherals such as the Power Glove sold poorly and advertised well.

How did the 10NES lockout chip change the business?

Nintendo's answer to the flood of unchecked software that helped sink Atari was a piece of silicon. The 10NES chip sat in every console and in every licensed Game Pak, and the two had to complete a handshake before a game would boot.

The chip was the enforcement mechanism for a licensing program. Publishers had to manufacture cartridges through Nintendo, accept minimum order quantities, limit how many titles they shipped in a year, and pass a review that ended in the gold Nintendo Seal of Quality. Retailers got a promise that the library had been filtered before it reached them. That promise is what kept the shelf space Nintendo had fought for.

It also produced litigation. Atari Games set up a subsidiary called Tengen to break the lock and ship unlicensed cartridges. The Federal Circuit held in 1992, in Atari Games Corp. v. Nintendo of America Inc., that "reverse engineering object code to discern the unprotectable ideas in a computer program is a fair use," yet Atari still lost, because it had obtained the 10NES source code from the Copyright Office under false pretenses. Cheat hardware such as the Game Genie fought Nintendo on the same ground from a different direction.

Is the NES still around in 2026?

The hardware is retired. Nintendo ended NES support in North America on August 14, 1995, and Wario's Woods was the last licensed game released there. In Japan, Nintendo kept selling the Famicom until September 2003 and repaired units until October 31, 2007, when spare parts ran out. A cheaper top-loading redesign, the NES-101, had arrived in October 1993 at $49.99, near the end of the system's retail life.

The lifetime figure Nintendo reports for the Famicom and the NES together is 61.91 million units, about 34 million of them in North America and 19.32 million in Japan.

What survives is the software. Nintendo sells the catalog through Nintendo Switch Online, where the classic library reached 87 NES titles in September 2026. An individual membership costs $19.99 a year in the United States, which is the cheapest legal route into the library. The NES Classic Edition, the small plug-in box with a fixed set of built-in games, is no longer manufactured.

Where to find one today. A loose front-loading NES with a controller and cables typically runs about $35 to $60 in 2026, while a fully refurbished console with a warranty from a specialist retailer runs closer to $170. Stone Age Gamer stocks refurbished NES hardware and accessories.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many NES units were sold?

Nintendo's lifetime figure for the Famicom and the Nintendo Entertainment System combined is 61.91 million units worldwide, of which about 34 million sold in North America and 19.32 million in Japan. The Famicom launched in Japan on July 15, 1983, and the NES reached its first American test market in New York on October 18, 1985.

Is the NES still supported by Nintendo in 2026?

No. Nintendo ended NES support in North America on August 14, 1995, and stopped repairing Famicom consoles in Japan on October 31, 2007. Nintendo does still sell NES games: the Nintendo Switch Online classic library held 87 NES titles in September 2026, and an individual membership costs $19.99 a year in the United States.

What was the best-selling NES game?
