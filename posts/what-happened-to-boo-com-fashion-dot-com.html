Who Owns Boo.com Now? Hostelworld Holds the Name

2026-04-02 | Updated 2026-10-28 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Boo.com is owned by Hostelworld Group plc, the hostel booking company that still trades in London, and no fashion store has sold anything at the address since the company went into receivership on 18 May 2000. The brand and the domain sold for about $375,000 after Boo.com spent roughly $135 million in 18 months. A travel site ran at boo.com from May 2007 until October 2010, and nothing has replaced it.

Quick facts
Launched
3 November 1999, from London
Founders
Ernst Malmsten, Kajsa Leander and Patrik Hedelin

Dead since 18 May 2000; no store at boo.com

Hostelworld Group plc, formerly Web Reservations International

What was Boo.com supposed to sell?

Boo.com was an online store for designer sportswear and street fashion, founded in 1998 by three Swedes: Ernst Malmsten, Kajsa Leander and Patrik Hedelin. They arrived with a record. Malmsten and Leander had built the Swedish online bookstore Bokus.com and sold it in 1998, and Hedelin brokered the deal.

The technology was the pitch. Shoppers could drag a garment onto a 3D model, zoom in and rotate it, while a cartoon assistant called Miss Boo offered suggestions. Nothing else in 1999 retail looked like it, and nothing else in 1999 retail cost as much to build.

Investors bought the story. J.P. Morgan, Goldman Sachs, Bernard Arnault of LVMH and the Benetton family put money in across three rounds. Before a single garment had been sold, Boo.com had committed about $25 million to advertising and public relations, and Malmsten counted that as money well spent. "With a marketing and PR spend of only $22.4 million we had managed to create a worldwide brand," he wrote in the account quoted in Dave Chaffey's case study of the company for Smart Insights.

The store that all of this was advertising did not open for another year.

Why could almost nobody use the site in 1999?

Boo.com opened on 3 November 1999, about six months later than planned, after a string of missed launch dates. What shoppers found was a shop that fought them.

The site leaned on JavaScript and Flash, which corporate firewalls blocked and older browsers handled badly. It did not run on Macintosh computers at all, which cut out a large share of the design and media crowd the brand was aimed at. Early visitors hit glitches and gaps in the catalogue.

Then there was the bandwidth. The 3D viewer and the Miss Boo animations had to download before a shopper saw a single price, and most of the audience in 1999 was on a dial-up modem. Our dial-up speed comparison puts a 56k line next to a 2026 connection and shows the gap. A store built for broadband, served over a line that slow, is not a store. It is a waiting room.

Traffic and sales both grew through the first quarter of trading and still came in under target, with returns running higher than the company had planned for. Boo.com kept rebuilding the site after launch, and each rebuild cost money it was already spending faster than it could raise.

How did $135 million disappear in 18 months?

In plain sight, mostly. By October 1999, a month before the store opened, Boo.com had around 400 employees and eight offices, with staff in Amsterdam, Munich, New York, Paris and Stockholm as well as London.

The headquarters and the call centre sat on Carnaby Street in London, one of the most expensive addresses in British retail, rather than somewhere cheaper. That single choice became the shorthand for how the company spent.

The founders were funding a global logistics and technology operation out of venture capital while the shop itself sold to a thin slice of the people who visited. The arithmetic was not unusual for the period. Pets.com ran the same equation in the United States, spending ahead of demand on the assumption that the next round would always arrive.

What set Boo.com apart was the speed. The roughly $135 million counted in the Smart Insights case study was gone in 18 months, and the store had been open for only six of them.

What happened on 18 May 2000?

Boo.com ran out of road. Malmsten spent the final weeks chasing a bridge round from backers who had already watched the Nasdaq slide through March and April of 2000.

"Unless we raise $20 million by midnight, boo.com is dead," Malmsten said on 18 May 2000, in a line recorded in the Smart Insights case study. Around half the money was pledged. It was not enough and it was not in time.

KPMG was called in as receiver that day to wind the company up and salvage what value was left. About 300 people lost their jobs across London, Stockholm, Paris, New York and Munich, and the European press treated the collapse as the first big internet casualty on that side of the Atlantic.

The verdict from the analysts was that the idea had been sound and the company had not been. Forrester Research called the failure "poor execution of a good idea" in an assessment reported by The Register in July 2000. Malmsten published his own account, "Boo Hoo: A Dot.com Story from Concept to Catastrophe", in 2001.

Is there anything at boo.com in 2026?

No shop, no store, no fashion. The domain belongs to Hostelworld Group plc, the hostel booking company listed in London, and it points visitors at hostelworld.com.

The wreckage was split in two at the liquidation. Bright Station, the British technology company run by Dan Wagner, bought Boo.com's e-commerce software from the receivers for 250,000 pounds. Fashionmall.com, a New York fashion portal, bought the brand, the domain and the Miss Boo character for about $375,000, the price Forbes filed in May 2000 under the headline "Boo.com Assets Sell for Spooky Price: $375,000". It relaunched boo.com in October 2000 as a portal pointing at other people's shops.

Web Reservations International, the Dublin company behind Hostelworld, later bought the domain and relaunched boo.com in May 2007 as a travel planning site. The private equity firm Hellman and Friedman bought Web Reservations International in 2009, and the travel version of Boo.com announced its closure in October 2010. The company wanted to "channel its resources" into its existing brands, an official told PhocusWire at the time.

Web Reservations International became Hostelworld Group plc and is still trading: it reported net revenue of 52.2 million euros for the first half of 2026, up 12 percent. The boo.com name has sat unused on its books ever since.

Did anyone make the Boo.com idea work?

Two companies founded in 2000, months after Boo.com closed, built what Boo.com had described. ASOS was founded on 3 June 2000, selling affordable fashion to young British shoppers. Net-a-Porter launched the same year under Natalie Massenet, selling luxury labels. Both started narrow, in one market, with plain product photography and pages that loaded.

The difference was not vision. It was sequencing. Boo.com spent about $25 million telling the world about a store before the store worked, then shipped a 3D viewer the average 1999 connection could not carry. ASOS and Net-a-Porter waited for broadband and spent on stock and logistics instead.

The shape of the failure keeps recurring, which is why the case is still taught in marketing courses. Amazon came within sight of the same cliff in 2000 and survived because it had revenue and a bond issue behind it. Boo.com had neither. It had a story, about $135 million and six months of trading.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who owns Boo.com now?

Boo.com is owned by Hostelworld Group plc, the London-listed hostel booking company that was called Web Reservations International when it bought the domain. Hostelworld has held the name since the late 2000s, ran a travel site there from May 2007 to October 2010, and now points boo.com at hostelworld.com. No fashion business has traded at the address since May 2000.

Why did Boo.com fail?

Boo.com failed because it spent about $135 million in 18 months on a global fashion site most shoppers in 1999 could not use. The store opened on 3 November 1999 needing JavaScript, Flash and a 3D product viewer, would not run on Macintosh computers, and crawled over dial-up connections. When investors refused a final $20 million, Boo.com went into receivership on 18 May 2000.

Is Boo.com still a website?

Not as a site of its own. Boo.com has not sold clothes since May 2000, and the travel site that Web Reservations International ran at boo.com from May 2007 closed in October 2010. The domain now belongs to Hostelworld Group plc and forwards visitors to hostelworld.com instead of loading a boo.com page.
