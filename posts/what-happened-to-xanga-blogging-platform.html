Is Xanga Still Around in 2026? What Is Left of It

2026-05-06 | Updated 2026-11-11 | By Dana Reyes | 7 min read

Xanga still exists in 2026 as Xanga 2.0, a paid WordPress-based service at xanga.com that has been closed to new members since the original site was taken down on August 31, 2013. It is still run by Xanga.com, Inc., the company Marc Ginsburg, John Hiler and Dan Huddle founded in New York in 1999, and no sale has been announced since. The FTC put Xanga at about 25 million registered accounts in 2005, the year before it fined the company $1 million for breaking children's privacy law.

How did a book review site become a teenage diary?

Xanga launched in 1999, founded in New York by Marc Ginsburg, John Hiler and Dan Huddle. The first version was not a diary platform. It was a place to post short book and music reviews and read what other people made of the same records.

The reviews were never the draw. The comment threads under them were. Xanga added weblog tools to user profiles and made blogging the core product in 2000, giving every account a dated entry stream and an open comment box. That sounds ordinary now. In 2000, most people online had never read a blog, let alone kept one.

The audience that turned up was narrower than the founders planned and far more committed than they expected. Xanga's center of gravity was American high school and college students, with a heavy concentration in the emo, punk and alternative music scenes. The posts were confessional: arguments with parents, breakups, friendship math, song lyrics pasted in without explanation, messages aimed at one person who would know exactly who they were for. GeoCities had given teenagers a homepage. Xanga gave them a deadline.

What were eProps and blogrings?

Xanga treated writing as a social act before the phrase social media was in general use, and it shipped the machinery early.

Comments and eProps arrived on December 8, 2000. An eProp was a one-click token of approval, the job a Like button would later do, and Xanga had it years before Facebook did. The supply was deliberately tight: two per entry was the most a reader could hand over at once.

Blogrings followed on March 6, 2001. These were user-made groups built around a band, a high school, an anime series or a mood, searchable by topic, and they are how strangers with the same taste found each other. Membership was rationed too. Classic accounts could hold four rings and premium accounts seven, until a relaunch in January 2004 raised the limit to eight for everyone.

The pages themselves were editable down to the markup. Users rewrote layouts with their own HTML and CSS, set backgrounds, fonts and cursors, and embedded a song that started playing the second a visitor arrived. Opening a Xanga page in 2004 usually meant reaching for the volume key first.

Why did the FTC fine Xanga $1 million?

On September 7, 2006, the Federal Trade Commission announced that Xanga.com and its principals, John Hiler and Marc Ginsburg, would pay a $1 million civil penalty for violating the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act. The Department of Justice filed the complaint on the agency's behalf in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. The FTC called it the largest COPPA penalty it had assessed to that date, more than twice the next largest.

The case was narrow and hard to argue with. Xanga's own terms said members had to be 13 or older, but the signup form let visitors through even when they entered a birth date showing they were younger. The complaint states that "approximately 1.7 million Xanga.com accounts were opened by children under age 13" over the previous five years, and that their personal information was collected, used and disclosed without notice to parents or parental consent.

"Protecting kids' privacy online is a top priority for America's parents, and for the FTC," said FTC Chairman Deborah Platt Majoras in the agency's 2006 announcement.

The same release put Xanga at about 25 million registered accounts in 2005, so the penalty landed on a large site with thin revenue. The order also required Xanga to delete information collected in breach of the rule. The lasting damage was operational: tighter age gates closed the youngest end of the signup funnel exactly when Xanga needed growth to sell advertising.

How did Facebook take Xanga's audience?

Facebook opened registration beyond schools and colleges to anyone 13 or older with a valid email address in September 2006, the same month the FTC penalty against Xanga became public.

Facebook did not beat Xanga at blogging. It removed the need to blog. A Xanga entry took a paragraph, some formatting and a reason to write. A status update took six words about a math test. Groups were easier to find than blogrings, the Like button was simpler than eProps, and a single uniform layout turned out to be what most people wanted once the novelty of hand-written HTML wore off.

Money was the other half of it. Xanga ran on advertising plus a paid premium tier, and its users were teenagers with no budget for subscriptions, writing the kind of posts advertisers were not eager to sit beside. MySpace lost its own audience to the same migration, and Tumblr later ran into the identical problem of a large young readership that is hard to monetize.

What happened in the 2013 fundraiser?

By spring 2013 the servers cost more than the site earned. On May 30, 2013 the Xanga team put two options to its users. As WP Tavern reported that year, "The first was to relaunch the service using WordPress as the platform while the second was to shut the site down." The target was about $60,000 by July 15, and John Hiler said he would add $10,000 himself once the campaign passed $50,000.

It came close. The drive took $58,640 from nearly 1,000 contributors, short of the $60,000 goal, and the team went ahead anyway. The rebuilt site would be paid, which also meant no ads on the blogs. WP Tavern's read of the plan was blunt: "the service will just be a branded WordPress Multisite install."

The old site was taken down on August 31, 2013 and replaced by a homepage announcing Xanga 2.0. Before the switch the team said it had backed up roughly 2 million blogs and about 25 terabytes of photos. A blogging membership was priced at $48 a year, which asked for loyalty in a year when WordPress.com, Tumblr and Medium were all free. Classic users ran a Change.org petition objecting to paying it just to reach their own archives.

Is Xanga still in business in 2026?

Yes, in the narrow sense that xanga.com still resolves and Xanga 2.0 is still the last version the company shipped. Xanga has been closed to new members since the original site came down in 2013, and no sale or change of owner has been announced since. The $48 figure is a 2013 price, and Xanga publishes no current one.

What Xanga is not is a working community. The blogrings, the subscription feed and the eProps culture that made the place worth visiting are gone. In that respect it has aged worse than LiveJournal, which still takes new accounts.

Most of what users wrote between 1999 and 2013 is not on the public web. The Archive Team wiki lists Xanga as only partially saved: a 25 terabyte copy was held privately rather than deposited with the Internet Archive, which has no more than an earlier test crawl. Some people downloaded their own archives during the 2013 migration. Most never did.

A separate project at xanga2.com advertises a rebuilt version of the 2003 experience, with blog rings, mood icons and music players, and is collecting signups on a waitlist. Its own About page calls the site a "fresh start" built from scratch and says old posts cannot be recovered. It has not launched, and it is not the original service coming back.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Xanga still online?

Xanga is still online in 2026 as Xanga 2.0, the WordPress-based version that replaced the original site in 2013. Old members can sign in at xanga.com, but Xanga has been closed to new members since the original platform was taken down on August 31, 2013, and it has no active community left.

When did Xanga shut down?

The original Xanga, the free platform that ran from 1999, was taken offline on August 31, 2013, after a fundraiser that asked users for about $60,000 by July 15, 2013 and raised $58,640. A paid service called Xanga 2.0 replaced it, so Xanga never formally closed as a company.

Who owns Xanga now?

Xanga is still run by Xanga.com, Inc., the New York company founded in 1999 by Marc Ginsburg, John Hiler and Dan Huddle. The Federal Trade Commission named Hiler and Ginsburg as the site's principals in its 2006 COPPA case, and no sale of Xanga or change of owner has been announced since the paid Xanga 2.0 relaunch in 2013.
