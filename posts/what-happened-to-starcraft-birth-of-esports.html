Is StarCraft Still Played in 2026? Yes, and Free

2026-04-15 | Updated 2026-09-23 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

StarCraft is still played in 2026. The Brood War league ASL ran its 21st season from March to May 2026 on the Korean streaming service SOOP, and Blizzard Entertainment, owned by Microsoft since October 2023, revealed a new StarCraft game at BlizzCon 2026 for a 2030 release. The original StarCraft and its Brood War expansion are free on Battle.net, and StarCraft: Remastered costs $14.99.

Why Blizzard threw out the first StarCraft build

Blizzard Entertainment began StarCraft in 1995, straight after finishing Warcraft II: Tides of Darkness. The first plan was cheap and obvious: reskin the Warcraft II engine with a science fiction look and ship it.

The demo at E3 1996 was a failure. Press and fans labelled it "orcs in space" and "Warcraft goes purple", and before the show closed Blizzard had switched most of its StarCraft demo stations over to Diablo.

So Blizzard started again. The engine was rebuilt, and Terran, Zerg and Protoss were redesigned to play as three different games rather than one unit list in three colors. That asymmetry was unusual for real-time strategy in the 1990s, where factions were mostly cosmetic variations on each other. The reworked build shown in early 1997 got a very different reception.

StarCraft shipped on March 31, 1998. It sold more than 1.5 million copies in under a year and finished as the number one selling PC game of 1998. By February 2009 it had passed 11 million copies worldwide, and roughly 4.5 million of those were sold in South Korea alone.

What made South Korea the center of the game

The 1997 Asian financial crisis hit South Korea hard, and the government answered it partly by pouring money into broadband. By the early 2000s the country had some of the fastest and cheapest consumer internet in the world.

The other half of the story was the PC bang, the Korean internet gaming cafe. There were about 100 of them in 1998. By 2001 there were roughly 23,000, a growth curve that Korean economists nicknamed "Starcnomics" after the game that drove it.

StarCraft fit that room perfectly. It ran on modest hardware, Battle.net let anyone queue against anyone for free, matches lasted twenty minutes, and the state of a game was legible to people standing behind the chair. Watching became as normal as playing. The same thing was happening in the West at LAN parties, but at a fraction of the scale and with none of the money.

Korea bought roughly 4.5 million of the 11 million copies StarCraft sold. No other market came close, and no other market built an industry on top of the game.

How a computer game got its own television channels

In 2000 the Korea e-Sports Association, known as KeSPA, was founded with the approval of the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. A government ministry recognized competitive gaming as a sport in 2000, more than a decade before most of the world took the idea seriously.

Two cable channels carried the matches. OnGameNet ran the OnGameNet Starleague and MBC Game ran the MBC Starleague, both with studio audiences, commentators and replay analysis. Team competition followed: Proleague was founded in 2003 and ran for 14 seasons.

The peak was a beach. In 2005 the SKY Proleague grand final between SK Telecom T1 and KTF MagicNs was staged on Gwangalli Beach in Busan, and ESPN reported in 2017 that more than 100,000 people turned up for it. Admission was free, and fans slept on the sand overnight to get near the stage.

The face of that era was Lim Yo-hwan, the Terran player known as BoxeR. He founded Team Orion in 2002; SK Telecom began sponsoring it in April 2004, creating SK Telecom T1. His fan club passed 600,000 members, and he entered the Korean esports Hall of Fame in 2012.

Who owns StarCraft today?

Microsoft owns StarCraft. Blizzard Entertainment became part of Activision Blizzard, and Microsoft closed its $68.7 billion purchase of Activision Blizzard on October 13, 2023. Every StarCraft decision since then has been a Microsoft decision.

For a while there were very few of them. On October 15, 2020, Blizzard said it would stop making new paid content for StarCraft II. The post framed it as a clearing of the decks: "this change will free us up to think about what's next, not just with regard to StarCraft II, but for the StarCraft universe as a whole."

What came next took six years. At BlizzCon 2026 in September, Blizzard revealed a new StarCraft game, shown through a cinematic called Dominion and set decades after StarCraft II. It is not a strategy game. It is an open-world shooter, it is led by Dan Hay, who ran the Far Cry series at Ubisoft, and it is targeted at 2030.

Hay described it on stage as "an open-world shooter where every step is earned, not from above, but down on the ground, in the mud, in the fire." Blizzard has shown no gameplay.

What the Brood War scene looks like in 2026

Brood War never stopped. The ASL, hosted by the Korean streaming service SOOP, is the league that replaced the old cable circuit, and Season 21 ran from March 23 to May 24, 2026, with 28 players and a prize pool of 78 million Korean won.

The final was played on May 24, 2026, on a special stage at the PlayX4 convention at KINTEX in Ilsan, in front of a crowd of about 1,500. Park Sang-hyun, the Zerg player known as Soma, beat Lee Young-ho 4-3 to take a second consecutive title.

Lee Young-ho is the Terran player called Flash, long argued to be the best Brood War player who ever lived, and he had come back to the league after a six-year absence to reach that final. Park was careful about what the win meant. "I won today, but I don't think I've surpassed him," he told Inven Global in 2026.

English-speaking viewers get the same matches rebroadcast on SOOP's YouTube channel by Dan "Artosis" Stemkoski and Nicolas "Tasteless" Plott, the pair who have called Korean StarCraft in English for most of two decades.

Why StarCraft II never replaced Brood War

StarCraft II arrived in three parts: Wings of Liberty in 2010, Heart of the Swarm in 2013 and Legacy of the Void in 2015. It sold well and reviewed well, and it went free to play on November 14, 2017.

It still lost the room. KeSPA moved Proleague from Brood War to StarCraft II in 2012, and closed the league entirely in October 2016 after 14 seasons. By then League of Legends had taken the Korean audience, and real-time strategy had stopped being the default competitive PC game the way it was when Counter-Strike and StarCraft split the genre between them.

Brood War outlasted its own sequel because the older game kept the higher skill ceiling and the audience that grew up on it. Blizzard acknowledged that in 2017 with StarCraft: Remastered, which updated the art and audio and left the mechanics untouched.

Playing either one in 2026 is easy. The original StarCraft and Brood War have been free from Battle.net since patch 1.18 in 2017, and StarCraft: Remastered costs $14.99. Neither needs the tricks involved in running 90s PC games on Windows 11, because Blizzard has kept both patched.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you still play StarCraft for free in 2026?

Yes. Blizzard made the original 1998 StarCraft and its Brood War expansion free to download with patch 1.18 in 2017, and both still run through the Battle.net desktop app in 2026. StarCraft: Remastered, which adds high definition art and ranked matchmaking, is a separate $14.99 purchase.

Who owns StarCraft now?

Microsoft owns StarCraft. The game is developed by Blizzard Entertainment, which was part of Activision Blizzard when Microsoft completed its $68.7 billion acquisition on October 13, 2023. Blizzard still runs StarCraft and StarCraft II under Microsoft ownership.

Is a new StarCraft game coming out?

Yes. At BlizzCon 2026, Blizzard revealed a new StarCraft game targeted at 2030, its first new entry in the series since StarCraft II: Legacy of the Void in 2015. It is an open-world shooter set decades after StarCraft II rather than a real-time strategy game, and it is led by former Far Cry director Dan Hay.
