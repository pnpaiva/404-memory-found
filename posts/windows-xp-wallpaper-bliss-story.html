Is the Windows XP Bliss Hill Still There in 2026?

By Theo Lindqvist · Updated November 3, 2026 · First published March 22, 2026 · 6 min read

The Windows XP wallpaper, named Bliss by Microsoft, is a photograph of a real hillside that Charles O'Rear shot in January 1996 off Highway 121 in southern Sonoma County, California. The hill is still there on private land, and it has been planted with grapevines for most of the past twenty years. It went bare and green again for a few days in February 2026, when a passing driver photographed it.

Who took the Windows XP Bliss wallpaper photo?

Charles O'Rear shot the frame in January 1996. He was a former National Geographic photographer living in California wine country, and the picture was not an assignment.

O'Rear was driving the stretch where California State Route 12 meets Route 121 in southern Sonoma County when the hillside came up green under a broken sky. He pulled over, set up a tripod and shot it on a Mamiya RZ67, a medium format film camera, loaded with Fujifilm Velvia.

He has never claimed more credit than that. "Photographers like to become famous for pictures they created," he told the Napa Valley Register in 2010, before adding that he did not create this one.

Microsoft named the image Bliss. O'Rear's own title for it was Bucolic Green Hills. Reporters have called it the most viewed photograph in history, which nobody can verify, but it did ship as the default desktop on every copy of Windows XP from 2001 onward.

Why was the grass in Bliss so green?

Two accidents lined up. The slope had carried grapevines, and those vines were pulled out after a phylloxera infestation, the root louse that forced growers across Napa and Sonoma to replant through the late 1980s and 1990s. Bare ground plus a wet Northern California January produced a hillside covered in grass and nothing else.

The second accident was the film. Fujifilm Velvia is a transparency stock built to saturate greens and blues, and a medium format frame holds detail that 35mm film would have thrown away.

O'Rear has been consistent about what was and was not done to the picture. "It's the real deal. It was made here in wine country, nothing retouched, no Photoshop, that is how it looked," he told NPR in 2014.

That covers his own frame rather than the file on your old desktop. Amateur Photographer's account of the photo says Microsoft cropped the left side and pushed the green further for the version that shipped with Windows XP, so the wallpaper is a tighter, brighter edit of what O'Rear handed over. Nothing in the scene was staged, and nothing in it was rare either. It is a hill in a farming county on a good morning.

How much did Microsoft pay Charles O'Rear?

The number has never been published. O'Rear signed a confidentiality agreement with Microsoft and has kept to it, saying only that it was the most he ever received for a single photograph. PetaPixel, writing in 2017, put the figure above $100,000.

The contrast with the rest of the Windows XP set is the part photographers remember. Autumn, another XP default background, was licensed as a royalty free stock image, and the same PetaPixel report says its photographer, Peter Burian, took home $45.

Getting the picture to Microsoft was its own problem. O'Rear had to hand over the original transparency, the physical piece of film, and couriers turned the job down once they understood the slide was worth more than they wanted to insure. Microsoft sent him a plane ticket and he carried it on board himself.

The image had reached Microsoft through the stock trade. It sat with the library Westlight, which Corbis, the company owned by Bill Gates, bought in 1998. The Windows XP team found it there and Microsoft acquired full rights in 2000, the year before the operating system shipped.

Who owns the Bliss hill, and can you visit it?

Somebody who grows grapes on it. The slope sits on private property in the Los Carneros grape district of southern Sonoma County, visible from Highway 121 and from nothing else. There is no plaque, no pull-in and no sign telling drivers what they are passing.

The vines that phylloxera killed were replanted. By 2006 the hill carried vineyard rows again, and photographs taken as recently as 2025 show the same rows, either green in spring or burned off brown by late summer.

The artist duo Goldin+Senneby found the location in 2006 and re-photographed the view for a work called After Microsoft, exhibited from 2007. Their version is rows of grapevines under a flat grey sky. Set the two frames side by side and the point makes itself.

Then came February 2026. A driver passing on 18 February found the hill bare and green again, the rows gone and the winter rain doing what it had done thirty years earlier, and posted a phone snapshot to Reddit. Tom's Hardware called the alignment "super rare", which is fair. For most of the past twenty years the hill has been either under vines or browned off, and the window in which it resembles the wallpaper runs for a couple of weeks at most.

Is Windows XP still running in 2026?

Hardly anywhere. Microsoft ended support on 8 April 2014, the day NPR ran its own segment on Bliss. StatCounter's September 2026 figures put Windows XP at 0.05 percent of Windows web traffic worldwide, and its United States desktop tables for 2026 sit near half a percent, which is a rounding error as a share and still a real number of tills, lab benches and factory terminals.

The wallpaper has outlived the operating system by a wide margin. Bliss works as a meme template, a Minecraft build, a gallery wall text and a T-shirt, and the photograph's thirtieth birthday in January 2026 was covered as news by technology and photography outlets on both sides of the Atlantic.

Microsoft has never repeated the trick, and the reason is structural rather than artistic. Windows 11 rotates a fresh Bing photograph onto the lock screen every day, macOS ships a new wallpaper with each release, and every phone owner picks their own. No single default image gets a decade of undivided attention now. The last Microsoft artifacts that reached that kind of saturation were Brian Eno's Windows 95 startup sound, the screensavers that ran on every idle office monitor and Clippy, and all three belong to the same closed era.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Windows XP wallpaper a real photo?

Yes. The Windows XP wallpaper, named Bliss by Microsoft, is a film photograph taken by Charles O'Rear in January 1996 on a Mamiya RZ67 medium format camera loaded with Fujifilm Velvia. O'Rear told NPR in 2014 that nothing in his frame was retouched; the saturated green came from the film stock and from a hillside stripped of grapevines after a phylloxera infestation. Microsoft did crop the image and raise its saturation before shipping it in 2001.

Where is the Windows XP Bliss hill located?

The Bliss hill stands on private land in the Los Carneros grape district of southern Sonoma County, California, near where State Route 12 meets Route 121 between Napa and Sonoma. Charles O'Rear photographed it there in January 1996. The site is a working vineyard with no marker or viewing area, so there is nothing to visit beyond a roadside view of somebody else's grapes.

How much was Charles O'Rear paid for the Windows XP wallpaper?

Charles O'Rear has never disclosed what Microsoft paid him for Bliss, because he signed a confidentiality agreement when the company bought full rights in 2000. He has said it was the most he ever earned from one photograph, and PetaPixel reported in 2017 that the figure was above $100,000. By comparison, the same report says Peter Burian received $45 for Autumn, another Windows XP default wallpaper.
