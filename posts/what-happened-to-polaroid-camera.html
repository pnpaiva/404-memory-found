Is Polaroid Still in Business? The Brand in 2026

2026-04-07 | Updated 2026-09-21 | By Dana Reyes | 7 min read

Polaroid still exists in 2026, but not as the Massachusetts company that invented instant film. The original Polaroid Corporation filed for bankruptcy in 2001 and its successor filed again in December 2008; the brand and the film now belong to the Smolokowski family through Polaroid B.V., a Dutch company. Its plant in Enschede is the only factory on Earth still making Polaroid film, and a pack of eight shots costs about $19.

What did Edwin Land actually invent in 1948?

Polaroid Corporation was founded in 1937 by Edwin Land, a Harvard dropout who had made his name in light-polarizing filters. The instant camera came later, and it started with a question. On a 1943 family holiday in Santa Fe, Land's three-year-old daughter Jennifer asked why she could not see the photograph he had just taken of her.

Land had an answer five years later. On November 26, 1948, the Polaroid Land Camera Model 95 went on sale at the Jordan Marsh department store in Boston for $89.75. The store had 57 cameras in stock. All 57 sold on the first day, along with every pack of film that went with them.

What the Model 95 did was collapse a week into a minute. Before it, a roll sat in the camera until it was finished, went to a drugstore, and came back days later. If the exposure was wrong, or someone blinked, the photographer found out then. Land's system developed the negative and the print together inside a sealed sandwich of reagents, and 60 seconds later the picture was in the owner's hand.

Why was the SX-70 such a big deal in 1972?

Land spent two decades chasing the version he really wanted: no timing, no peeling, no chemical-coated negative to throw away. He showed it at Polaroid's annual meeting on April 25, 1972, where he called it "absolute one-step photography", as Fast Company recounted in 2022 on the camera's fiftieth anniversary.

The SX-70 folded flat enough for a coat pocket, opened into a single-lens reflex camera in chrome and leather, and pushed out a white square that developed in the open air while its owner watched. Each frame carried more than a dozen engineered layers that had to fire in sequence, at room temperature, without a darkroom anywhere in the process.

It also fixed the shape of everything Polaroid sold afterwards. The white square frame became the brand, more recognizable than the company's logo. Andy Warhol, Ansel Adams and Walker Evans all worked with Polaroid materials, which gave the format a standing in galleries that ordinary consumer film never had.

Why did Polaroid miss digital photography?

Not for lack of research. Polaroid ran a serious electronic imaging program through the 1980s, and by 1989 digital imaging accounted for close to 42% of the company's research and development spending, according to the study Mary Tripsas and Giovanni Gavetti published through Harvard Business School in 2000. Working sensors and prototypes existed years before Polaroid shipped a digital camera in 1996.

What the company lacked was a reason to want one. Polaroid sold cameras near cost and made its margin on film, which ran around 65% gross. Every digital picture was a picture that earned the company nothing, so senior management kept treating electronic imaging as a front end bolted onto a film business. The engineers lost that argument for a decade.

The warning had already come and gone. Polavision, Land's instant home movie system, launched in 1977 into a market Betamax and VHS had taken first, and Polaroid wrote down about $68 million on it in 1979. Land gave up the chief executive job in 1980, left the board in 1982, and died in 1991 holding 535 patents. Kodak made the same mistake on a larger scale, and for the same reason.

How did Polaroid go bankrupt twice?

Revenue peaked at about $3 billion in 1991 and slid through the decade as one-hour photo labs, then cheap digital cameras, took the casual snapshot market. On October 11, 2001, Polaroid Corporation filed for Chapter 11 protection carrying roughly $950 million in debt, one month after September 11 and with no buyer waiting.

The sale took ten months. In June 2002 a bankruptcy judge approved the transfer of the business to One Equity Partners, the private equity arm of Bank One, for $255 million in cash plus about $200 million in assumed trade liabilities, as CFO magazine reported. Executives who ran the company into bankruptcy collected bonuses. Around 6,000 retirees lost the lifetime benefits they had been promised.

The second collapse was uglier. Minnesota businessman Tom Petters bought the Polaroid name in 2005, and in December 2008 the companies he controlled, Polaroid among them, went into bankruptcy as federal investigators unwound his fraud. Petters was sentenced in 2010 to 50 years in prison for a Ponzi scheme prosecutors put at $3.65 billion. By then the Polaroid badge was turning up on televisions and DVD players, the same licensing afterlife Atari has lived through.

Who owns Polaroid today?

Polaroid is a Dutch company now. When the last film plant, in Enschede in the Netherlands, closed in 2008, two men who met at the closing event bought its machinery for about $3.1 million and leased part of the building: Austrian collector Florian Kaps and the plant's production manager, Andre Bosman. They named it the Impossible Project after a line of Land's that Bloomberg Businessweek quoted in 2016: "Don't undertake a project unless it's manifestly important and nearly impossible."

The machines came with no formulas. Polaroid's chemistry was proprietary and several key suppliers had shut down, so it took two years to make film that worked at all. The first packs shipped in 2010, muted and slow and unpredictable, and the people who had been hoarding expired film in their refrigerators bought them anyway.

The money came from the Smolokowski family, Polish investors who took a stake in 2012 and bought the Polaroid brand itself in May 2017. Film maker and trademark ended up in the same hands: the business trades as Polaroid B.V., the trademarks sit in PLR IP Holdings, and the name went from Polaroid Originals back to plain Polaroid in March 2020. The Enschede plant is still the only factory on Earth making Polaroid film, with close to 140 employees.

Is instant film worth buying in 2026?

Instant photography stopped being a niche some time ago. Fujifilm announced in April 2025 that its Instax line had passed 100 million cameras and printers sold since 1998, and Polaroid has held the expensive, heritage position beside it instead of competing on price.

The economics are the same ones Land set in 1948: the camera is cheap, the film is not. A Polaroid Now Generation 3, released in March 2025, sells for about $130, and the small Polaroid Go starts nearer $80. A pack of eight i-Type color shots runs about $19 in 2026, close to $2 per picture. That per-shot number, not the camera price, is what the decision actually rests on.

What $2 buys is one physical object that cannot be duplicated, deleted or scrolled past, which is the whole appeal to buyers who have never known a world without a camera in their pocket. Old prints are a separate problem with a cheaper answer: scanning photos and negatives costs a fraction of a dollar per image, well under the price of shooting new ones.

Where to find a vintage Polaroid camera today

Original SX-70 bodies still work when the electronics and the light seals do, and both fail with age. Professionally refurbished chrome SX-70s from specialist restorers start around $399 in 2026. Untested bodies from flea markets and estate sales cost far less and usually need a service, which is worth pricing before buying one. Condition notes and seller history matter more than the listing photograph here, which is why vintage Polaroid cameras on Etsy are an easier place to start than an unlabeled box at a market. Check which film the body takes before paying: 600 and i-Type packs are made today, while several older pack formats have not been produced for years, and budget about $2 a shot on top of whatever the camera costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Polaroid still in business in 2026?

The original Polaroid Corporation of Cambridge, Massachusetts filed for bankruptcy in October 2001, and the successor company that carried the name filed again in December 2008.
