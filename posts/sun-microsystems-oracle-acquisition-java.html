Is Sun Microsystems Still Around? Oracle Owns It

By Theo Lindqvist · Updated November 7, 2026 · First published October 23, 2026 · 7 min read

Sun Microsystems is not still around. Oracle bought the company for $9.50 a share, about $7.4 billion, and announced the completed acquisition on January 27, 2010, after which Sun Microsystems, Inc. became Oracle America, Inc. and the brand was retired. Java and Solaris are still Oracle products in 2026, with Solaris 11.4 supported to November 2037, but the SPARC processor line stopped with the M8 in 2017.

Where the Sun Microsystems name came from

Sun Microsystems was founded on February 24, 1982 in Santa Clara, California, by Scott McNealy, Vinod Khosla and Andy Bechtolsheim, with Bill Joy arriving from Berkeley soon after. The name was an acronym: SUN stood for Stanford University Network, the project behind the workstation prototype Bechtolsheim had built as a graduate student.

The product was a Unix workstation, and for most of the 1980s and 1990s that was a very good business. Engineers, banks, animation studios and telephone companies bought Sun machines because the alternatives were mainframes or PCs, and neither did the job.

Two pieces of Sun software outlived the hardware. Solaris, Sun's version of Unix, shipped on the workstations and later on the servers. Java, announced at SunWorld in San Francisco in May 1995 with Netscape on stage promising to put it inside Navigator, was built so the same program could run on any machine.

By 1999 Sun was selling the servers the web ran on, and its advertising said so: the company called itself the dot in dot-com.

How big did the company get before 2001?

Sun's own filings hold the peak. In the Form 10-K405 for the fiscal year ended June 30, 2001, the company reported revenues of $18.3 billion, approximately 43,700 employees and business in more than 170 countries. A later annual report puts fiscal 2001 net revenues at $18,250 million, the same figure rounded.

That growth came out of the dot-com build-out. Every startup with funding needed servers, and Sun sold expensive ones at high margins. The stock followed. TheStreet reported that Sun shares traded as high as $64.65 in September 2000 before losing most of their value.

The headcount mattered as much as the revenue. Sun hired into demand that was weeks from disappearing, and a company of 43,700 people built around its own processors had little room to shrink gracefully.

What the dot-com crash did to the numbers

The customers went first. Startups that had bought Sun servers on venture money stopped existing in 2001, from names as public as Pets.com down to firms nobody remembers, and their nearly new hardware went onto the second-hand market to compete with Sun's own sales force.

Then the losses started. Sun's June 2003 earnings release reported fiscal 2003 revenues of $11.434 billion, down 8.5 percent on the year, and a net loss of $2.378 billion, or 75 cents a share. Most of that was a non-cash impairment expense of $2.125 billion, and the fiscal 2003 annual report attributed roughly $1.56 billion of the write-down to goodwill from Sun's purchase of Cobalt Networks.

The deeper problem was architectural. Sun had bet on its own SPARC processors and its own operating system while the market moved to cheap x86 servers running Linux. By the time Sun filed its fiscal 2004 annual report, the stock that had touched $64.65 closed at $3.97 on September 2, 2004, held by about 23,400 stockholders of record. Shareholders approved a one-for-four reverse split in 2007.

Sun was not the only supplier to the build-out that never recovered. Nortel, which sold the network gear that sat beside Sun's servers, filed for bankruptcy in 2009 rather than find a buyer.

Why the IBM deal fell apart in April 2009

By 2009 Sun was looking for a buyer, and IBM was the obvious one. Talks became public in March. Bloomberg reported that Sun's board, led by co-founder Scott McNealy, broke off exclusive negotiations on April 4 after rejecting an offer of about $9.40 a share as too low. IBM never confirmed a price.

Price was not the only sticking point. Reporting at the time, later backed by Sun's own proxy filing, says Sun wanted a guarantee that IBM would complete the deal even if it drew federal antitrust litigation, and IBM would not give one. The proxy also records IBM coming back on April 19 prepared to pay only $9.10 a share, with no further flexibility.

Oracle stepped in the next day. On April 20, 2009 it offered $9.50 a share in cash, about $7.4 billion in total and $5.6 billion net of Sun's cash and debt, a 42 percent premium to Sun's closing price of $6.69 the previous Friday. It beat IBM's rejected number by a dime.

The European Commission then opened an in-depth investigation in September 2009 over what Oracle would do with MySQL, the open-source database Sun had bought in 2008. Brussels cleared the deal unconditionally on January 21, 2010, reasoning that rival PostgreSQL "is considered by many database users to be a credible alternative to MySQL".

Who owns Sun Microsystems now?

Oracle Corporation does, and it did not keep the name. Oracle's Form 8-K records the merger as effective January 26, 2010, with the completion announced on January 27. Sun Microsystems, Inc. was folded in and renamed Oracle America, Inc., which is how the entity still appears in Oracle's filings. There is no Sun Microsystems to be still around in 2026: no products under the name, no logo, no ticker.

The people left in stages. Jonathan Schwartz, chief executive when the deal closed, announced his resignation on February 4, 2010 in a Twitter post written as a haiku, which the New York Times called the first time a Fortune 200 executive had quit that way.

The engineering left in 2017. Oracle filed a California layoff notice that September covering 1,008 permanent job losses across Santa Clara and San Diego, effective October 31, and reporters tied the cuts to the Solaris and SPARC teams. Simon Phipps, who had run Solaris development at Sun, called it a "classic silent EOL" in comments reported by The Register in 2017. Oracle's media agency told the same outlet: "We decline comment."

The campus went too. Facebook took over Sun's old Menlo Park site and kept Sun's monument sign, turned around, with its own logo on the front and the Sun logo still readable from behind.

What happened to Java and Solaris under Oracle

Larry Ellison said at the April 2009 announcement that Java was the "single most important software asset we have ever acquired" and that Solaris was "by far the best Unix technology available in the market", both quoted by Computerworld that year. Seventeen years on, only one of those has aged well.

Java is everywhere and is now a revenue line. Oracle sued Google over Android's use of Java interfaces and lost at the Supreme Court on April 5, 2021, when the justices held 6-2 that copying about 11,500 lines of Java SE interface code was fair use. Oracle changed the business model instead: since January 2023 it has licensed Java SE by headcount, counting every employee in an organization whether or not those people write Java.

Solaris is alive but closed in. Oracle is still shipping Solaris 11.4 updates in 2026, with the January 2026 critical patch update delivered as Support Repository Update 89, and its published Lifetime Support Policy runs Premier Support to November 2031 and Extended Support to November 2037. What ended was new development. There has been no Solaris 12, and the SPARC M8 of 2017 was the last processor Oracle designed for the line. Fujitsu, not Oracle, carried the hardware on: its published roadmap sells SPARC M12 servers to the end of August 2029 and supports them to the end of November 2034.

Where to find one today: Sun hardware still turns up second-hand, and the small things are the easiest to live with: Type 5 and Type 6 keyboards, badges, Solaris media kits, SunSoft manuals and Java promotional items from the late 1990s. Parts-only SPARCstations go cheap, complete working machines cost considerably more, and the heavier the machine the more the shipping hurts. For the memorabilia, a search for vintage Sun Microsystems items on Etsy is the practical starting point.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much did Oracle pay for Sun Microsystems?

Oracle agreed on April 20, 2009 to pay $9.50 a share in cash for Sun Microsystems, about $7.4 billion in total and $5.6 billion net of Sun's cash and debt. The merger took effect on January 26, 2010 and Oracle announced the completed acquisition on January 27, six days after the European Commission cleared it. IBM had been rejected at about $9.40 a share earlier that month.

Is Solaris still supported in 2026?

Yes. Oracle Solaris 11.4, the current release since 2018, was still receiving updates in 2026, and Oracle's published Lifetime Support Policy carries Premier Support to November 2031 and Extended Support to November 2037. What ended was new development: the Solaris and SPARC engineering teams were cut in Oracle's layoffs of September 2017, and there has been no Solaris 12.

Does anyone still use SPARC servers?

Yes, mostly where migrating away would cost more than staying. Oracle's SPARC M8 of 2017 was the last processor Oracle designed for the line, and Fujitsu kept building SPARC servers afterwards, with M12 sales running to the end of August 2029 and support to the end of November 2034. Anyone running Solaris on SPARC in 2026 is doing so on hardware designs close to a decade old, supported rather than developed.
