Why Google+ Failed and When Google Shut It Down

By Dana Reyes · Updated November 12, 2026 · First published March 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Google+ is gone. Google closed the consumer version on April 2, 2019 after two data leaks, and retired the last workplace version, Google Currents, on July 5, 2023. It failed because Google built it to solve Google's problem with Facebook rather than a problem its users had, and by 2018 Google's own engineering leadership reported that 90 percent of Google+ sessions lasted under five seconds.

What was Google+ supposed to do for Google?

Google+ opened on June 28, 2011 as an invitation-only field trial, and it was not really built for the people signing up. It was built for Google. Facebook was pulling attention and data into a space Google's crawlers could not reach, and Google's advertising business depended on people moving around the open web instead.

The project ran under the internal codename Emerald Sea, led by Vic Gundotra with Bradley Horowitz as product lead. Social was made the company's priority that year, and teams across search, Gmail, YouTube and Android were pushed to wire Google+ into whatever they were already building.

It was not the first attempt. Google Buzz, bolted onto Gmail on February 9, 2010, built public contact lists out of people's email, and the Electronic Privacy Information Center filed a Federal Trade Commission complaint about it on February 16, 2010, one week later. Google settled with the FTC in 2011 and apologized for the launch. Google Wave, announced in 2009, drew so little use that Google handed the code to the Apache Software Foundation. Google+ was the third try, and this time the whole company was told to care.

Why did Circles never catch on?

Circles was the feature every review led with in 2011. You dragged contacts into groups called family, work or anything else, then chose which group saw each post. Facebook had nothing that clean at the time, and the problem was real: most people do not want a manager reading what they tell their college friends.

The trouble was the setup cost. Circles asked for sorting work before the first post, then asked again every time someone new showed up. A social network has to be useful in the ten seconds between opening it and getting bored, and Circles spent those ten seconds on filing.

There was a deeper problem. Real social graphs overlap. One person can be a coworker, a neighbor and a friend at the same time, and a system that wants a single label per group does not describe that. Facebook later shipped its own list tools and watched them go mostly unused for the same reason. People want to post something and be done, not maintain a database of their relationships.

How empty was Google+ really?

Empty enough that outside measurement found it almost immediately. comScore put the average visitor at about three minutes a month on Google+ against nearly seven hours on Facebook, figures The Wall Street Journal reported in February 2012, seven months after launch.

Google eventually published a number of its own. Announcing the shutdown in October 2018, Google vice president of engineering Ben Smith wrote that "90 percent of Google+ user sessions are less than five seconds." Nine visits in ten ended before the page had a chance to be interesting.

The gap between accounts and activity is the whole story. Registrations were easy for Google to grow, because signing up for other Google products signed you up for Google+ as well. Attention was the part it could not manufacture. People opened the stream, found posts from technology journalists and Google employees, did not find their sister or their neighbors, and closed the tab.

Social networks run on a loop: your people are there, so you go, so you post, so your people keep going. Google+ never closed that loop. The collapse of MySpace a few years earlier showed how fast a network empties once the loop breaks, and the story of Friendster showed the same thing a decade before that. Google+ never got the loop started at all.

Why did forcing Google+ on YouTube backfire?

On November 6, 2013, Google made a Google+ account a requirement for commenting on YouTube. The stated goal was better comments. The effect was a revolt by the people who made YouTube worth watching.

A petition demanding the old comment system back opened on change.org the same day and closed with 239,774 supporters. The sharpest single protest came from Jawed Karim, who co-founded YouTube and uploaded the site's first video in 2005: he rewrote the description of that video, "Me at the zoo," to say he could not comment any more because he did not want a Google+ account. The Register reported the protest on November 8, 2013, and it spread through creators and commenters who had never asked for a social network.

Google backed off in stages. The real-name rule, which had pushed away people posting under pseudonyms, was dropped in July 2014. The YouTube requirement went in July 2015, along with the Google+ profile requirement on other Google products. By then the damage was structural. Google had spent four years teaching hundreds of millions of people that Google+ was something done to them, and that is a hard lesson to unteach.

Is Google+ still around in 2026?

No. Google+ shut down for consumers on April 2, 2019, and nothing has replaced it under that name. The last piece of it, the workplace version called Google Currents, was retired on July 5, 2023, with administrators given until August 8, 2023 to pull their data out through Google Takeout. There is no Google+ account to sign into in 2026. Google still owns the name and has not used it since.

Two security failures set the ending date. Google found a bug in a Google+ API in March 2018 that had exposed profile fields for up to 500,000 accounts, and said nothing publicly at the time. When the story reached the press in October 2018, Google announced the bug and the shutdown together, citing "low usage and engagement" and a service that "has not achieved broad consumer or developer adoption." On December 10, 2018 a second bug surfaced, exposing data from about 52.5 million accounts over six days in November. NPR reported that Google then moved the shutdown up four months, to April 2019.

In 2020 Google agreed to pay $7.5 million to settle the class action over the leaks, covering anyone who held a Google+ profile between January 1, 2015 and April 2, 2019 and had data exposed. Claims closed that October, with each claimant due $5 and no more than $12. Google denied wrongdoing and said it found no evidence of misuse.

What survived Google+?

More than the name suggests. In March 2015 Sundar Pichai said Google+ would be broken into Photos and Streams, with Hangouts continuing on its own. Google Photos launched at Google I/O on May 28, 2015 and outlived the network it was born in, and Google's messaging work carried on into Google Meet and Google Chat.

Google Currents ran until July 5, 2023, when Google folded its function into Spaces in Google Chat. That is where the Google+ idea actually landed: not a public square built to fight Facebook, but group conversations inside a company's own account.

The public posts were not all lost. Volunteers from Archive Team, who work alongside the Internet Archive without being part of it, copied public Google+ posts and profiles before the shutdown, capped at 500 comments per post. Private and deleted posts were not saved.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who watched Google Reader disappear in 2013. Google ships, Google measures, and Google closes what does not clear the bar. Forbes ran its 2011 estimate under the headline "Google+ Cost $585 Million To Build (Or What Rupert Paid For MySpace)," counting about $125 million in staff time and $460 million in acquisitions: On2 at $123 million, Widevine at $158 million and Slide at $179 million. Google+ is the most expensive item on that list, not an unusual one.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did Google+ shut down?

Google+ shut down for consumers on April 2, 2019, almost eight years after it launched on June 28, 2011. The workplace version, renamed Google Currents, kept running until July 5, 2023, which is when Google+ ended for everyone. Google has not used the Google+ name on a product since.

Why did Google shut down Google+?

Google announced the end of Google+ in October 2018 and gave two reasons: almost nobody used it, and a bug in a Google+ API had exposed profile data for up to 500,000 accounts. Google's own announcement cited "low usage and engagement" and said 90 percent of sessions lasted less than five seconds. A second bug disclosed on December 10, 2018, affecting about 52.5 million accounts, moved the shutdown up from August 2019 to April 2019.

Did Google+ users get money from the data breach settlement?

Some did. Google agreed in 2020 to pay $7.5 million to settle the class action over the Google+ data leaks, covering people who held a Google+ profile between January 1, 2015 and April 2, 2019 and had data exposed. Claims closed on October 8, 2020, and each approved claimant was due $5, rising to no more than $12 depending on how many people filed. Google denied wrongdoing throughout.
