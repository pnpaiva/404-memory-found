Why the T-Mobile Sidekick Died and What It's Worth

2026-10-02 | By Theo Lindqvist | 6 min read

The T-Mobile Sidekick was Danger, Inc.'s Hiptop, sold in the United States from October 2002 at $199 after rebate. T-Mobile stopped selling it on July 2, 2010, and the Danger cloud service that made it work was shut down on May 31, 2011. No Sidekick has been sold since the Samsung-built Sidekick 4G of 2011, and surviving handsets work only as collectibles.

Quick facts
Launched
October 1, 2002, as the Danger Hiptop, sold by T-Mobile
Launch price
$199 after a $50 rebate, plus $39.99 a month for unlimited data
Units sold
About 1 million by 2009; around 250,000 US users in late 2005
What replaced it
Microsoft's Kin in 2010, then the Samsung Sidekick 4G in 2011
Status today
Discontinued. Sales ended July 2, 2010; data service ended May 31, 2011

What was the Danger Hiptop, and what did it cost in 2002?

Danger, Inc. was founded on December 9, 1999 by Andy Rubin, Joe Britt and Matt Hershenson, three engineers who had worked at Apple, WebTV and Philips. Their product was the Hiptop, a hinged slab whose screen swiveled a quarter turn to reveal a full QWERTY keyboard.

The Hiptop reached stores on October 1, 2002. In the United States it was sold by T-Mobile under a different name, the T-Mobile Sidekick, and it cost $199 after a $50 mail-in rebate, with unlimited data on an introductory plan of $39.99 a month.

Flat-rate data was the whole pitch. For one monthly fee a Sidekick owner got email, a real web browser and instant messaging, including AOL Instant Messenger, on a keyboard built for typing rather than for dialing.

The design decision that defined the Sidekick, and later destroyed it, was where the data lived. Contacts, calendars, notes and photos were held on Danger's servers and mirrored to the handset, which made a lost or broken phone a minor problem. Danger also ran a software Catalog, a downloadable app store that predated Apple's by roughly six years.

Rubin did not stay to see any of it. He left Danger in 2003 and started a company called Android, which Google bought in 2005.

How many people actually carried a Sidekick?

The Sidekick was never a mass-market phone in the way the Motorola Razr V3 was. By late 2005, an estimated 250,000 people were using one on T-Mobile's network in the United States. By 2009, roughly 1 million Sidekicks had been sold across the whole run.

What the numbers miss is who was carrying them. The Sidekick landed with American teenagers and with the music industry at the exact moment those two groups were living on MySpace and in chat windows, and a phone with unlimited typing was worth more to them than a camera or a thin profile.

It also produced one of the first celebrity data leaks. In February 2005, the contents of Paris Hilton's Sidekick account, including her address book, her notes and her photos, were pulled out of T-Mobile's systems and posted online, and the phone numbers of a long list of musicians and actors spread from there.

The case ended in court the same year. A Massachusetts teenager who admitted breaking into T-Mobile's computers, along with other offenses, was sentenced in September 2005 to 11 months in a juvenile detention facility.

Who bought Danger Inc., and what did Microsoft do with it?

Microsoft announced on February 11, 2008 that it was buying Danger, and closed the deal that April. Neither company disclosed the price. The figure reported at the time, and repeated ever since, was around $500 million.

Danger's staff were folded into a group called Premium Mobile Experiences inside Microsoft's Entertainment and Devices Division, and set to work on a project codenamed Pink. Pink shipped in 2010 as the Kin, a pair of social phones that Microsoft withdrew the same year.

The purchase also pointed Danger's engineering in a new direction. Microsoft wanted the platform rebuilt on a Windows CE kernel instead of the Java-based system Danger already had running, and the original Sidekick line was wound down in favor of the Kin.

That left an awkward gap. For most of 2008 and 2009 the Sidekick was a product whose owner was busy replacing it, still serving around a million handsets from a data center it had inherited rather than designed.

What caused the 2009 Sidekick data loss?

In the first days of October 2009, Sidekick data service failed and stayed down for about two weeks. Microsoft had contracted Hitachi Data Systems to work on the storage area network holding Sidekick user data.

A backup was running when the work began. It was two days into a planned six-day job when the backup was stopped so the upgrade could go ahead, on assurances that a backup was not necessary and over the objections of Danger's own engineers. The main database and its backup then both failed.

On October 10, 2009, T-Mobile told Sidekick owners that contacts, calendar entries, to-do lists and photos no longer on their handsets "almost certainly has been lost as a result of a server failure at Microsoft/Danger." For a platform built on the premise that the server was the safe copy, that sentence was the end of the argument.

The data came back anyway. Microsoft restored from an offsite tape copy about a month old and roughly 800 GB in size, and on October 15, 2009 Roz Ho, the corporate vice president running Premium Mobile Experiences, said in a Microsoft statement that "We rebuilt the system component by component, recovering data along the way." Most customer data was recovered, though full restoration took more than two months.

T-Mobile gave affected customers a free month of data service and, where content was permanently lost, a $100 card. A class action followed and settled in 2011, with class members offered a $35 T-Mobile gift card, a $17.50 check, or up to 12 free downloads.

When did T-Mobile stop selling the Sidekick?

T-Mobile stopped selling the original Sidekick on July 2, 2010, eight days after Microsoft killed the Kin. Application development for the platform was halted on September 21, 2010, and the Danger Developer Zone and its archives closed on September 30, 2010.

The real ending came on May 31, 2011, when Danger's cloud services were switched off after almost nine years. After that date a Sidekick could still make calls, but email, messaging, the browser and the whole synced account were gone, because none of it had ever run on the phone.

T-Mobile did reuse the name. The Sidekick 4G, built by Samsung as the SGH-T839, went on sale on April 20, 2011 running Android 2.2 with a 5-row keyboard under a 3.5-inch touchscreen. It kept the hinge and the name and almost nothing else, and T-Mobile had it on the discontinued list inside a year.

Nothing has carried the Sidekick name since. The brand shows up in nostalgia clips and fan concept art rather than in a carrier lineup, and no new model has been announced as of October 2026. The answer to the question people type is that the Sidekick is discontinued twice over: the hardware in 2010 and 2012, and the service that made it a Sidekick in 2011.

Where to find one today

Original Sidekick and Sidekick II handsets still trade among collectors, usually as display pieces, since the service behind them is gone and the phones predate the US networks still in operation. Rough, untested units go cheap; a clean working one with its box sits in the low hundreds and climbs from there. Collector marketplaces such as Etsy's T-Mobile Sidekick listings are the practical place to look.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why was the T-Mobile Sidekick discontinued?

Microsoft bought Danger, Inc., the company behind the T-Mobile Sidekick, in 2008 and moved its engineers onto a replacement phone, the Kin. T-Mobile stopped selling the original Sidekick on July 2, 2010, and the Danger cloud service the phone depended on was shut down on May 31, 2011, which left the hardware unable to do most of what it was sold to do.

Can you still use a T-Mobile Sidekick in 2026?

Not as a phone. The T-Mobile Sidekick kept its owner's email, contacts, calendar and browser on Danger's servers, and those servers were turned off on May 31, 2011. A surviving Sidekick in 2026 powers on and works as a collectible, but there is no account to sync, and the 2G networks the original models used are no longer running in the United States.

What replaced the T-Mobile Sidekick?

Microsoft replaced the Sidekick platform with the Kin, which it launched and withdrew in 2010. T-Mobile then put the name on the Samsung-built Sidekick 4G, an Android 2.2 phone released on April 20, 2011 and dropped within a year. After that, the keyboard phone market the Sidekick had created went to Android handsets and the iPhone.
