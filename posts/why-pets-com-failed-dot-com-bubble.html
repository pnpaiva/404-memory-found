What Happened to Pets.com? The Sock Puppet and the Domain

By Dana Reyes · Updated November 28, 2026 · First published March 24, 2026 · 8 min read

Pets.com today is only a web address: PetSmart bought the domain and trademarks in December 2000, and pets.com redirects to petsmart.com in 2026. The company itself stopped taking orders on November 9, 2000, nine months after its Nasdaq IPO, and finished liquidating as IPET Holdings on January 18, 2001. Its sock puppet mascot was sold off separately and now survives in museum collections, in a 2002 car-loan campaign and as a collectible toy.

What is pets.com today, and when did the company shut?

Pets.com today is a redirect owned by PetSmart: type the address into a browser in 2026 and it lands on petsmart.com. The company that built the site stopped taking orders at 11 a.m. Pacific on November 9, 2000, and finished liquidating as IPET Holdings on January 18, 2001.

PetSmart bought the Pets.com domain, the trademarks and subsidiaries including Flying Fish Express in December 2000, weeks after the web store shut, as Computerworld reported at the time. The sock puppet, the customer list and the warehouse equipment were not part of that deal and were sold off separately.

The Computer History Museum sums up that run in one line on its display label: the company "went from IPO to liquidation in just 268 days." The reason it could not last longer starts with the product it sold.

Why did Pets.com lose money on every bag of dog food?

The merchandise was under water before any other cost. The IPO prospectus filed with the SEC in February 2000 reports net sales of $5,787,000 for 1999 against a net loss of $61,778,000, with gross margin negative through the year. The company was paying more for the products than its customers paid for them.

The category made that hard to fix. Dog food, cat litter and birdseed are heavy, cheap per pound, and predictable enough that shoppers already bought them on a schedule at a store two miles away. Pets.com had to warehouse the pallets, discount the goods to pull people online, then pay a carrier to haul 40-pound bags across the country.

Every extra order deepened the hole rather than closing it. The same physics broke Webvan and its grocery delivery network and Kozmo's one-hour courier model inside the same 18 months. Pets.com tried to outrun the math with advertising, and the biggest bet aired on the most expensive night of television in the country.

A sock puppet singing Chicago to an empty house for $1.2 million

Pets.com ran its first national commercial during Super Bowl XXXIV in January 2000, at a price reported ever since as $1.2 million. In the spot, the sock puppet dog sings an off-key version of Chicago's "If You Leave Me Now" because its owner has left the house, a pitch for never having to drive to the pet store again. The agency was TBWA/Chiat/Day, and the ad finished fifth in USA Today's Ad Meter that year.

It was one of 14 dot-com advertisers in that game, which paid an average of $2.2 million per spot, according to Wikipedia's tally of the so-called Dot-com Super Bowl. The company's own filings never broke out the cost of the single slot, so the $1.2 million figure traces to press coverage rather than to the books.

The quarterly numbers show the scale better than the one spot does. Pets.com reported net sales of $8.8 million for the second quarter of 2000 in its Form 10-Q, while it spent roughly $17 million on advertising in the same quarter, a MarketWatch figure that Tedium cited in its 2017 account of the collapse. Boo.com burned its funding on the same theory, that attention bought early would pay for itself later. The attention was real, and almost all of it belonged to the puppet.

Where is the Pets.com sock puppet now?

The sock puppet now lives in museum collections and old commercials rather than on a working brand. The Computer History Museum in Mountain View, California shows a boxed toy version in its exhibition Revolution, and The Henry Ford in Dearborn, Michigan holds a Fun-4-All Corp. hand puppet catalogued as object 2017.101.1, which is not on public exhibit.

The character was created by TBWA and performed by the comedian Michael Ian Black. The Henry Ford's record notes it appeared on talk shows and as a float in the Macy's parade, and calls Pets.com "one of the most public victims" of the dot-com bubble burst. Not everyone agreed on who thought of it: a 2000 Form 10-Q discloses that Pets.com sued Robert Smigel, the creator of Triumph the Insult Comic Dog, after he claimed the company "stole the idea for our Sock Puppet from him."

The puppet outlasted its owner. The rights sold out of the liquidation for $125,000 to Hakan and Associates and the car lender Bar None, Inc., through a venture called Sock Puppet LLC; sources split on whether the deal closed in 2001 or 2002. In July 2002 Bar None aired nine commercials with the dog and a new slogan, "Everyone deserves a second chance." For most fans today, the toy made during the campaign is the only version they can actually own.

The box is what separates the two toy rows. Fun-4-All made the hand puppets in 1999 and 2000, and a loose one with a worn collar is the most common thing on offer; an unopened box with the microphone and tags intact is what boxed sellers are pricing. Fading, missing microphones and stained fabric all push a puppet back toward the bottom of the loose range.

Where to find one today: boxed and loose puppets from the 1999 and 2000 run turn up regularly in Etsy listings for the Pets.com sock puppet; compare several before paying more than the ranges above. The $20,100 bid made in February 2000 looks stranger once the company's own stock price is in view.

How fast did the collapse happen after the IPO?

Pets.com sold 7,500,000 shares at $11.00 on February 11, 2000, raising $82,500,000 gross and leaving $76,725,000 for the company before expenses after a $5,775,000 underwriting discount. Amazon was the largest stockholder, projected at about 30 percent after the offering in the filings.

Nine months later the board gave up. It adopted a Plan of Complete Liquidation and Dissolution on November 4, 2000, announced the wind-down on November 7, and the stock, issued at $11.00, was trading around 19 cents that day. PetSmart had offered less than the company's net cash, and the board turned it down.

There had been an attempt to sell. Merrill Lynch contacted more than 50 domestic and international prospects over the summer of 2000, and fewer than eight were willing to meet in person, by the company's own account. The idea did not die with the company, though; it moved to a rival.

What replaced Pets.com in online pet retail?

Chewy did, and the numbers show how far the model has come. Chewy reported net sales of $12.60 billion for fiscal 2025 and 21.3 million active customers on March 25, 2026. "We exited 2025 from a position of real strength," chief executive Sumit Singh said in that announcement, citing record free cash flow of $562 million.

The ownership runs through the same company that holds the old domain. PetSmart bought Chewy in 2017 for $3.35 billion, then took it public in June 2019 at $22 a share, and Chewy now trades on the New York Stock Exchange on its own.

What changed is the plumbing rather than the idea. Cloud infrastructure replaced the server farms Pets.com had to build, parcel rates and regional warehouses made heavy goods shippable, and automatic reordering turned a one-time discount buyer into a subscription. Amazon survived the same crash by having the cash to wait for those things to arrive. Pets.com did not, and all it left behind was a redirect and a dog made from a sock.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happened to Pets.com?

Pets.com announced its shutdown on November 7, 2000, laid off 255 of its 320 employees, and stopped taking orders two days later, after losing money on its products and spending heavily on advertising. PetSmart bought the Pets.com domain and trademarks in December 2000, which is why pets.com still redirects to petsmart.com in 2026.

Where is the Pets.com sock puppet now?

The Pets.com sock puppet survives in museums: the Computer History Museum displays a boxed toy in its Revolution exhibition, and The Henry Ford holds a Fun-4-All hand puppet as object 2017.101.1, not on exhibit. The character's rights went to Hakan and Associates and Bar None for $125,000, and Bar None last used it in nine commercials in July 2002.

How much is a Pets.com sock puppet worth?

A loose Pets.com sock puppet toy was listed for roughly $10 to $50 in November 2026, and boxed examples for roughly $48 to $125, though those are asking prices rather than completed sales. The original mascot drew a $20,100 winning bid in a February 2000 charity auction on Amazon, a price no toy has come near since.
