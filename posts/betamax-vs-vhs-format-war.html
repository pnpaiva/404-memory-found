Betamax vs VHS: Why Sony Lost the First Format War

2026-09-10 | Updated 2026-09-27 | By Theo Lindqvist | 6 min read

Betamax lost to VHS because a VHS cassette recorded two hours to Betamax's one, the first VHS deck sold in America undercut Sony by $295, and JVC licensed its format to anyone who would build it. Sony's picture advantage was 250 horizontal lines against 240, and the two-hour Beta II mode traded most of that away. Betamax no longer exists in any form: Sony stopped building recorders in 2002 after more than 18 million sales, and the last blank Betamax cassettes shipped in March 2016.

What did a Betamax cost, and what did VHS cost?

Sony showed Betamax in Tokyo on May 10, 1975. The first machine Americans could buy was the LV-1901, a 19-inch Trinitron television with a Betamax recorder built into the cabinet, which reached stores in November 1975 at $2,495. The stand-alone deck followed in February 1976: the Sony SL-7200, $1,295, one hour of video on an L-500 cassette.

One hour covered a sitcom with room to spare. It did not cover a film, a baseball game or an evening out of the house. Sony had built the Betamax cassette small enough to slip into a coat pocket, and that choice fixed the running time before anyone knew what buyers would do with the machine.

RCA answered on August 23, 1977 and shipped that October. The SelectaVision VBT200 was built by Matsushita, ran VHS tapes and recorded four hours, and RCA sold it with a slogan that made the whole argument in five words: "Four hours, $1,000, SelectaVision."

A shopper in late 1977 was comparing a $1,295 Sony that taped one hour against a $1,000 RCA that taped four. The price gap was $295 and the time gap was four to one, both pointing the same direction.

How did JVC's open license beat Sony's closed one?

JVC put the first VHS recorder on sale in Japan on October 31, 1976, and the HR-3300 held two hours on one cassette. Sony answered in 1977 with the slower Beta II speed, which fit two hours onto the same L-500 tape.

The deeper difference was who was allowed to build the machines. Sony treated Betamax as a Sony product and licensed it narrowly. JVC treated VHS as a standard and licensed it to anyone willing to manufacture, starting with its parent company Matsushita, then RCA, Sharp, Zenith and Magnavox. The two formats were not really rival products. They were rival supply chains.

That showed up in the tape aisle. By 1984, 40 companies were making VHS cassettes against 12 making Betamax cassettes, which meant lower prices, more shelf space and more rental stock, because video shops stocked whatever most customers could play at home. Betamax was down to about 25 percent of VCR sales by 1981 and 7.5 percent by 1986. In 1988 Sony began selling VHS recorders under its own name, which settled the matter without anyone using the word surrender. Sony ran the closed-format play again with MiniDisc, and finally won one two decades later in the HD DVD fight by lining up studios and hardware partners first.

Was Betamax actually the sharper format?

Slightly, at first, and by less than the legend claims. At its original Beta I speed, Betamax resolved 250 horizontal lines against 240 for VHS. Ten lines, on a 1977 television, across a living room.

Sony then gave that edge away itself. The two-hour Beta II mode that answered VHS ran the tape at half speed and dropped the picture to roughly the same 240 lines, so the setting nearly every owner actually used was no longer the sharper one.

Sony did eventually build the machine the legend describes. ED Beta, introduced in 1988, reached up to 500 lines of resolution and outclassed everything else sold to consumers. It arrived the same year Sony started making VHS decks, aimed at a public that had stopped listening a decade earlier. Where the engineering did pay off was professional: Betacam, a broadcast derivative that used a similar cassette shell, became standard equipment in television news long after consumer Betamax was finished.

Did adult films really decide the format war?

This is the version that gets repeated, and it does not survive the timeline. Sony had rules about what it would attach its own name to, and those rules governed what Sony itself produced and distributed. They never stopped anyone recording whatever they liked onto a blank Betamax cassette, and adult titles were duplicated onto both formats, because distributors wanted revenue from both sets of owners.

The bigger problem with the story is that it is not needed. Betamax was already losing on the two numbers buyers compared in the shop, and losing badly on the number of companies making tapes and machines. Two hours beat one, $1,000 beat $1,295, and 40 factories beat 12. Nothing about the outcome needs a more colorful explanation than that.

Is Betamax still around in 2026?

No. Betamax is dead at both ends, hardware and media. Sony stopped manufacturing Betamax recorders in 2002, after selling more than 18 million of them worldwide, then kept the cassette line running for another 14 years for the owners who were still recording.

That ended too. In a Japanese-language statement in November 2015, Sony said it "will end the shipment of Betamax video cassettes and micro MV cassettes in March 2016", as CBC News reported at the time. The last blank tapes shipped that March, about 41 years after the Tokyo launch.

The peak is worth holding next to the ending. Sony shipped roughly 50 million Betamax cassettes in 1984, the same year it was outnumbered more than three to one by VHS tape manufacturers. Nothing succeeded Betamax in its own line: VHS took the household, DVD took VHS, and streaming took the shelf. A closet full of Betamax tapes in 2026 is content stored on a format whose last new machine was built 24 years ago.

What did Sony win in the Supreme Court in 1984?

Universal City Studios and Walt Disney Productions sued Sony in federal court in Los Angeles in 1976. Their argument was that a machine built to record television was a machine built to infringe copyright, and that Sony should answer for what its customers taped. They asked the court to stop the sale of the Betamax.

The case took eight years. On January 17, 1984 the Supreme Court ruled 5 to 4 for Sony in Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, 464 U.S. 417. Recording a broadcast at home to watch it later, which the Court called time-shifting, was fair use, and the majority opinion put the rule in one line: "The Betamax is, therefore, capable of substantial noninfringing uses."

That holding outlived the format by decades. It is the legal ground under the cassette deck, the CD burner, the DVR and most recording hardware sold since. Sony lost the format war and won the case that kept the entire home video business legal, including the VHS trade that had beaten it.

Where to find one today

Sony has not built a Betamax recorder since 2002 and has not shipped a blank cassette since March 2016, so every working deck in circulation is at least 24 years old, and forty-year-old rubber belts are the usual point of failure. For most households the tapes matter more than the hardware, and a mail-in transfer is the practical route: Legacybox accepts Betamax alongside VHS and Hi8, with published 2026 kit prices starting at $34.99 for two tapes and $159.99 for ten. Whichever route you take, read the tape before the machine that can read it becomes the scarce part.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did Betamax fail if the picture was better?

Betamax held a 250-line picture against 240 lines for VHS at its original speed, and Sony gave most of that back with the two-hour Beta II mode in 1977. Buyers in late 1977 were comparing a $1,295 one-hour Sony deck with a $1,000 four-hour RCA SelectaVision, and by 1984 there were 40 companies making VHS cassettes against 12 making Betamax.

When did Betamax stop being made?

Sony stopped manufacturing Betamax recorders in 2002 and kept producing blank Betamax cassettes for another 14 years. The last Beta tapes shipped in March 2016, about 41 years after Betamax launched in Tokyo on May 10, 1975.

Can you still play Betamax tapes in 2026?

Only on a secondhand Betamax deck, because Sony has not built one since 2002, or through a transfer service that still accepts the format. Anyone holding Betamax tapes in 2026 depends on hardware that is at least 24 years old, which is why the tapes are usually worth digitizing before working machines get scarcer.
