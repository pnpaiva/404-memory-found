Are Beanie Babies Worth Anything? 2026 Prices

2026-09-09 | Updated 2026-09-26 | By Dana Reyes | 5 min read

Ty Inc. is still in business, still privately owned by founder Ty Warner, and still selling Beanie Babies. Only first- and second-generation animals from 1993 and 1994, prototypes and employee editions reach hundreds or thousands of dollars.

How did a $5 plush toy become an investment?

Ty Warner founded Ty Inc. in 1986 and showed the first nine Beanie Babies at the New York Toy Fair on 8 February 1993: Legs the Frog, Squealer the Pig, Spot the Dog, Flash the Dolphin, Splash the Whale, Chocolate the Moose, Patti the Platypus, Brownie the Bear and Pinchers the Lobster. Each one was under-stuffed with plastic pellets so it slumped like a real animal, each carried a name, a birthday and a poem on a heart-shaped tag, and each sold for about $5.

Three decisions turned the toy into an asset. Warner kept Beanie Babies out of the big chains, including Toys R Us and Walmart, so they appeared only in small gift shops and card stores, in small quantities per order. He retired designs without warning, which meant any animal could vanish from the shelf overnight. And he changed tags, fabrics and colors between production runs, which handed collectors variants to hunt.

By 1996 the pattern was fixed: a retirement announcement, an empty shelf, and a secondary price with no relationship to the cost of fabric and pellets.

What made 1997 and 1998 the peak?

McDonald's poured on the first tank of fuel. In April 1997 it put miniature Teenie Beanie Babies in Happy Meals, ordered 100 million of them for a promotion scheduled to run from 11 April to 15 May, and ran out weeks ahead of schedule as adults bought meals they had no intention of eating. Later that year Ty Inc. released Princess, a purple bear honoring Diana, Princess of Wales, with proceeds going to her memorial fund. Princess was scarce enough at first to become the emblem of the whole craze.

The second accelerant was online auctions. From 1995 onward every collector in the United States could see a live price for every animal, so a retired Beanie Baby carried a quote the way a stock does, and magazines and price guides began publishing portfolios of which ones to hold. Ty Inc. sales reached $1.4 billion in 1998, and Warner, who owned the company outright, became a billionaire on a product that retailed for the price of a sandwich.

None of the resale money reached Ty Inc. What the company got instead was free advertising: every resale price printed in a newspaper sent another buyer into another gift shop.

Why did the Beanie Babies bubble burst?

Bubbles end when the new buyers run out, and by 1999 most households that wanted Beanie Babies already had bins of them. Ty Inc. had also never stopped producing. Retiring a design creates scarcity only if the factory slows down, and the factory did not; it moved on to new animals in growing numbers, so the total in circulation climbed while the pool of people willing to pay $50 for one shrank.

Then Ty Inc. ended the game itself. A notice went up on the company website in 1999, tacked onto a release about new animals: "On December 31, 1999 - 11:59 p.m. (CST) All Beanies will be retired." CBS News later called it the day Beanie Babies died. Collectors read it as a last chance and prices spiked. A few months later the company said demand had changed its mind, and production restarted in 2000 with a bear called The Beginning.

The retirement that was supposed to lock in value turned out to be a marketing event, and the market stopped believing. Secondary prices fell through 2000 and never recovered. It was the lesson the dot-com companies were about to learn: a price that depends on the next buyer paying more is not a value, it is a queue.

Is Ty Inc. still in business in 2026?

Yes. Ty Inc. is still trading, still privately held by Ty Warner, and still selling Beanie Babies alongside newer plush lines such as Beanie Boos. The company never filed for bankruptcy and never sold itself to a conglomerate, which is unusual for a 1990s craze brand; most of the toy fads of that decade ended with the maker absorbed or shut down.

Warner's own reputation took the damage instead. In 2013 he pleaded guilty to tax evasion over income hidden in Swiss bank accounts. The Justice Department said he failed to report more than $24.4 million in gross income and evaded roughly $5.6 million in federal tax between 1999 and 2007, and that he paid a civil penalty of more than $53 million on top of about $27 million in back taxes and interest.

In January 2014 Judge Charles Kocoras sentenced him to two years of probation, at least 500 hours of community service in Chicago high schools and a $100,000 fine, rather than the prison term prosecutors had asked for. "Mr. Warner's private acts of kindness, generosity and benevolence are overwhelming," Kocoras said at the hearing, as Chicago Magazine reported in 2014.

Which Beanie Babies do collectors still pay for?

Almost none of them. The honest figure for a 1990s animal pulled out of a closet in 2026 is $1 to $5, and less if the heart tag is bent or missing. Princess, the bear owners are most certain is worth a fortune, usually changes hands for $25 to $35. The listings asking $10,000 are asking prices, not sales, and the tag quirks sellers describe as rare turn up on millions of them because Ty Inc. ran that bear across several factories.

A short list does sell for real money: first- and second-generation animals from 1993 and 1994 with the early tags intact, documented prototypes, color variants that were pulled quickly, and bears made for Ty Inc. employees or single events. Condition is most of the price, and the tag is most of the condition.

Where to find one today: a common 1990s Beanie Baby costs a few dollars, and complete sets, shop display pieces and intact heart tags are the more interesting buys. The vintage Ty listings on Etsy are a sensible place to start. Treat any claim of rarity in a listing title as wrong until a completed sale proves otherwise.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Beanie Babies worth anything in 2026?

Most Beanie Babies are worth $1 to $5 in 2026, whatever their retirement status says. Only first- and second-generation animals from 1993 and 1994 in mint condition, documented prototypes and employee editions reach hundreds or thousands of dollars, and the Princess bear that Ty Inc. released in 1997 typically sells for $25 to $35.

Does Ty still make Beanie Babies?

Yes. Ty Inc. announced in 1999 that it would retire the whole line on 31 December 1999, restarted production in 2000, and still sells Beanie Babies in 2026 alongside newer lines such as Beanie Boos. The company remains privately owned by its founder, Ty Warner.

When did the Beanie Babies craze end?

The Beanie Babies craze peaked in 1998, when Ty Inc. sales reached $1.4 billion. It ended across 1999 and 2000, after Ty Inc. announced the retirement of the entire line on 31 December 1999 and then reversed the decision, which broke collector confidence and sent secondary prices down for good.
