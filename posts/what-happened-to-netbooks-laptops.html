Do Netbooks Still Exist in 2026? What Replaced Them

2026-04-03 | Updated 2026-09-10 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

Netbooks no longer exist as a product category. Asus and Acer, the two companies that started and dominated it, ended production in January 2013, a little over five years after the $299 Asus Eee PC 701 created the market in October 2007. The cheap web laptop slot now belongs to Chromebooks, which shipped 11 million units in the first half of 2025 and which Google began rebranding as Googlebook in May 2026.

What made the Asus Eee PC sell out in 2007?

The netbook started with one machine. Asus put the Eee PC 701 on sale in October 2007 with a 7-inch screen, a 900 MHz Intel Celeron M processor, 512 MB of memory and a 4 GB solid-state drive, at $299 in the United States. It weighed about two pounds and ran a customized build of Xandros Linux instead of Windows.

The pitch was subtraction. A mainstream laptop in 2007 cost several times as much, shipped with Windows Vista and carried hardware most buyers never pushed. Asus took nearly all of it out and sold what was left as an appliance for email, the web and a word processor. The three Es in the name stood for "Easy to learn, easy to work, easy to play," which is a fair description of the customer Asus was aiming at.

It worked immediately. The first production run sold out, and Asus shipped more than 300,000 Eee PCs in 2007 alone, in a category that did not yet have a name. Within 18 months Acer, Dell, HP, Lenovo, Samsung and MSI were all selling something similar, and the Acer Aspire One had become the biggest seller of the lot.

Why did Intel and Microsoft rush into the $300 laptop?

Both companies faced the same threat. A cheap laptop running Linux on a non-Intel chip cut both of them out, and the volume was real enough to matter.

Intel answered in April 2008 with the Atom. The N270, the chip in most netbooks, ran at 1.6 GHz on a single core and drew 2.5 watts. It was slow on purpose. Intel needed a part cheap enough to own the low end without giving anyone a reason to skip a Core processor in a full-price laptop.

Microsoft's answer was messier. Windows Vista could not run on netbook hardware, so the company kept Windows XP Home alive for what it called ultra-low-cost PCs, then set limits on what a machine could be and still qualify. Engadget reported in May 2008 that the terms capped screens at 10.2 inches, memory at 1 GB, storage at 80 GB and processors at a single core running 1 GHz or less, with no touchscreens. A manufacturer that wanted the cheap license could not build a better netbook, because the ceiling was written into the contract. Microsoft was blunt about the result once Windows 7 was ready: at the launch event in New York in October 2009, Steve Ballmer said netbooks "disappointed a lot of customers with graphics that were kind of slow and small screens."

How big did netbooks actually get?

Bigger than most people remember. DisplaySearch counted 16.4 million netbooks shipped in 2008 and 33.3 million in 2009, growth of 103 percent in a single year, with revenue climbing from $6.65 billion to $11.4 billion. At that point netbooks were close to a fifth of all portable computers shipped worldwide.

2010 was the peak, at 38.8 million units. Analysts were still forecasting growth, and every major PC maker had a netbook line in its catalog.

The economics underneath were the problem, and they mattered more than the unit counts. A $299 machine has to pay Intel for the processor, Microsoft for the license and a retailer for shelf space, and very little is left. Worse, netbook buyers were often the same people who would otherwise have bought a $700 laptop, so the industry was trading high-margin sales for low-margin ones and calling it growth. Compaq had already demonstrated what a PC price war does to the company that starts it.

Did the iPad kill the netbook?

It did most of the damage, and it did it in public. Steve Jobs introduced the iPad on January 27, 2010, and went after the category by name during the presentation. "The problem is, netbooks aren't better at anything," he said. Apple had already refused to build one: on the October 2008 earnings call, Jobs told analysts, "We don't know how to make a $500 computer that's not a piece of junk."

The iPad started at $499, more than most netbooks cost, and Apple said it sold more than 300,000 of them on the first day of US sales, April 3, 2010. For the three things that sold netbooks, email, browsing and video, the tablet was simply better, which is the same move Apple had pulled by arriving late and winning on the experience with the iPod.

The crossover came fast. In the second quarter of 2011, tablets outshipped netbooks 13.6 million to 7.3 million, and netbook shipments for the full year fell about 17.5 percent to roughly 32 million, according to figures reported by The Register at the time. Cheaper full-size laptops squeezed from the other side. A 10-inch screen and a single-core Atom had nothing left to defend.

Can you still buy a netbook in 2026?

Not a new one. The netbook is a discontinued category, not a shrinking one. Dell left first, and in January 2013 Asus and Acer, the company that started the category and the company that sold the most of them, both confirmed they had stopped production. Nothing has carried the netbook label since, and no manufacturer has tried to bring it back.

What survives is the shape. A $250 Chromebook in 2026 is roughly the same size, price and purpose, and cheap small Windows laptops exist in that range too. What does not exist is a machine sold as a netbook, with the Atom processor and the licensing cap that defined the word between 2007 and 2013.

Where to find one today. Working Eee PC 701s, Acer Aspire Ones and HP Minis still turn up from vintage electronics sellers, generally for well under the $299 the first Eee PC cost in 2007. Retro computing sellers on Etsy list them alongside other 2000s hardware. Check that the seller has tested the battery, which is the part that fails first, and expect to install a lightweight Linux distribution, because Windows XP has had no security updates since 2014.

What replaced netbooks after 2013?

Chromebooks took the slot and fixed the part that broke the netbook: the software bill. Google launched Chrome OS laptops in 2011, and because the operating system cost manufacturers nothing, the same $250 of hardware did not have to fund a Windows license. Schools bought them by the million.

They are still selling. Canalys counted 11 million Chromebooks shipped in the first half of 2025, up 10.6 percent year over year, with Lenovo at 31 percent of the market and education the largest buyer. That is the netbook's audience, more than a decade after the netbook died, buying the netbook's product under a different name.

The name is changing again. On May 12, 2026, Google announced Googlebook, an Android-based laptop line built around Gemini that takes over from the Chromebook brand, with Asus, Dell, HP and Lenovo signed up as partners. Google's vice president for ChromeOS confirmed that new Chromebook and Chromebook Plus models are still in the pipeline. The cheap laptop that does the basics keeps getting renamed, but the market Asus found in 2007 has never gone away.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did netbooks stop being made?

Netbooks stopped being made at the start of 2013. Asus and Acer, which between them started and dominated the category, both confirmed in January 2013 that they had ended production, and Dell had already left. The netbook category therefore ran from October 2007 to January 2013.

What was the first netbook?

The Asus Eee PC 701, released in October 2007 at $299, is generally counted as the first netbook. It had a 7-inch screen, a 900 MHz Intel Celeron M processor, 512 MB of memory and a 4 GB solid-state drive, and it ran Xandros Linux rather than Windows. Asus shipped more than 300,000 of them in 2007.

Are Chromebooks the same thing as netbooks?

Chromebooks are the netbook idea with better economics. Google launched Chrome OS laptops in 2011, two years before the netbook category ended in January 2013, at similar sizes and prices but with no Windows license to pay for. Canalys counted 11 million Chromebook shipments in the first half of 2025, and Google began replacing the Chromebook brand with Googlebook in May 2026.
