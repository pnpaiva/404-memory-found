Who Owns Packard Bell Now? Acer Relaunched It in 2026

2026-09-18 | Updated 2026-10-19 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

Packard Bell is owned by Acer, which picked up the brand through its purchase of Gateway and then left it dormant for about 13 years. Acer relaunched Packard Bell at IFA in Berlin in September 2026 as a budget lifestyle brand led by the 14.5-inch DotBook laptop at 499 euros. The collection is sold in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and Acer has announced nothing for the United States.

How a dead radio name became America's best-selling PC

Packard Bell started out as a Los Angeles radio maker, and by the 1980s the name was a trademark nobody was using. A group led by Beny Alagem bought it in 1985, and in 1986 Alagem and two largely passive investors, Jason Barzilay and Alex Sandel, formed Packard Bell Electronics, Inc.

The company chased a customer the rest of the industry ignored: the person buying a first computer from a store that had never sold one. That meant Sears, Walmart, Best Buy and Circuit City rather than computer dealers, and machines that shipped preloaded and cabled so a buyer who had never heard of a sound card could switch one on.

It worked quickly. Worldwide sales rose about 120 percent in 1994 to an estimated $3 billion, and from October 1994 through March 1995 Packard Bell sold more PCs in the United States than anyone else. Fortune measured the grip in June 1995: "Over half the shelf space allotted to PCs in stores such as Sears goes to Packard Bell."

Why Packard Bell computers earned such a bad reputation

The machines were cheap, and plenty of buyers felt they were built like it. Packard Bell shipped its own front end, Packard Bell Navigator, aimed at the same nervous first-time owner that Microsoft Bob was chasing, and it drew the same complaint about standing between people and Windows.

The lasting damage came from the parts. Compaq accused Packard Bell in 1995 of taking components out of returned computers and recycling them into machines sold as new, an allegation Fortune reported that June while the company was still the best-selling PC brand in the country.

Federal investigators reached the same subject. In October 1998 Packard Bell agreed to pay the United States government $3.5 million to resolve allegations that it had put used equipment into computers sold as new, in a case that turned on sales to the military and to government resellers under contracts requiring new hardware. The company did not admit wrongdoing. CNN reported at the time that 22 state attorneys general had opened their own investigations into the recycling practice.

That is the part worth holding on to. A brand built entirely on price has nothing left to trade on once shoppers stop trusting what is in the box.

What NEC bought when it merged its PC business in

NEC had been buying into Packard Bell for years before it took control. In 1996 Packard Bell also bought Zenith Data Systems from Groupe Bull of France, which handed it a business customer base and a European footprint it had never had, and that year the two sides combined their PC operations as Packard Bell NEC.

The combination briefly put the company back on top of US unit shipments, and it did not hold. Compaq pushed past it in retail, the discount maker eMachines attacked from underneath, and Packard Bell could not charge a premium because nobody believed it had earned one.

NEC kept feeding the business anyway. By the time it stopped, Forbes put the total at more than $2 billion sunk in since 1995. Very little of that reached the problem, because the problem was a thin-margin retail model carrying a name that had become a punchline in the same stores that made it.

When did Packard Bell stop selling computers in the United States?

On November 3, 1999, NEC announced it would shut the US operations. Forbes wrote the same day that NEC had poured more than $2 billion into Packard Bell NEC "only to have it continue to bleed red ink like a stuffed pig."

By 2000 the name was off American shelves. The retail space it had effectively invented for home computers did not disappear with it: that went to Compaq, Hewlett-Packard and Gateway, and later to cheaper laptops from Acer and Dell.

The European business ran on. NEC sold it in 2006 to Lap Shun "John" Hui, a co-founder of eMachines, and that sale carried a condition that decided who owns the brand today. Gateway held a right of first refusal over Hui's interest in Packard Bell under a June 2006 agreement. When Acer agreed in August 2007 to buy Gateway for about $710 million, Gateway said it intended to exercise that right, and Acer agreed to fund the purchase. Packard Bell has been an Acer brand ever since.

Is Packard Bell still around in 2026?

Yes, if you live in Europe, the Middle East or Africa. Acer kept Packard Bell selling in Europe for a few years after the takeover, then let it go quiet for roughly 13 years: the website stayed up and the products stopped, which is the usual fate of a name that outlives the company that earned it.

At IFA in Berlin in September 2026, Acer relaunched the brand as a budget lifestyle range under the line "Tech it easy," timed to what it calls Packard Bell's 100th anniversary. That anniversary takes some liberties, since Acer dates the brand to Los Angeles in 1926 while the Packard Bell partnership itself came later.

The relaunch covers nine products. The lead item is the DotBook, a 14.5-inch Windows 11 laptop starting at 499 euros, or roughly $580 at 2026 exchange rates. Around it sit the DotLoop turntable at 149 euros, the DotLook audio glasses at 99 euros, the DotLine 4G feature phone at 69 euros, the DotShine ring light at 39 euros and the DotTune speaker at 34.99 euros. Valerie Piau, EMEA vice president at Acer Europe, pitched it in 2026 as everyday technology kept simple and offered "all at a fair price."

Dates inside Acer's own announcement do not agree. The release describes the collection reaching European retail from the fourth quarter of 2026, while its availability list puts the DotBook on sale in September 2026 and the DotLine in November. Acer has announced nothing for the United States.

Can you still buy a 1990s Packard Bell?

The beige machines are a separate market from anything Acer sells. Complete towers, keyboards, mice, restore discs and the occasional boxed system turn up through Etsy's vintage Packard Bell listings, mostly out of house clearances.

Condition and completeness drive the asking price far more than the model does, so compare several listings before committing. Assume the hard drive is dead, assume the clock battery is flat, and treat the original restore discs as the thing worth paying for, because without them a working machine is a lot harder to put back to how it shipped.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Packard Bell still in business in 2026?

Packard Bell exists in 2026 as a brand owned by Acer, not as an independent company. Acer relaunched it at IFA in September 2026 with nine budget products led by the DotBook laptop, sold across Europe, the Middle East and Africa. The company founded as Packard Bell Electronics, Inc. in 1986 stopped selling computers in the United States in 1999.

Why did Packard Bell fail in the United States?

Packard Bell sold on price through general retail, and that model broke once Compaq pressed from above and eMachines from below in the mid 1990s. Trust was the other half of it: Compaq accused the company in 1995 of recycling parts from returned machines into computers sold as new, and in October 1998 Packard Bell paid the United States government $3.5 million to settle similar allegations without admitting wrongdoing. NEC shut the US operations on November 3, 1999.

Can you buy a Packard Bell laptop in the United States?

No. The Packard Bell range Acer announced in September 2026, including the 499 euro DotBook, is sold only in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and Acer has not announced a United States release. Anyone in the US who wants the name has to buy a 1990s machine second hand, because no new Packard Bell computer has been sold there since 2000.
