Who Owns EarthLink Now? Trive Capital Bought It

2026-10-05 | By Dana Reyes | 5 min read

EarthLink is still in business in 2026, but it is private: Trive Capital bought the consumer internet business from Windstream Holdings for $330 million in cash at the end of 2018 and runs it from Atlanta. The company that charged $19.95 a month for unlimited dial-up in the 1990s now resells fiber, fixed wireless and satellite service under its own brand.

How EarthLink sold unlimited dial-up for $19.95 a month

EarthLink Network started in 1994 in Los Angeles, built by Sky Dayton around one idea: charge a flat monthly fee for internet access instead of billing by the hour.

The price was $19.95 a month for unlimited dial-up, set against rivals that still ran a meter and sent bills that punished anyone who stayed online after dinner.

It worked quickly. By 1995 EarthLink sold dial-up service in 98 cities, and in January 1997 the company went public on Nasdaq.

What EarthLink actually sold was a modem connection, a phone number and a stack of setup software. The advantage was not technology. It was a predictable price and a support line that answered, which mattered a great deal in a decade when getting online meant typing settings into a dialog box by hand.

Flat-rate pricing then spread across the whole industry, which removed the thing that had made EarthLink different. From 1997 onward the company competed on reach and service rather than on the novelty of unlimited access, and it signed up subscribers about as fast as it could install phone lines to serve them.

Why Apple put $200 million into EarthLink in 2000

On January 5, 2000, at Macworld Expo in San Francisco, Apple announced a multi-year partnership with EarthLink and a $200 million investment in the company.

The terms were unusually concrete. EarthLink became the exclusive provider inside the Internet Setup software shipped with every new Macintosh sold in the United States, Apple earned a share of every Mac owner who subscribed, and Apple took a seat on EarthLink's board.

"EarthLink and Apple share a passion for providing the highest-quality Internet access to Macintosh users, and together we're going to do it better than anyone else," Steve Jobs said in Apple's announcement that day.

Weeks after that, EarthLink merged with MindSpring of Atlanta in a deal completed in February 2000, and the combined company became the second-largest internet service provider in the United States, behind AOL.

That was the peak of EarthLink's leverage. It was the default ISP on new Macs, it had $200 million of Apple's money, and it was second only to the biggest brand on the consumer internet. Everything that happened after 2000 was a response to losing one of those three advantages at a time.

What broadband did to EarthLink's subscriber base

The subscriber count peaked in the middle of the decade. EarthLink closed the fourth quarter of 2004 with 5.4 million total subscribers, the largest base it ever reported.

The mix inside that number was the problem. In that same quarter EarthLink added 84,000 broadband subscribers and 118,000 subscribers to its cut-price PeoplePC service, while its premium dial-up base fell by 211,000.

Every one of those trades moved money in the wrong direction. A premium dial-up customer paid the most and cost the least to serve. A broadband customer paid more but arrived with a wholesale bill attached, because EarthLink rented the line from the cable or telephone company that owned it.

That squeeze is what finished off the independent broadband sellers of the period, Excite@Home among them. Owning the customer is worth much less than owning the wire.

EarthLink kept looking for a second act and kept not finding one. It moved about 30,000 wireless subscribers into a joint venture with SK Telecom in 2005, and it built municipal Wi-Fi networks for Philadelphia and Anaheim before walking away from that business and handing the equipment back.

How Windstream bought EarthLink and sold it a year later

By 2017 EarthLink was something to be bought rather than a buyer. Windstream Holdings completed its merger with EarthLink Holdings Corp on February 27, 2017, issuing about 88 million Windstream shares and assuming roughly $435 million of EarthLink long-term debt, in a transaction Windstream valued at about $1.1 billion.

The fit lasted under two years. On the last trading day of 2018, Windstream agreed to sell the EarthLink consumer internet business to Trive Capital, a Dallas private equity firm, for $330 million in cash.

What Trive Capital bought was more than a customer list. The package included the Midtown Atlanta office building that became EarthLink's headquarters again, and a brand name that millions of Americans had once paid every month without thinking about it.

"Our goal is to provide the right technology at the right price, with a world class consumer experience," chief executive Glenn Goad told Telecompetitor in 2019, setting out a relaunch built on internet access plus privacy and security products.

Dial-up barely figured in that plan, although it has never quite died: a short list of providers still sells 56k access in 2026.

What EarthLink sells in 2026 and who runs it

EarthLink is a private company, owned by Trive Capital and run from Atlanta, and it is still selling internet service in 2026. It publishes no subscriber count, because a private company does not have to.

The model changed more than the logo did. EarthLink sells fiber, fixed wireless and satellite plans as a retail layer on top of networks that other carriers own, wrapped in its own billing, support and security extras, rather than digging trenches of its own.

Its business division has been buying regional fixed wireless and fiber operators outright, which is how a brand that went asset-light in 2019 ended up holding network assets again.

Old earthlink.net email addresses still work through the company's webmail, and that is the quietest reason the brand outlasted its own product. An email address printed on 20 years of forms is much harder to abandon than an internet provider.

So EarthLink in 2026 is neither a revival nor a shell. It is the same name, 32 years after the first $19.95 bill, selling internet access in a market where most households take whatever the cable company already ran down the street.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is EarthLink still in business in 2026?

Yes. EarthLink still sells internet service in 2026 as a privately held company owned by Trive Capital, which bought the consumer business from Windstream Holdings at the end of 2018, and it operates out of Atlanta rather than the Los Angeles area where Sky Dayton founded it in 1994.

Who owns EarthLink now?

EarthLink is owned by Trive Capital, a Dallas private equity firm that paid Windstream Holdings $330 million in cash for the EarthLink consumer internet business in a deal struck on the last trading day of 2018. Windstream had itself acquired EarthLink Holdings Corp less than two years earlier, on February 27, 2017.

Do earthlink.net email addresses still work?

Yes. EarthLink still runs webmail for earthlink.net addresses in 2026, decades after the dial-up accounts that created most of them. That is why the domain still shows up in inboxes long after EarthLink stopped being the internet provider for the person sending the mail.
