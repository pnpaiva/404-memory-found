Is Winamp Still Around in 2026? Yes, and Still Free

By Theo Lindqvist · Updated November 10, 2026 · First published March 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Winamp still exists. The free desktop player is owned by Winamp Group, a Belgian company led by Alexandre Saboundjian, and version 5.9.2 from April 2023 still installs and runs on Windows 10 and Windows 11. The company sells artist services under the Winamp name and has a Deezer-powered streaming player announced for the first half of 2027.

Who owns Winamp today?

Winamp belongs to Winamp Group, a Belgian company run by chief executive Alexandre Saboundjian. The same business bought the player from AOL in January 2014 under the name Radionomy, traded as Llama Group through the 2024 source code release, and took the Winamp name itself in 2025.

That makes three owners across the player's life. Nullsoft, the two-person company that wrote it. AOL, which paid about $80 million for Nullsoft in 1999. And the Belgian buyer that has held it since 2014.

TechCrunch reported in January 2014 that AOL sold Winamp and the Shoutcast streaming platform for $5 million to $10 million in cash and shares, and took a 12 percent stake in the buyer as part of the deal. AOL had paid roughly eight times that for Nullsoft fifteen years earlier.

"We want to rebuild the story for Winamp," Saboundjian told TechCrunch in 2014, and the plan he has followed since is to make the name a business rather than keep shipping a desktop player. Winamp for Creators sells musicians a website builder, merchandising and a fan subscription tool, and a paid listening service is meant to follow it.

What does the free Winamp player do in 2026?

The legacy desktop player is still free, still offered at winamp.com, and still the version almost everyone searching for Winamp actually wants. Version 5.9.2, Build 10042, shipped on April 26, 2023 and remains the newest desktop release in November 2026, which puts it more than three years without an update.

It installs and runs on Windows 10 and Windows 11. It plays local files, MP3 and FLAC included, along with Shoutcast internet radio. Milkdrop visualizations work, and so does the Winamp 2 skin format, which is why skin archives from the late 1990s have outlived several of the companies involved.

What changed is that the free player is no longer the product. The company sells subscriptions to musicians under the Winamp for Creators name, and the desktop build sits alongside that business as a legacy download rather than something in active development.

Work on the classic player happens outside the company instead. WACUP, the Winamp Community Update Project, is a separate modernization by an independent developer that patches and replaces parts of the 5.x player, and it ships new builds far more often than Winamp itself.

How a teenager's side project became the default MP3 player

Justin Frankel was 18 and had left college in Arizona when he wrapped a Windows interface around an MP3 decoding engine and released the result in April 1997. The name was literal: Windows plus AMP. He built it with Dmitry Boldyrev under a two-person company called Nullsoft.

Winamp 2.0, released on September 8, 1998, is the version people remember. It added a plug-in system, a usable playlist window and skins, and the skin format turned a media player into something people decorated. Within months there were dozens of plug-ins and hundreds of skins, almost none of them made by Nullsoft.

The llama came from the same period. A demo MP3 bundled with version 1.91 carried a line inspired by the Chicago outsider musician Wesley Willis: "Winamp, it really whips the llama's ass." It became the brand's mascot by accident and stayed.

The timing did the rest. MP3 files were spreading through IRC channels, FTP servers and, from June 1999, Napster, and every one of those files needed something to play it. On Windows, that something was almost always Winamp.

Why did AOL shut Winamp down in 2013?

AOL bought Nullsoft in 1999 for about $80 million. On paper it fit: AOL wanted digital media, and Winamp was the most used media player on Windows. In practice the two cultures could not share a building, and the product damage that followed was self-inflicted.

Winamp3, a 2002 rewrite on a new codebase, broke compatibility with Winamp 2 skins and plug-ins. Users refused it and kept running the old version, so Nullsoft retreated with Winamp 5 in December 2003, merging the classic engine with the newer features and skipping version 4 entirely.

It was too late. iTunes and the iPod took over music libraries, and Windows Media Player came preinstalled on every PC. On November 20, 2013, AOL announced that it would stop supporting the Winamp software, service and website on December 20, 2013, after 15 years.

The shutdown never happened. Radionomy bought Winamp and Shoutcast weeks before the deadline, and AOL kept a minority stake in the buyer instead of switching the servers off.

Is Winamp open source in 2026?

No. In September 2024 the owner, then trading as Llama Group, put the Winamp desktop source code on GitHub. It was not open source in any accepted sense. The Winamp Collaborative License banned forking, which is both the normal way people work on GitHub and a requirement of GitHub's own terms.

Developers read the repository instead of using it. The license was revised to drop the no-forking clause, which fixed little, because distribution stayed prohibited. The published tree was then found to contain third-party code from companies including Dolby, Fraunhofer and Microsoft that the owner had no right to redistribute.

The company deleted every Winamp repository in October 2024, about three weeks after publishing it. The Register summed up the episode in its 2024 headline: "Open sourcing of Winamp goes badly as owners delete entire repo."

Winamp is therefore closed source in 2026, and the code that briefly appeared carries no license anyone can build on. Community work on the classic player runs on reverse engineering and plug-ins, not on that release.

What is Winamp Group building for 2027?

On July 28, 2026 the company announced a partnership with Deezer. Deezer supplies white-label streaming technology and its licensed catalog, Winamp builds the consumer product on top, and the result is a paid subscription sold under the Winamp name.

"Twenty-five years ago we changed the way people listened to digital music, and we believe the time has come to challenge the listening experience once again," Saboundjian said in that announcement.

The described player puts streaming tracks next to local files, internet radio and podcasts in one customizable window, which is close to what the 5.x player already does minus the licensed catalog. The company says the next-generation Winamp Player is due in the first half of 2027, and that the classic desktop app stays available alongside it.

Treat the date as a plan rather than a release. The same company has announced rebuilt Winamp players before and the thing that ships today is still the April 2023 build. Until the new player arrives, what people searching for Winamp get is a small free program that reads a folder of MP3 files, which is what most of them were looking for anyway.
