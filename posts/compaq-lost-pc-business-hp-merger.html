Who Owns Compaq Now? What Is Left of the Brand

2026-09-09 | Updated 2026-09-26 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

Compaq no longer exists as a company. It was absorbed into Hewlett-Packard in an all-stock merger worth about $25 billion that closed on May 3, 2002, and HP stopped selling Compaq-branded PCs in 2013. HP Inc. still owns the trademark and licenses it to other manufacturers, which is why Compaq televisions sell in India and Compaq laptops sell in Brazil.

How did Compaq beat IBM at its own game?

Compaq Computer Corporation was founded in February 1982 by Rod Canion, Jim Harris and Bill Murto, three managers who had just left Texas Instruments. The plan was a portable machine that ran every piece of software written for the IBM PC without copying a line of IBM's code.

The obstacle was the BIOS, the small program that tells an IBM PC how to talk to its own hardware. Compaq solved it with a clean-room project. One team studied what IBM's BIOS did and wrote a specification of its behavior. A second team, which had never seen IBM's code, wrote fresh software to match that specification.

The Compaq Portable shipped in March 1983 at $2,995. It ran Lotus 1-2-3, which was the only compatibility test most business buyers cared about. In that first full year of production Compaq shipped 53,000 machines and booked more than $111 million in revenue, the largest first year any American company had recorded to that point. The entire PC clone industry starts at that machine.

How did Compaq become the world's biggest PC maker?

In 1986 Compaq joined the Fortune 500 in its fourth year of existence, the youngest company to manage it. That September it shipped the Deskpro 386, the first personal computer built around Intel's 32-bit 80386 processor. IBM, which was supposed to set the pace for the standard it had created, took about seven months to answer with the PS/2 Model 80.

That was the moment the PC standard stopped belonging to IBM. Compaq decided what a PC was, and the rest of the industry followed.

It sold those machines through computer dealers rather than its own sales force, and it built that channel from nothing. "There was nobody going exclusively through dealers," Canion said in a 1985 Computerworld interview quoted by Entrepreneur. "That was our own invention."

By 1994 Compaq had passed IBM to become the largest PC maker on earth, twelve years after three men walked out of Texas Instruments. It got there with premium engineering sold through resellers who earned a healthy margin on every box. Both halves of that sentence became the problem.

What went wrong for Compaq in 1991?

Cheap clones got good. Dell, Gateway 2000 and AST were shipping machines that ran the same software for hundreds of dollars less, and buyers stopped paying extra for the Compaq badge.

On October 25, 1991 Compaq reported a third-quarter loss of $70 million, cut 1,440 jobs, about 12 percent of its workforce, and removed Canion as president and chief executive. Chairman Ben Rosen had already commissioned his own study of how cheaply a Compaq could be built. Chief operating officer Eckhard Pfeiffer was named to replace Canion the same day.

Pfeiffer cut prices hard and went after ordinary shoppers rather than corporate buyers. In August 1993 Compaq launched the Presario at $1,399, a complete machine with a monitor and a modem in the box, stacked in electronics stores instead of sold by a reseller. The Computer History Museum dates the launch to August 27, 1993. Within a year Compaq was the biggest PC maker in the world.

Why did buying Digital Equipment backfire?

Pfeiffer wanted a full-service computer company rather than a box shop, so on January 26, 1998 Compaq agreed to buy Digital Equipment Corporation for $9.6 billion, the largest acquisition the computer industry had seen. Digital shareholders received $30 in cash and about 0.945 Compaq shares for each share they held.

The deal closed in June 1998 and the accounting was brutal. Compaq wrote off $3.2 billion of purchased in-process technology and accrued $1.7 billion of restructuring costs, "$1.4 billion of which related to Digital and was recorded as a component of the purchase price allocation," according to its Form 10-Q for the second quarter of 1998. Separation benefits covered roughly 19,700 people, about 14,700 of them from Digital.

It also bought a culture that did not fit. Digital sold minicomputers and services to engineers through its own sales force. Compaq sold consumer PCs through retailers.

Early in 1999 Compaq warned that first-quarter earnings would miss expectations. On April 19, 1999 the board pushed out Pfeiffer along with chief financial officer Earl Mason, and Rosen took over an office of the chief executive. "We have re-energized this company before, and working together, we will do it again," Rosen said in Compaq's announcement that April. Michael Capellas got the job a few months later.

How did Dell push Compaq into HP's arms?

Dell built each machine after the customer ordered it and sold direct. No dealer markup, no warehouse full of last quarter's configurations, no third party's margin to protect. Compaq could not copy that without wrecking the thousands of resellers who carried its products, so it half-copied it for years and the price gap stayed open.

Dell passed Compaq in the United States over 1999, selling 7.02 million PCs for a 16 percent share of that market against Compaq's 6.86 million and 15.7 percent. The worldwide lead went in the first quarter of 2001. CNN reported in April 2001 that Gartner Dataquest and IDC put Dell's shipments up 34.3 percent to 4.16 million units, or 12.8 percent of the global market, with Compaq stalled at 3.9 million units and 12.1 percent.

Compaq was not a small company in 2001. It was a slow one, in a market that had stopped paying for anything except cost. On September 3, 2001 Hewlett-Packard announced it would buy it in an all-stock deal worth about $25 billion, with most of the promised $2.5 billion in annual savings coming from 15,000 job cuts.

Is Compaq still in business in 2026?

No. Walter Hewlett, an HP director and the son of one of its founders, fought the merger, arguing that "Hewlett-Packard can create better value for stockholders as a stand-alone company than as a company combined with Compaq," as Computerworld reported in 2001. Shareholders approved it narrowly on March 19, 2002, Hewlett contested the result in Delaware court and lost, and the deal closed on May 3, 2002.

HP kept the name on budget desktops and Presario laptops for another decade, then stopped selling Compaq-branded machines in 2013. What survives is a trademark. HP Inc. owns it and rents it out, the same arrangement that keeps Packard Bell alive under Acer.

Two licensees carry the name in 2026. Positivo Tecnologia has sold Compaq laptops in Brazil since 2014. Ossify Industries, an Indian manufacturer, signed a licensing deal with HP in 2018 and sells Compaq LED televisions, tablets and soundbars across South Asia and the Middle East. Nothing new wears the badge in the United States, and none of it is made by anyone with a connection to Houston.

Where to find one today. Compaq sold too many Portables for them to be rare, so the 1983 machine is one of the cheapest ways into vintage computing. VintageComputer.com's collector notes put a unit in good working order under $75, with complete systems that still have the carry case and manuals costing more. Presario desktops from the 1990s turn up more often still. Sellers list both, along with original manuals, badges and software boxes, on Etsy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Compaq still in business?

Compaq is not in business as a company. It was absorbed into Hewlett-Packard when the $25 billion merger closed on May 3, 2002, and HP stopped selling Compaq-branded PCs in 2013. The name survives only as a trademark that HP Inc. licenses to other manufacturers.

Who owns the Compaq brand now?

HP Inc. owns the Compaq trademark, inherited when Hewlett-Packard bought Compaq in 2002, and licenses it to other companies. Positivo Tecnologia has used it on laptops in Brazil since 2014, and Ossify Industries has used it on televisions and tablets in South Asia and the Middle East under a deal signed with HP in 2018.

Why did Compaq fail?

Compaq lost the PC business it helped invent because it defended a dealer network that Dell's build-to-order, sell-direct model had made expensive. Dell took the United States lead in 1999 and the world lead in the first quarter of 2001, and the $9.6 billion purchase of Digital Equipment Corporation in 1998 added costs instead of profits, including a $3.2 billion write-off.
