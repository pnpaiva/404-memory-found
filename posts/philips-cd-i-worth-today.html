Is the Philips CD-i Worth Anything? 2026 Prices

2026-10-07 | Updated 2026-10-27 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

The Philips CD-i is worth real money in 2026, but modestly: a working console runs about $300, while the Nintendo-licensed CD-i discs are the expensive part of the platform. Philips launched it in the United States on December 3, 1991 as a multimedia player, sold about 570,000 units worldwide, and dropped the product line in 1998. Nobody has revived the format since.

What was the Philips CD-i supposed to be?

Philips did not build the CD-i as a games machine. Compact Disc Interactive was sold as one black box for music, reference discs, training material and children's titles on an ordinary television set, and it reached buyers through audio dealers and department stores rather than the toy aisle.

The first US players went on sale on December 3, 1991. Sources disagree on what they cost. Giant Bomb lists $699.99 for the launch player, Wikipedia's CD-i article puts the price nearer $1,000, and a University of Luxembourg history page says around $800. All three figures are before a single disc.

Early players shipped with a multimedia remote instead of a gamepad, so anything that needed timing felt wrong from the first minute. The catalog leaned on encyclopedias, golf instruction and children's stories, which is what Philips thought people wanted from a disc player attached to the living room television.

The strategy had a logic to it in 1991. One machine for music, video and software was a reasonable bet before cheap PCs took the multimedia work and purpose-built consoles took the games. The CD-i ended up in the middle, doing neither job well, at a price that assumed it did both.

How Nintendo handed Philips the rights to Zelda and Mario

Sony announced its Super Nintendo CD add-on, the Play Station, at the Summer Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago on June 1, 1991. The next morning Nintendo announced it would build its CD peripheral with Philips instead, and Sony learned about the switch from the audience.

"Our engineers reached the conclusion that from a technical standpoint that it was better for Nintendo to work with Philips," Howard Lincoln of Nintendo of America told The New York Times in 1991, in a quote reproduced by Kotaku in 2018.

The Super NES CD-ROM never shipped. What survived was a side clause: Philips could use Nintendo characters on its own hardware. Link: The Faces of Evil and Zelda: The Wand of Gamelon both reached North America on October 10, 1993. Philips also published Hotel Mario, and Zelda's Adventure followed, dated May 10, 1996 by Wikipedia and 1996 by Zelda Wiki, though contemporary previews had pointed at 1994.

Nintendo kept its distance from all four. "Nintendo's only input was we ran the design document and character sketches past them for their approval," said Dale DeSharone, who ran the Zelda development at Animation Magic, in an interview published by Zelda Universe in 2013. In the same interview he said Nintendo "were mostly interested in the look of the Link and Zelda characters." Nintendo has never treated the games as canon.

Why the CD-i lost to the Sega CD and the PlayStation

The CD-i's best trick was full motion video, and video cost extra. By 1993 Philips had introduced a $250 peripheral that added memory and full-motion video support, and the Digital Video Cartridge that played MPEG-1 movies was the thing the platform's showcase software assumed you had fitted.

Stack that up and the math stops working. A player at $700 or more, plus the cartridge, sat against the $299 Sega CD, which launched in North America on October 15, 1992 and at least plugged into a Genesis the family already owned. The 3DO made the same expensive multimedia argument in 1993 and failed the same way.

Then the price of entry collapsed underneath Philips. The Sega Saturn arrived in 1995 and the PlayStation followed in the United States that autumn, both built for games and both cheaper than a CD-i with the video cartridge installed.

Time Extension puts the end at 1996, when Philips abandoned the system with around 570,000 units sold worldwide. Philips dropped the product line in 1998. Either way, the total is a rounding error beside the tens of millions of units the consoles it was measured against sold.

Is the Philips CD-i still made in 2026?

No. Philips stopped selling CD-i players in the late 1990s and announced the end of the line in 1998, and no company has revived the format since. There is no modern CD-i, no licensed reissue and no official download store for the library.

The company behind it is still here. Koninklijke Philips, the Dutch group that built the machine, now sells health technology, and it has never brought the format back. Nothing about the CD-i name is for sale as a product in 2026.

What exists instead is a second-hand market and emulation. MAME covers a good part of the library, and the surviving players keep a small repair and collecting community busy.

The nearest thing to a revival is a tribute. Arzette: The Jewel of Faramore, released in February 2024, developed by Seedy Eye Software and published by Limited Run Games, copies the hand-painted look of the CD-i Zelda cutscenes. Jeffrey Rath and Bonniejean Wilbur, the original CD-i voices of Link and Zelda, recorded new lines for it. It runs on Nintendo Switch, PlayStation and Xbox consoles and on Windows, which makes it the cheapest way to see what the platform looked like without owning one.

What breaks on a 30-year-old CD-i, and which discs cost the most

A working Philips CD-i is roughly a $300 machine in 2026. PriceCharting's record for the Philips CD-i System shows about $299.99 loose and the same complete, around $600 sealed, about $120 for a box alone and about $75 for a manual.

Bundles move the number a long way. A CD-i 450 sold at $299.99 in April 2023, while a renewed CDI 220 with a gamepad, a Digital Video Cartridge and a new timekeeper chip went for $550 in July 2021.

Condition on these players is mostly the disc drive and the clock. CD-i hardware uses a battery-backed timekeeper chip that dies with age, taking saves and settings with it and stopping some discs from booting, which is why sellers advertise a replacement chip as a selling point. Pay the premium for the repaired unit.

The discs carry the real money, and they are the Nintendo ones. PriceCharting puts Zelda: The Wand of Gamelon at about $222 complete in box, about $496 sealed and about $150 loose, with disc-and-manual copies selling between roughly $137 and $175 in late 2025. Graded sealed copies are asked at several thousand dollars, but confirmed sales at that level are rare enough that the asking prices are not a guide.

Where to find one today. Budget about $300 for a tested console with a controller, and more for a boxed set with the Digital Video Cartridge included. A retro hardware specialist is a safer route than an unknown seller for a 30-year-old optical drive, and Stone Age Gamer's catalog search is a reasonable place to start looking for players and accessories.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Philips CD-i worth anything in 2026?

Yes, modestly. A working Philips CD-i console runs about $300 in 2026, with PriceCharting showing roughly $299.99 loose and about $600 for a sealed system. The real money on the platform sits in its Nintendo-licensed discs: Zelda: The Wand of Gamelon averages about $222 complete in box and about $496 sealed.

Why did the Philips CD-i fail?

The Philips CD-i failed because the first US players cost $700 or more at launch in December 1991, the best software wanted a $250 add-on for full-motion video, and the machine was weak at games against the $299 Sega CD and later the PlayStation. Philips abandoned the system with about 570,000 units sold worldwide and dropped the product line in 1998.

What Zelda games came out on the Philips CD-i?

Three Zelda games reached the Philips CD-i: Link: The Faces of Evil and Zelda: The Wand of Gamelon, both released in North America on October 10, 1993, and Zelda's Adventure, dated 1996. Philips also published Hotel Mario. All four came from a licence Nintendo granted Philips in 1991 after their Super NES CD-ROM partnership collapsed, and Nintendo does not treat them as canon.
