Flooz.com Explained: The Dot-Com Cash That Vanished

2026-10-09 | Updated 2026-10-29 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Flooz.com sold an online gift currency from 1999 until August 2001, when it suspended its site and filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy. It raised about $35 million from investors, spent $8 million of that on a Whoopi Goldberg advertising campaign, and left unused flooz balances worthless and nonrefundable. Flooz.com is still defunct in 2026, and the bankruptcy case did not close until 2012.

Quick facts

$8 million, fronted by Whoopi Goldberg

How did the flooz gift currency work?

Flooz was a gift currency rather than a bank account. A buyer charged a dollar amount to a credit card, named a recipient, and Flooz.com emailed that person a balance to spend at the online stores in its network.

The value moved one way. Dollars went in, flooz came out, and flooz could not be turned back into dollars. It could only be spent at a store that had agreed to take it.

That one-way valve was the whole product. Flooz.com collected real money up front and issued a credit that worked only inside its own merchant list, which made the company responsible for keeping that list long enough to be worth something.

It worked for a while. Roughly 30 online retailers accepted flooz at checkout by the summer of 2001, according to the E-Commerce Times, and the brand was familiar enough that people gave the currency as a present.

The weakness was structural. A dollar is worth a dollar everywhere. A flooz was worth a dollar at about 30 websites, and only while the company behind it kept paying its bills.

What did the Whoopi Goldberg campaign cost?

Flooz.com spent $8 million on the advertising campaign that introduced the currency, with Whoopi Goldberg fronting its television advertising, CNNMoney reported in its account of the collapse.

Set that against the money the company had. Flooz.com raised about $35 million from investors, so the launch campaign alone accounted for close to a quarter of everything that went in.

The logic was not unreasonable. A new currency is worth nothing until people recognize it, and recognition in 1999 was bought on television. Goldberg gave an abstract product a face, and a television spot explained it faster than a press release could.

The second half of the equation was the problem. Advertising told people flooz existed. It did not tell them where to spend it, and the list of places was short.

By the time the money ran low, Flooz.com was carrying a consumer brand far larger than the commerce underneath it.

Which stores accepted flooz, and why did they stop?

The names in the network were real ones. The online store of Barnes & Noble and Tower Records both took flooz at checkout, and about 30 retailers accepted it by 2001.

For a merchant the arrangement was cheap. The retailer picked up a customer holding credit that could only be spent inside the network, and Flooz.com handled the billing.

It ended without warning. On August 8, 2001 Flooz.com suspended operations and told the roughly 30 e-tailers that accepted its currency that it would stop processing transactions, the E-Commerce Times reported. Shoppers who clicked through to pay with flooz found nothing on the other end.

The retailers moved fast. Barnesandnoble.com removed flooz from its site because of the problems the company was having, a spokesperson told the same outlet in 2001.

That was the moment the currency stopped being a currency. A balance is spendable only while somebody accepts it, and a merchant network that can be switched off in an afternoon is not money in a wallet.

How did a credit-card ring drain Flooz.com's cash?

While the merchant network thinned, something worse was moving through the payment pipe. Over roughly three months in 2001, Flooz.com unknowingly sold about $300,000 of its currency to a ring of credit-card thieves operating out of Russia and the Philippines.

The method suited the product. Stolen card numbers bought flooz, the flooz moved by email, and the value came out the other end as merchandise. The FBI alerted the company to the scheme.

The fatal blow came from Flooz.com's own payment processor rather than from the thieves. Once charges began appearing on the statements of cardholders whose numbers had been stolen, the processor withheld the daily reimbursements the company lived on and froze other accounts until it was holding about $1 million of Flooz.com's money by the second week of August 2001.

The Deseret News set out that sequence on August 27, 2001. A business that collects cash today and settles with merchants later cannot survive having its incoming cash withheld.

Robert Levitan, the chief executive, pointed at both the fraud and the dot-com slump when he explained the decision to stop.

Is Flooz.com still around in 2026?

No. Flooz.com has been dead since August 2001, nothing has revived it, and no company runs a working version of the currency today.

The sequence sits in the public record. Operations were suspended on August 8, 2001. The company closed for good over the following weekend and said it would file for bankruptcy. A Chapter 7 petition for Flooz.Com, Inc. was filed on August 31, 2001 in the Southern District of New York, case number 1:01-bk-14833.

Chapter 7 means liquidation rather than reorganization: assets are sold and creditors are paid if anything is left over. The docket names Robert E. Gerber as the judge and David R. Kittay as the trustee.

Reports at the time did not agree on which chapter was coming. The Deseret News wrote in August 2001 that the company planned to seek Chapter 11 protection, which would have meant reorganizing and carrying on. The docket shows what was actually filed, and it was Chapter 7.

The case outlived the company. It reopened in 2010 and closed in 2012, more than a decade after the website went dark.

For anyone still holding flooz, the answer arrived in 2001 and has not changed. Unused balances became worthless and were not refundable.

Why did no buyer want Flooz.com?

Between the suspension and the final shutdown, Flooz.com tried to sell itself. Levitan said the company had been in merger discussions with a number of companies and could not find a suitable partner. When it gave up he put it plainly: "We have decided that there's no other choice," he told Adweek in 2001, adding that all employees had been terminated.

The official explanation leaned on the market rather than on the fraud. The notice on the site blamed "dramatic changes in capital markets and the general slowdown in the economy," which was true of nearly every dot-com that year and said nothing about the frozen $1 million.

Analysts were less diplomatic. Forrester's James Crawford called flooz "essentially glorified loyalty points masquerading as cash" in the E-Commerce Times in 2001, and said he could not think of a company that would want to merge with Flooz.com after it switched off its site.

There was also little left to buy. The merchant contracts were worthless once the site stopped processing, and the one asset with any value was a list of people who had reason to be angry. Flooz.com was shopping a switched-off consumer currency in the middle of a collapse that had already taken Pets.com.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happened to Flooz.com?

Flooz.com, the online gift currency launched in 1999, suspended its website on August 8, 2001 and shut down for good later that month, after the FBI alerted it to a credit-card fraud ring and its payment processor withheld about $1 million of its funds. A Chapter 7 bankruptcy petition for Flooz.Com, Inc. was filed on August 31, 2001 in the Southern District of New York.

Was Whoopi Goldberg really the face of Flooz?

Yes. Whoopi Goldberg fronted the $8 million advertising campaign that introduced Flooz.com in 1999, appearing in television advertising for the online gift currency. Flooz.com raised about $35 million from investors in total, so that one campaign accounted for close to a quarter of it.

Did Flooz customers get their money back?

No. When Flooz.com shut down in August 2001, unused flooz balances became worthless and were not refundable, and what was left of the company went into a Chapter 7 liquidation in the Southern District of New York. That case, number 1:01-bk-14833, did not close until 2012.
