Nintendo 64 Value in 2026: What an N64 Is Worth

By Marcus Vale · Updated November 12, 2026 · First published October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

A Nintendo 64 is worth about $65 to $90 loose in 2026 and roughly $202 to $246 complete in its box, on the SportsCardPro guide built from PriceCharting's completed sales, all of it under the $199.99 the console cost at its US launch on September 29, 1996. The Nintendo 64 still exists only as used hardware: Nintendo retired it in Japan on April 30, 2002, ended North American production in 2003, and offers no repair or warranty service today. What a console, its box and a complete set cost now

A loose Nintendo 64, meaning the console with a controller and the cables and nothing else, runs about $65 to $90 in 2026. The SportsCardPro guide, which is built on PriceCharting's completed-sales data rather than asking prices, has logged the gray NTSC console at $65.76 and at $69.00 in its 2026 snapshots, and a BGR price roundup published this year put the average loose sale nearer $90.

Keeping the cardboard changes the math. The same guide puts a console with its original box at about $140, and a genuinely complete package with box, inserts and manual between about $202 and $246, depending on which snapshot you read. Complete is where the money sits, and complete is scarce, because the box was the first thing thrown out in 1997.

Every one of those figures is under the $199.99 Nintendo charged at the US launch on September 29, 1996, and that is before any adjustment for three decades of inflation. A console that outsold almost everything in its first Christmas is now a cheap shelf item.

Two parts decide whether a cheap console is a good one. The original controller's analog stick wears down with use, and a worn stick drifts in menus and in Super Mario 64. The console also needs its power brick and a composite or S-Video cable, and the listings at the bottom of the market are exactly the ones that leave those out.

Where to find one today

Tested hardware costs more than the loose guide price, and for a 1996 console that is usually money well spent. Stone Age Gamer's Nintendo 64 section stocks consoles, controllers and replacement parts, which beats gambling on an untested unit from a marketplace seller.

Are Nintendo 64 games worth anything in 2026?

More than the console, in several cases. The SportsCardPro guide lists Super Mario 64 at $34.99 loose and $116.73 complete, Mario Kart 64 at about $46 loose and about $116 complete, and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time at $32.46 loose and $122.50 complete in 2026.

Read those next to the hardware numbers and the market looks strange. A single loose copy of Super Mario 64 is worth roughly half a loose console, and a complete copy of Ocarina of Time at $122.50 is worth close to a boxed console at $140.

The reason is that cartridges survived differently. Consoles sat in closets intact while boxes and manuals went in the bin, so complete software is rarer than working hardware even for titles that sold in the millions.

The practical advice for anyone clearing out a cupboard in 2026 is to price the games before the machine. Three common complete cartridges can be worth more than the console, the controller and the cables put together.

Which Nintendo 64 cartridge costs the most?

ClayFighter Sculptor's Cut, a 1998 release that Blockbuster handed out as a rental exclusive before selling the stock to customers, is the most expensive standard Nintendo 64 release. The SportsCardPro guide puts it at about $948 for the cartridge alone and about $6,444 complete in 2026.

That cartridge, with no box and no manual, is worth more than ten complete consoles. It is the clearest measure of where this market went. The hardware stayed cheap because 32.93 million consoles were built, while cartridges that shipped in the thousands kept climbing.

The pattern repeats below the record. Late releases, licensed sports titles with small print runs and the games stocked for rental rather than sale are the ones that cost real money, while the titles that sold millions can be had for the price of lunch.

It also means the grail prices and the ordinary prices are moving in opposite directions, which is why a guide figure from three years ago is no help when you are pricing a shelf today.

Is the Nintendo 64 still supported by Nintendo?

No. Nintendo retired the console in Japan on April 30, 2002 and ended production for North America in 2003, and there has been no warranty service, no first-party repair and no new hardware since. Lifetime sales came to 32.93 million units, 20.63 million of them in the Americas, so about 63 percent of every Nintendo 64 ever built was sold in this hemisphere.

Its replacement was the GameCube, which reached US stores in late 2001 and moved Nintendo to a small optical disc. The N64 kept selling alongside it for two more years at the budget end before the line closed.

What Nintendo still sells is the software. A rotating set of Nintendo 64 games runs on the Nintendo Switch through the Expansion Pack tier of Nintendo Switch Online, emulated, with save states and a wireless controller that copies the 1996 shape.

That is the real fork for anyone pricing this out in 2026. The subscription is the cheap way to play the famous titles, and original hardware is the only way to play a cartridge you already own on the console it was cut for.

How the 1996 sell-out set today's supply

Nintendo put the console in US stores on September 29, 1996 with two games, Super Mario 64 and Pilotwings 64. By October 24, 1996, UPI reported that Nintendo of America had sold 460,000 of them at $200 each.

Then the shelves emptied. Stores ran short through November and December 1996, and Nintendo of America told reporters late that month that it expected to top 1.6 million systems in the United States by December 31.

Peter Main, Nintendo of America's executive vice president of sales and marketing, told The Seattle Times in December 1996 that the quarter made the console "easily the fastest selling system ever." In an Associated Press report the same week, carried by The Spokesman-Review, Main put a dollar figure on it: "Nintendo 64 hardware and software retail sales will have averaged more than $5 million a day from our Sept. 29 launch through Dec. 31."

All of that matters to a buyer three decades later. A console that moved 20.63 million units in the Americas left a deep second-hand pool, and that supply is why gray plastic consoles have stayed flat while scarce cartridges have not. The system's anchors hold their value better, and GoldenEye 007 in 1997 is the title that sold consoles to people who had already finished Super Mario 64.
