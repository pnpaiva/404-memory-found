Are Pagers Still Used in 2026? Hospitals Say Yes

2026-09-30 | By Theo Lindqvist | 5 min read

Pagers are still used in 2026, almost entirely in healthcare and emergency response. Spok Holdings, the largest US paging carrier, reported approximately 675,000 wireless units in service on December 31, 2025, down from approximately 720,000 a year earlier. The devices survive because paging radio penetrates hospital walls that cellular signals do not.

How many pagers are still in service in the United States?

Spok Holdings, the largest paging carrier left in the country, reported approximately 675,000 wireless units in service on December 31, 2025, down from approximately 720,000 a year earlier. Spok is publicly traded, so that count is filed rather than estimated.

The fall is steady rather than sudden. Spok paging revenue came to $68.6 million in 2025, down 3.4% from 2024, and the company's annual report for that year states plainly that "demand for wireless services will continue to decline for the foreseeable future."

What has not happened is a shutdown. Spok counted more than 2,200 hospitals among its customers at the end of 2025, and average revenue per unit rose rather than fell, from $7.97 a month in 2024 to $8.20 in 2025. A shrinking network with rising per-unit revenue is what a business looks like when the customers who remain have nowhere else to go.

That 675,000 figure is a floor for the country rather than a full census, because it counts only Spok. American Messaging, the other national paging carrier, runs its own network and does not publish a subscriber count, and neither do the fire and EMS departments that operate paging on their own frequencies.

Why hospitals never gave up the beeper

A national survey of 620 hospital-based clinicians, published in the Journal of Hospital Medicine in 2017, found that 495 of them, or 79.8%, had been issued a pager by their hospital. Of the same group, 304, or 49%, said the pager was still the most common way patient care messages reached them.

The reason is physics before it is habit. Paging networks run roughly in the 150 to 450 MHz range, where longer wavelengths pass through concrete, steel and lead-lined radiology rooms. Cellular data sits much higher up the spectrum, and a basement corridor that kills a phone signal is an ordinary Tuesday inside a hospital.

Paging is also a broadcast rather than a conversation. Every transmitter in a region sends the same message at the same instant, a technique called simulcast, so there is no handoff between towers and no data session to negotiate. When a storm or a stadium saturates the cellular network, the paging channel is still sending.

Meredith Barrett, MD, an assistant professor of transplant surgery at Michigan Medicine, described that persistence to Becker's Hospital Review in 2023: "They're like the cockroaches of the healthcare system." She also told the publication that her pager works in places her phone does not, and that leaving it in the car is how she ends a shift.

What killed the pager for everyone else

Paging was enormous before it collapsed. More than 61 million pagers were in use by 1994, and the American paging business took in more than $4.4 billion in revenue at its 1996 peak.

Then cell phones got cheap. By 2000 the number of pager users in the United States had fallen to roughly 37 million. A device that could only tell you to call someone back had no answer to a device that let you make the call.

Motorola, which held about 85% of the American pager market at its height, stopped manufacturing and servicing pagers in 2002. Revenue kept sliding behind the subscribers: US paging service revenue was down to $489 million by 2011, about a ninth of the 1996 figure.

Paging did try to answer. Carriers pushed alphanumeric units that could show a full text message, then two-way models that could send a canned reply, which walked the device toward the phone at exactly the moment the phone got cheap. It was a race paging could not win, because the feature that decided it was the voice call paging had never carried.

The same curve flattened a lot of 1990s hardware. Payphones emptied out for the same reason, and the handsets that replaced both, among them the Nokia 3310, did everything a pager did plus the part that mattered.

What a pager costs to run in 2026

Paging is a subscription now, not a purchase. Spok reported average revenue per unit of $8.20 a month across 2025, which is roughly what a carrier collects for each active pager on a hospital contract, up from $7.97 in 2024.

The hardware usually belongs to the carrier. Spok's current device, the GenA pager announced in November 2021, is a one-way alphanumeric unit with an ePaper display, over-the-air reprogramming and an antimicrobial housing. It is a 2020s product built to do a job defined in the 1990s.

Where to find one today. Numeric and alphanumeric pagers from the 1990s turn up regularly as display pieces, props and desk objects, and the usual place to look is Etsy's vintage pager listings. Buying the plastic is the cheap part. Putting a pager back on a live network means a carrier contract, and $8.20 per unit per month is the figure Spok actually collected in 2025.

Who still carries a pager besides doctors

Volunteer fire departments and EMS squads across the country still issue tone-and-voice pagers. Those sit on the department's own radio frequency and open the speaker when a dispatcher sends the alert tone, which is why a volunteer asleep at home at 3am gets woken by the call and not by a muted phone.

Utilities, industrial plants and some campus security teams keep paging for the same reason. Spok sells the service as critical messaging rather than as a cheaper phone, and the pitch is reliability under conditions where the cellular network is the thing that broke.

For everyone else the pager function moved inside the phone and stayed there. The same consolidation absorbed the standalone GPS units that used to sit on windshields. A page is a push notification now, carried on the network everyone is already paying for, which is exactly why the 675,000 units Spok still runs are the ones that cannot rely on it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you still buy a pager in 2026?

Yes. Pagers are still sold and serviced in the United States in 2026, mainly by Spok Holdings and American Messaging, and Spok launched a current-generation device, the GenA pager, in November 2021. Most pagers are leased with a service contract rather than bought outright, and Spok collected an average of $8.20 per unit per month across 2025.

Why do doctors still use pagers instead of phones?

Paging networks broadcast in the 150 to 450 MHz range, which passes through the concrete, steel and lead-lined rooms that block cellular signals inside hospitals, and every transmitter in a region sends the same message at once instead of handing a call between towers. A national survey of 620 hospital clinicians published in the Journal of Hospital Medicine in 2017 found 79.8% had been issued a pager, and 49% still received patient care messages by pager most often.

How many pagers are left in the United States?

Spok Holdings, the largest US paging carrier, reported approximately 675,000 wireless units in service on December 31, 2025, down from approximately 720,000 at the end of 2024. That is a fraction of the more than 61 million pagers in use in 1994, and it does not include the tone-and-voice pagers that volunteer fire departments run on their own radio frequencies.
