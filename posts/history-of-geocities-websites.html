Does GeoCities Still Exist? Where the Pages Went

By Dana Reyes · Updated November 10, 2026 · First published March 23, 2026 · 7 min read

GeoCities no longer exists. Yahoo shut the service down on October 26, 2009 and deleted the member-built pages, which Wired put at 38 million, and Yahoo Japan closed the last working version on March 31, 2019. The name now sits with Yahoo Inc., about 90 percent owned by funds managed by affiliates of Apollo Global Management since 2021, while the surviving pages live in the Internet Archive and an Archive Team torrent of about 641 GB.

How Beverly Hills Internet became a city of neighborhoods

The service started in 1994 as Beverly Hills Internet, a small Los Angeles company run by David Bohnett and John Rezner. It handed out free web space and did one thing no other host bothered with: it gave every page an address inside a themed neighborhood.

A science fiction page lived in Area 51. A celebrity fan page went to Hollywood. Programmers took SiliconValley, families took Heartland, and money pages went to WallStreet. The address told a visitor what the page was about, on a web with no search engine worth using yet.

The company took the GeoCities name and grew fast. Yahoo's announcement of the purchase in January 1999 counted more than 3.5 million members, called homesteaders, who had built over 32 million pages between them. The same release cited Media Metrix figures putting GeoCities above 19 million unique visitors in December 1998 and third among the most trafficked sites on the internet, behind AOL and Yahoo.

Free hosting was a crowded business by then, and the rivals aged the same way. Angelfire outlived GeoCities by seventeen years before Lycos closed it in 2026.

Why the first FTC internet privacy case named GeoCities

In August 1998, months before Yahoo made an offer, the company settled charges brought by the Federal Trade Commission. The agency said GeoCities had collected personal details through its membership form and passed them to advertisers while telling members the data was for something else.

"GeoCities misled its customers, both children and adults, by not telling the truth about how it was using their personal information," said Jodie Bernstein, director of the FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection, in the agency's 1998 announcement.

The settlement carried no fine and no admission of wrongdoing. What it carried instead was a set of rules the rest of the web later inherited: post a clear privacy notice saying what is collected and who receives it, get parental consent before collecting anything from children 12 and under, and link to the FTC's own privacy education material for five years.

It was the FTC's first internet privacy case. Critics at the time called the terms a slap on the wrist, since nobody paid anything. The privacy policy link at the bottom of almost every page in 2026 traces back to a consent order about a free homepage service.

What Yahoo paid, and the protest that followed

Yahoo agreed to buy GeoCities on January 28, 1999 in an all-stock deal. CNN reported the value that day at about $3.57 billion, calculated from Yahoo's closing price of $335.875 against the 31.4 million GeoCities shares outstanding, against a market capitalization of $2.3 billion before the news.

Later accounts of the same deal do not agree. TechCrunch, writing about the shutdown in 2009, put the purchase at $2.87 billion. eWeek that same week called it $3.6 billion. The gap is Yahoo's share price, which moved hard between the announcement in January and Yahoo taking control in May 1999.

The trouble started with paperwork. On June 25, 1999 Yahoo posted new terms of service that appeared to hand it the right to reuse anything a member had published, and locked members out of editing their own sites until they accepted.

Members answered with a boycott that became known as the Haunting. They stripped their pages back to bare backgrounds, deleted the animated GIFs and the visitor counters, and replaced the content with excerpts of the offending terms. Whole neighborhoods went grey at once.

Yahoo gave in within days. The rewritten terms stated plainly that "Yahoo does not claim ownership of the Content you place on your Yahoo GeoCities Site," and the organizers called the boycott off on July 6, 1999. A user base had renegotiated a platform contract by refusing to use the platform, five years before Facebook existed.

Who owns GeoCities today?

The name belongs to Yahoo Inc., and Yahoo is roughly 90 percent owned by funds managed by affiliates of Apollo Global Management, which completed a roughly $5 billion purchase in September 2021. Verizon kept the other 10 percent. There is no product left for anyone to run, and the brand is a dormant asset attached to a domain that serves no pages.

Yahoo announced the shutdown on April 23, 2009 and stopped taking new members that day. Traffic had already collapsed: comScore counted 11.5 million US unique visitors in March 2009, down 24 percent from 15.1 million a year earlier, though VentureBeat cited slightly different comScore numbers at the time.

The service went dark on October 26, 2009, and the pages went with it. Wired put the count at 38 million at last measure, a figure it attributed to Yahoo rather than to any audit. Yahoo kept no copy. Members were told to download their own files, and old geocities.com addresses now land on a Yahoo page explaining that the service closed.

One outpost lasted a decade longer. Yahoo Japan announced on October 1, 2018 that its own version was closing, blaming profitability and system problems. It stopped serving pages on March 31, 2019, and deleted the stored files a year later, on March 31, 2020.

How much of it did anyone manage to save?

Two rescue efforts ran at the same time. The Internet Archive ran deep crawls from July to October 2009, including sites the public nominated, and serves those captures through the Wayback Machine as its GeoCities Special Collection.

Archive Team, the volunteer group Jason Scott started in 2009, ran its own harvest with dozens of people and hundreds of machines. In 2010 it published the result as a single torrent, listed on its own wiki at 641.4 GB, which iTnews reported at the time as 652 GB. It is still the largest public block of this material anywhere.

Scott did not soften his view of what Yahoo had done. He told Time in 2009 that the site had been "the largest self-created folk-art collection in the history of the world," and that Yahoo "found the way to destroy the most massive amount of history in the shortest amount of time with absolutely no recourse."

Artists Olia Lialina and Dragan Espenschied then built something out of that torrent. Their project One Terabyte of Kilobyte Age started posting automated screenshots of rebuilt pages in 2013, one every twenty minutes, captured at 800 by 600 pixels in period-correct browsers. Rhizome has since treated it as an art archive rather than a backup.

What replaced it for people who want a homepage?

The direct heir is Neocities, launched by Kyle Drake in 2013 with the stated goal of reviving the free hosting GeoCities used to provide. A free site gets 1 GB of storage and 200 GB of monthly bandwidth, runs no server-side code, and the service counted more than 1,763,200 sites hosted as of May 2026.

Scale brings its own problem. Ars Technica reported on February 5, 2026 that Bing had dropped roughly 1.5 million Neocities sites from search results under its low-quality content policy, cutting Drake's traffic from that source to near zero and routing his appeals to an automated support bot. Microsoft restored the front page within a day of being asked about it, and most member subdomains stayed blocked.

The bigger answer is that the audience moved. Squarespace, Wix and WordPress.com absorbed the people who wanted a presentable page, and social platforms absorbed everyone who only wanted somewhere to post. Both traded the handmade look for templates.

Bohnett describes the loss as one of purpose rather than design. "GeoCities was not about self-promotion. It was about sharing your interest and your knowledge," he told Gizmodo in 2022. A few pages from that era are still served at their original addresses, though every one of them is hosted somewhere else.

Frequently Asked Questions

When did GeoCities shut down?

Yahoo shut GeoCities down in the United States on October 26, 2009, six months after announcing the closure on April 23, 2009. Wired put the number of pages deleted at 38 million, a figure Yahoo supplied, and Yahoo kept no copy of them. Yahoo Japan ran a separate GeoCities until March 31, 2019 and deleted its stored files on March 31, 2020.

Can you still see old GeoCities pages?

Yes. The Internet Archive crawled GeoCities from July to October 2009 and serves those captures through the Wayback Machine as its GeoCities Special Collection. Archive Team's parallel harvest, published in 2010 as a torrent its wiki lists at 641.4 GB, holds more material again. Neither copy of GeoCities is complete, and the pages nobody ever linked to are the ones most likely missing.

How much did Yahoo pay for GeoCities?

Yahoo announced its purchase of GeoCities on January 28, 1999, and CNN valued the all-stock deal that day at about $3.57 billion, based on Yahoo's closing price of $335.875. Later accounts differ: TechCrunch put the purchase at $2.87 billion in 2009 and eWeek at $3.6 billion, because Yahoo stock moved between the announcement and the closing in May 1999. Yahoo shut the service down ten years after buying it, in October 2009.
