Does the Motorola Razr V3 Still Work in 2026?

By Marcus Vale · Updated November 8, 2026 · First published April 1, 2026 · 6 min read

The Motorola Razr V3 no longer works as a phone in the United States. It was a 2G-only GSM handset, and T-Mobile shut off the last major US 2G GSM network on August 3, 2026, after AT&T switched its own off in 2017 and Verizon followed in 2020. Lenovo has owned the Razr name since October 30, 2014 and sells folding Razrs from $799 to $1,899, while original V3s trade as collectibles.

Why can't a Razr V3 register on a US network?

The V3 is a quad-band GSM handset with a 2G radio and nothing else. The networks it needs are gone.

AT&T switched off its 2G GSM network in 2017. Verizon followed in 2020. T-Mobile held on the longest, and Fierce Network reported that the carrier had once set April 2, 2024 as the end date before pushing it back. The last major US 2G GSM network went dark on August 3, 2026.

A T-Mobile spokesperson told Fierce Network in 2026 that the remaining 2G user base was "de minimis" and that "Any remaining customers have been informed and provided ample time to migrate to modern network technologies." What was left on the network by then was alarm panels, utility meters and logistics trackers, not collectors' flip phones.

So a V3 in 2026 powers on, lights the etched keypad and snaps shut with the same sound, and cannot register on a carrier. No calls, no texts, no 911. It is hardware that outlived its own infrastructure, the way the pay phones still standing in the United States did.

What did the Razr V3 cost when it launched?

Motorola released the V3 in the United States on November 16, 2004, exclusively through Cingular Wireless, at $499 with a two-year contract. NBC News put the price at about $500 and described the phone as the star of a portfolio of 20 new Motorola handsets, which is a fair measure of how much the company was betting on one design.

That made it the most expensive handset in Motorola's range, and the exclusivity meant anyone on another carrier had to wait or switch providers to get one.

The body justified the premium. The V3 measured 13.9 millimeters thick and weighed 95 grams in a market full of plastic bricks. The chassis was metal rather than painted plastic. The keypad was a flat etched sheet instead of rubber nubs, backlit so the digits glowed through it.

Motorola sold roughly 130 million V3s across every variant over about four years of production. The company never published an audited lifetime total, so that figure is a tally assembled afterwards and repeated by the tech press rather than a certified number. The phone sold on how it looked, in the same way the Nokia 3310 sold on how much abuse it survived.

How long was the Razr V3 America's best-selling phone?

Twelve straight quarters, which is three full years at number one. The Razr led The NPD Group's ranking of handsets bought by US adults until the third quarter of 2008, when the iPhone 3G took the top spot.

NPD's figures for that quarter, published on November 10, 2008 and reported by Engadget, put the top five as the iPhone 3G, the Motorola Razr V3, the BlackBerry Curve, the LG Rumor and the LG enV2. Ross Rubin, NPD's director of industry analysis, called the result a "watershed shift in handset design from fashion to fashionable functionality."

The rest of that data explains the shift. US adult handset purchases fell 15 percent year over year to 32 million units in the quarter, and consumer handset revenue fell 10 percent to $2.9 billion, while phones with QWERTY keyboards climbed to 30 percent of sales from 11 percent a year earlier.

Buyers had started paying for keyboards and software. The V3 offered neither. BlackBerry owned corporate email by then, and Apple had made a touchscreen the thing to beat.

What ended the original Razr's run?

Success, mostly. Motorola spent the back half of the decade re-releasing the same phone. The V3i added a better camera and a memory card slot. The V3x and V3xx bolted on 3G. The Razr2 arrived in 2007 as the actual sequel, three years after the original, with the same hinge and the same silhouette.

None of them changed what the phone was underneath. The V3 shipped with a 0.3-megapixel camera, almost no storage and no way to add software, which nobody minded in 2004 and everybody minded by 2007.

The bill came due fast. Motorola's handset business was losing money by the end of 2007, and in March 2008 the company said it would separate its mobile devices unit into a standalone public company. The V3 was discontinued that same year, after holding the top of the US rankings for most of the previous four.

Motorola had built the most popular phone in America with nothing queued behind it, and its handset division spent the following years shrinking into a takeover target.

Who owns the Razr name today?

Lenovo. Google, which had bought Motorola Mobility for $12.5 billion in 2012, sold the handset business on, in a deal that closed on October 30, 2014 at a purchase price of about $2.91 billion: roughly $660 million in cash, $750 million in Lenovo shares and a $1.5 billion three-year promissory note.

Google kept the majority of the patent portfolio. Motorola went to Lenovo with the brand, about 3,500 employees, its Chicago headquarters and more than 2,000 patent assets. "This partnership has always been a perfect fit," Lenovo chairman and chief executive Yang Yuanqing said in the announcement that day.

Lenovo brought the name back in 2019 as a vertically folding smartphone and has shipped a new one almost every year since. The 2026 line was announced on April 29, 2026, with pre-orders on May 14 and shipping on May 21. It runs $799 for the Razr, $1,099 for the Razr+, $1,499 for the Razr Ultra and $1,899 for the book-style Razr Fold.

The reaction was muted. 9to5Google reported that the 2026 phones are close to spec-identical to the 2025 ones at higher prices, which is a strange echo of what Motorola did to the V3 twenty years earlier.

Where to find one today

Original V3s are still easy to find, and what one costs depends almost entirely on whether the box survived. Asking prices on Etsy's Motorola Razr V3 search, checked in November 2026, ran from about $32 for a gray handset sold for parts to $422.26 for a sealed pink one, with new-in-box black examples around $327 to $360 and a used working unit listed at $260.

Those are asking prices rather than sold prices, and the spread between a parts phone and a sealed one is the entire market. Read the listing text before paying a premium: many are described as untested or seller refurbished, which usually means a replacement shell or a fresh battery rather than an untouched 2004 unit.

And nothing bought in 2026 will make a call in the United States, whatever a listing says about being unlocked, because the network it needs no longer exists. A loose handset with a tired battery is a desk ornament, and for most buyers that is the point.
