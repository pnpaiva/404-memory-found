Is Microsoft Bob Still Around? Only as a Download

2026-05-07 | Updated 2026-09-24 | By Theo Lindqvist | 6 min read

Microsoft Bob is not sold anywhere in 2026. Microsoft discontinued the cartoon-house interface in early 1996, less than a year after it reached stores on March 10, 1995 at $99, and has never revived it. The only working copies today are the free disk images preserved on the Internet Archive, and Bob's two survivors, Comic Sans and Clippy, outlasted the product by three decades.

Quick facts
Launched
March 10, 1995, by Microsoft, at $99
Copies sold
About 58,000, according to retail tracker PC Data
Discontinued
Early 1996, less than a year after launch
Status today
Discontinued and unsupported; free disk images on the Internet Archive
Owner today

What was Microsoft Bob supposed to do?

Microsoft Bob replaced the Windows desktop with a cartoon house. Rooms held furniture, and the furniture was the software. A calendar on the wall opened a calendar. A pen and paper on the desk opened a letter writer. A checkbook opened a finance program. Files, folders and drive letters never appeared on screen at all.

A cartoon guide came with the house. The default was Rover, a yellow dog who talked in a speech balloon and addressed the user by name. The other guides included Scuzz the rat and Chaos the cat, each with a scripted personality that Microsoft's team called a social interface.

The premise made sense in 1994. Millions of Americans were about to buy a first computer, and Windows 3.1 met them with the Program Manager, file paths and gray dialog boxes. Microsoft's answer was to hide the operating system behind a living room. Bob ran on Windows 3.1 and Windows 95, wanted a 486 processor, 8 MB of RAM and 30 MB of free disk space, and reached stores on March 10, 1995 at a retail price of $99.

Why did Microsoft Bob fail so badly?

The friendliness cost time. Writing a letter in Windows 3.1 took two clicks on a program icon. In Microsoft Bob it meant loading the house, crossing to the right room, finding the right object, clicking it and waiting for a cartoon character to stop talking. Every task carried that tax, and the tax never got smaller as users learned the product.

The hardware math was worse. Bob asked for 8 MB of RAM in a year when budget consumer PCs aimed at first-time buyers still shipped with 4 MB. The audience the product was designed for owned the machines least able to run it.

Reviewers were blunt. The New York Times in 1995 called the house the work of an "esthetically challenged sixth-grader" and wrote that the guides "speak primarily through dialogue balloons, addressing you by name as incessantly as an insurance broker." The same review argued the software was not as simple to use as Microsoft had advertised, which was the whole pitch. Beginners found Bob patronizing. Anyone with basic computer literacy found it pointless.

How many copies of Microsoft Bob actually sold?

Retail tracker PC Data put total sales from launch to discontinuation at roughly 58,000 copies. PCWorld has cited a lower figure of about 30,000, so treat 58,000 as the high end of a small number either way. Microsoft had expected sales in the millions, on the scale of Microsoft Works and Encarta.

The comparison that stings is internal. PC Data counted about 2.75 million retail copies of Windows 95 in the first month after Windows 95 went on sale in August 1995. Bob sold about 2 percent of that across its entire commercial life.

Microsoft pulled the product in early 1996 without an announcement. A planned second version never shipped. The reputation outlived the software: PCWorld ranked Microsoft Bob the seventh worst tech product of all time in its 2006 list, and TIME later included it among the 50 worst inventions. Bill Gates, who had championed the project, addressed it at the Microsoft Research Faculty Summit in 2013: "We were just ahead of our time, like most of our mistakes."

Who owns Microsoft Bob today?

Microsoft still owns Microsoft Bob and has done nothing with it since 1996. The code was never open sourced, never bundled into a Windows anniversary release, and never sold to another company. There is no legal storefront where a copy can be bought in 2026, and no version that installs on a modern Windows machine without an emulator or a virtual machine running Windows 3.1 or Windows 95.

The name went in a different direction. Microsoft owned the bob.com domain, and in 1999 traded it to Bob Kerstein of Falls Church, Virginia, in exchange for windows2000.com, which Kerstein had registered in 1996. Kerstein kept bob.com and still uses it for his collectible stock and bond certificate business, which means the most valuable asset left from the project belongs to someone who had nothing to do with it.

Where to find Microsoft Bob today

The free route is the Internet Archive, which hosts several preserved Microsoft Bob releases, including the OEM CD-ROM edition, as downloadable disk images at no cost. Collectors who want the physical object look for the 1995 boxed release or the jewel-case CD from independent vintage-software sellers, and Etsy listings for Microsoft Bob software are the practical place to watch, since complete boxes surface only occasionally and prices are set listing by listing.

How did Comic Sans and Clippy come out of Microsoft Bob?

Microsoft designer Vincent Connare saw a build of Bob in 1994 and objected to Rover speaking in Times New Roman. "Dogs don't talk in Times New Roman!" he told The Guardian in 2017. He drew a set of comic-book letters with a mouse to make the point, and that sketch became Comic Sans. The font missed Bob's deadline and went out to the world with Windows 95 instead, which is how a cartoon dog produced the most recognized typeface in computing.

Rover outlived the house too. Microsoft brought the dog back in Windows XP in 2001 as the animated Search Companion, and retired him when the feature was dropped in Windows Vista in 2007. The broader idea, an animated helper layered on top of a normal interface rather than replacing it, shipped in Office 97 as the Office Assistant and gave the world Clippy the paperclip. Clippy was mocked for a quarter century, but it rode Office to tens of millions of desktops, which is the audience Bob never reached.

Why was Microsoft Bob hidden on Windows XP install discs?

Bob got one final job, and almost nobody knew about it until 2008. Microsoft wanted about 30 MB of dummy data on Windows XP install CDs so the disc image would be bulkier and harder to pirate, and the engineer handling it needed a file that looked like noise. He took the Microsoft Bob floppy disk images, combined them, encrypted the result with a key produced by mashing the keyboard, and shipped the ciphertext as ballast on millions of discs.

Raymond Chen told the story in Microsoft's TechNet Magazine in 2008 with a line that has followed the product ever since: "Bob was actually more useful dead than alive." In 2020 retired Microsoft engineer Dave Plummer identified himself to The Register as the person who did it. Every Windows XP CD pressed in that era carried an encrypted copy of Microsoft's most famous flop, and no user could ever open it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Microsoft Bob still available in 2026?

Microsoft Bob is not sold or supported anywhere in 2026. Microsoft discontinued it in early 1996 and has never reissued it. The only way to run Microsoft Bob today is to download one of the preserved disk images from the Internet Archive and start it inside an emulator or a virtual machine running Windows 3.1 or Windows 95.

Why did Microsoft Bob fail?

Microsoft Bob failed because its cartoon-house interface made every task slower than the standard Windows desktop, because it demanded 8 MB of RAM when many 1995 home PCs shipped with 4 MB, and because Windows 95 arrived in August 1995 with a friendlier desktop that removed the problem Bob was built to solve. Retail tracker PC Data counted about 58,000 copies sold before Microsoft discontinued it in early 1996.

Did Comic Sans come from Microsoft Bob?

Comic Sans was drawn in 1994 for Microsoft Bob, after designer Vincent Connare objected to the cartoon dog Rover speaking in Times New Roman. The font was not finished in time to ship with Microsoft Bob in March 1995 and went out with Windows 95 instead, which is how it reached hundreds of millions of computers.
