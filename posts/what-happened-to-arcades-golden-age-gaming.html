Are Arcades Still Around in 2026? 7,100 Are Left

By Marcus Vale · Updated November 7, 2026 · First published April 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Arcades are still around in 2026, but almost entirely as food and entertainment venues rather than rooms full of cabinets. IBISWorld counts about 7,100 US arcade, food and entertainment complex businesses on roughly $6.0 billion in revenue in 2026, and the largest of them is Dave & Buster's, which reported 184 Dave & Buster's stores in 43 states, Puerto Rico and Canada in August 2026. The pure coin-op arcade peaked in 1982, when US machines took an estimated $8 billion in quarters, more than pop music and Hollywood films combined.

How big did arcades get in 1982?

Taito put Space Invaders in Japanese cabinets in 1978, and within four years coin-op video games were the largest entertainment business in the United States. Histories of the golden age of arcade video games put 1982 arcade takings at about $8 billion in quarters, against roughly $4 billion for pop music and $3 billion for Hollywood films that year. Arcades outearned both combined.

The machines were everywhere. Time counted more than 13,000 arcades in the United States in January 1982, and once the bars, laundromats, pizza places and bowling alleys are added, an estimated 1.5 million coin-op machines were on location by the end of that year.

Pac-Man did most of the damage to allowances. Namco released it in 1980 and Midway began distributing it in North America that October. About 100,000 cabinets sold in the first year and took more than $1 billion in quarters. The decline started almost the moment the peak was reached.

What actually killed the arcade business?

The collapse began at the peak rather than decades later. US arcade revenue fell from about $8 billion in 1982 to roughly $5 billion in 1983 and bottomed near $4 billion in 1984, a drop of about 30 percent in a single year followed by another leg down.

Home hardware finished the job. The Nintendo Entertainment System reached North America in 1985 and proved that home gaming was not a cheap imitation of the arcade. Super Mario Bros. and The Legend of Zelda had no cabinet equivalent, and once the console was paid for they cost nothing per play.

The industry had also overbuilt. Single malls carried competing arcades, cabinets got more expensive to buy and to keep running, and earnings per machine dropped as the novelty wore off. Games were tuned to end quickly rather than to be finished, a design that looked reasonable when there was no alternative and hostile the moment there was one.

Why did Street Fighter II bring players back?

Capcom put Street Fighter II in arcades in 1991, and it stands as one of the three highest-grossing video games ever released, alongside Space Invaders and Pac-Man. Across all its versions the game has sold more than 200,000 arcade cabinets and over 15 million software units worldwide, for estimated gross revenue above $10 billion. By 1994 an estimated 25 million people in the United States had played it.

The second joystick is what made it work. High-score games were solitary. Street Fighter II was public competition, judged by whoever stood behind the cabinet. Players traveled between arcades looking for better opponents, tournaments formed, and the first recognizable competitive gaming scene grew out of the line of quarters along the cabinet edge.

It extended the arcade's life without reversing the decline. The revival concentrated in cities that already had arcade communities and skewed heavily toward young men, a far narrower audience than the families who filled the rooms in 1982.

Who runs the arcades that are left in 2026?

Arcades did not vanish. They changed owners and business models. IBISWorld counts about 7,100 businesses in the US arcade, food and entertainment complex industry in 2026, on revenue of roughly $6.0 billion. The number of businesses has grown about 3.1 percent a year since 2021, while revenue slipped 1.0 percent in 2026.

Almost none of that is a room full of cabinets. The largest operator is Dave & Buster's, which reported 184 Dave & Buster's stores in 43 states, Puerto Rico and Canada for the quarter ended August 4, 2026, alongside its Main Event bowling and games brand. That quarter brought revenue of $544.1 million, down 2.4 percent, and comparable store sales down 2.9 percent. The company told investors it had seen "continued improvement in overall same store sales during the third quarter to date," which is the careful version of a business trying to stop a slide.

The economics are now food and drink with games attached. Chuck E. Cheese has run on that arithmetic since the 1970s: the games bring the family in, the pizza pays the bill. Round1, the Japanese operator, keeps filling dead mall anchor space with bowling lanes, crane machines and imported Japanese cabinets.

Where can you still play a wall of cabinets?

The pure arcade survives at the margins, and the bar format carries most of it. Barcade opened in a former metal shop on Union Avenue in Williamsburg, Brooklyn in 2004, pairing craft beer with cabinets on free play, and it now runs eight other locations, three of them in Manhattan.

Even that model is not safe. In late July 2026 the owners gave notice that the Williamsburg lease ends in under 1,000 days. "This neighborhood has changed so much, as far as nightlife goes, that it financially just doesn't make a lot of sense," co-founder and chief executive Paul Kermizian told News 12 Brooklyn in 2026, adding that an extension was possible but that he did not expect one. The bar that proved beer and Donkey Kong could cover a Brooklyn rent is leaving because the rent won.

For a full wall of machines, Galloping Ghost Arcade in Brookfield, Illinois charges one all-day admission with every game set to free play, and Funspot in Laconia, New Hampshire carries the Guinness World Records title for the largest arcade in the world.

Where to find one today: original uprights move mostly through specialist dealers and collector shows rather than general marketplaces, and the cheaper way in is a piece of the real thing. Marquees, control panel overlays, instruction cards and reproduction side art turn up on Etsy. If what you want is the games rather than the furniture, emulation costs a fraction of a cabinet, and the best ways to play Sega Genesis games today need no quarters at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are arcades still around in 2026?

Yes. Arcades still operate across the United States in 2026, though mostly as food and entertainment venues rather than rooms of cabinets. IBISWorld counts about 7,100 US arcade, food and entertainment complex businesses on roughly $6.0 billion in revenue in 2026, and the largest operator, Dave & Buster's, reported 184 Dave & Buster's stores in 43 states, Puerto Rico and Canada in August 2026.

How many arcades are left in the United States?

IBISWorld puts the US arcade, food and entertainment complex industry at about 7,100 businesses in 2026, a count growing around 3.1 percent a year since 2021. That figure covers bowling and games complexes and family entertainment centers rather than coin-op arcades alone, which is why it is larger than the number of traditional arcades, a group that has shrunk steadily since the 13,000 US arcades Time counted in January 1982.

Why did arcades die out?

Arcades collapsed because the business peaked and home consoles arrived at the same time. US arcade revenue fell from about $8 billion in 1982 to roughly $5 billion in 1983 and bottomed near $4 billion in 1984, and the Nintendo Entertainment System's 1985 North American launch gave players games no arcade had, at no cost per play.
