Who Owns WorldCom Now? Verizon and MCI in 2026

2026-10-16 | Updated 2026-11-05 | By Dana Reyes | 5 min read

WorldCom no longer exists as a company. It filed for Chapter 11 protection on July 21, 2002 with $103.9 billion in assets after an accounting fraud the courts put at $11 billion, then reorganized under the MCI name. Verizon Communications closed its purchase of MCI on January 6, 2006, and in 2026 MCI Communications Services still files with regulators as a Verizon Business entity rather than selling anything to consumers.

What was the WorldCom accounting fraud?

WorldCom sold long distance. It bought wholesale network capacity from other carriers, resold calls below the prices of the former Bell companies, and spent the 1990s buying rivals, including MCI Communications, with its own rising stock. The deals of those years are mostly remembered now for their size, which is also the fate of AOL Time Warner.

The payments to those other carriers were where the fraud sat. "Line costs are what WorldCom pays other companies for using their communications networks," the Congressional Research Service wrote in its 2002 report on the scandal, and they were an operating expense that hit earnings every quarter.

The finance department moved large amounts of them onto the balance sheet as capital spending instead, the treatment a company uses when it buys a building. On June 25, 2002 WorldCom admitted it had classified over $3.8 billion in line-cost payments as capital expenditures. Booked that way, the Congressional Research Service noted, the expense could be pushed into the future, "perhaps for 10 years or even longer."

WorldCom's own internal auditor, Cynthia Cooper, pulled the thread in 2002, not the outside firm that signed off on the accounts. The revenue was real and the profit was manufactured, and by the time the courts were finished the overstatement stood at $11 billion.

How big was the Chapter 11 filing in July 2002?

WorldCom filed for Chapter 11 protection on July 21, 2002 with $103.9 billion in assets, the figure BankruptcyData compiled for CNBC's ranking of the largest bankruptcies in United States history.

Bankruptcy league tables measure what a company says it owns rather than what it owes, which is why that number dwarfs the accounting hole that caused the filing. On the asset measure, WorldCom sat at the top of the American list until the failures of 2008.

Chapter 11 is a reorganization, not a shutdown, and almost nothing visible changed for customers. The fiber kept carrying traffic, the switches kept routing calls, and employees kept showing up while lawyers converted debt into equity.

Shareholders got nothing like their money back. Creditors are paid first in a reorganization, and the common stock that millions of retirement accounts held through the 1990s was wiped out.

What did change was the name. The company came out of court under the MCI name rather than WorldCom's, because the brand attached to the fraud was worth less than nothing to the enterprise customers who had to defend their vendor choice to a board.

Why did Bernard Ebbers get 25 years?

Bernard Ebbers, the chief executive who assembled WorldCom out of acquisitions, was convicted in March 2005 of conspiracy, securities fraud and filing false reports with regulators.

Judge Barbara Jones of the United States District Court in Manhattan sentenced him on July 13, 2005. Ebbers was 63, and reporters in the room treated the term as a life sentence rather than a number of years.

Prosecutors had asked for more. Jones said her reading of the federal sentencing guidelines pointed to a range from 30 years to life, then concluded that "30 years would be excessive" and settled on 25, as the Las Vegas Sun reported in 2005.

The defense had argued his health and his record of charitable giving in Mississippi. The judge weighed both and cut the term below the guideline range without accepting that either excused the fraud.

His testimony had been the gamble of the trial. Ebbers told the jury he had left the numbers to his finance chief and did not understand accounting, a defense the jury rejected in a case where the finance chief had already pleaded guilty and testified for the government.

How did Verizon end up owning the MCI name?

Verizon Communications bought the reorganized company. Verizon and MCI closed their merger on January 6, 2006, a transaction Verizon valued at about $8.5 billion in the 2005 annual report it filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

Verizon did not keep MCI as a consumer brand. It put the former MCI operations serving companies and government agencies into a new unit called Verizon Business, and sold everything else under the Verizon name.

That left MCI where corporate names usually end up after a merger: on the filings the operating entities still have to make with regulators, rather than on anything a customer buys.

So the ownership answer has two parts in 2026. WorldCom has not been the name of an operating company since the Chapter 11 reorganization, and MCI belongs to Verizon, which has run the business as Verizon Business since January 2006.

Where does the MCI name still show up in 2026?

In regulatory dockets. On June 23, 2026 the Florida Public Service Commission logged document 03694-2026, a filing from MCI Communications Services LLC, doing business as Verizon Business Services.

It is an application under Section 214 of the Communications Act and Section 63.71 of the Federal Communications Commission's rules for permission to stop selling four legacy business services: ISDN Primary Rate Interface, Full T1, Digital PBX Trunks and Virtual Foreign Exchange. The area covered runs across the contiguous 48 states and the District of Columbia.

Those are the products that tied an office phone system to the network before voice moved to IP. Twenty years after the merger, the MCI entity is still filing paperwork, and what it is filing is the retirement of the circuit-switched catalog it was built on.

What a consumer will not find is an MCI bill. The name survives as a filer, not as a product. CompuServe ended up in the same condition, a name that outlived its own service, and anyone who followed Nextel knows the order these things go in: the advertising stops first, the network goes next, and the filings outlast both.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happened to WorldCom?

WorldCom collapsed in 2002 after an accounting fraud the courts put at $11 billion, and it filed for Chapter 11 protection on July 21, 2002 with $103.9 billion in assets. It reorganized under the MCI name instead of its own, and Verizon Communications closed its purchase of MCI on January 6, 2006. No operating company has carried the WorldCom name since.

Who owns MCI now?

Verizon Communications owns MCI, and has since the two companies closed their merger on January 6, 2006, a deal Verizon valued at about $8.5 billion. Verizon folded the former MCI business into a unit called Verizon Business. In a filing the Florida Public Service Commission logged on June 23, 2026, the entity still appears as MCI Communications Services LLC, doing business as Verizon Business Services.

How much was the WorldCom fraud worth?

The WorldCom fraud reached $11 billion in overstated results. It began with the company's admission on June 25, 2002 that it had booked over $3.8 billion of line costs as capital spending, and it ended with chief executive Bernard Ebbers sentenced to 25 years in prison on July 13, 2005 by Judge Barbara Jones in Manhattan.
