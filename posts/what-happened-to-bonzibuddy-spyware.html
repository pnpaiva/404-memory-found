Is BonziBuddy Still Around? Gone From PCs in 2004

2026-04-08 | Updated 2026-09-21 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

BonziBuddy is not around anymore. Bonzi Software discontinued the purple gorilla assistant in 2004, after a class action over its fake Windows alerts and a $75,000 Federal Trade Commission penalty for collecting children's data without parental consent. Joe Bonzi and Jay Bonzi still hold the name, and bonzi.com has pointed at a BonziBUDDY NFT page since February 2022.

What was BonziBuddy and what did it actually do?

BonziBuddy was a free desktop assistant released in 1999 by Bonzi Software Inc., a California company run by two brothers, Joe Bonzi and Jay Bonzi. It lived on the Windows desktop, told jokes, ran web searches, read email aloud and offered shopping tips, all in a synthesized voice.

The program was built on Microsoft Agent, the same animated-character framework behind Clippy in Microsoft Office. The first release borrowed Peedy, a green parrot from Microsoft's stock character set. In May 2000 Bonzi Software swapped in a character of its own: a purple gorilla named Bonzi.

The Federal Trade Commission described the product plainly in its February 2004 complaint, calling BonziBUDDY "intelligent agent software that displays an interactive, animated purple gorilla on the desktop of users' computers." The basic version, the complaint noted, was free.

Free was the whole business model. In 1999 there was no common vocabulary for software that earned its keep from your attention and your browsing history, so the gorilla arrived before anyone had built defenses against it.

Why did security researchers call BonziBuddy spyware?

Because of what it installed and changed without a clear ask. BonziBuddy reset the browser homepage to bonzi.com, added an Internet Explorer toolbar, tracked what users did online and served its own advertising. Taking it off a machine was harder than putting it on.

In 2002 Consumer Reports Web Watch labeled BonziBuddy spyware, one of the first mainstream consumer verdicts on a category that barely had a name yet. Antivirus vendors followed, and the classification stuck.

It still holds in 2026. Microsoft's malware encyclopedia lists the program as "Adware:Win32/BonziBUDDY", and Microsoft Defender removes it on sight. That is a rare thing: a 1999 consumer product with a live threat-database entry more than two decades later.

The defense at the time was the license agreement, which disclosed some of the data collection. Almost nobody read it. The gap between what a user thought they had agreed to and what the agreement actually permitted is the whole story of desktop adware in the early 2000s, and BonziBuddy sat at the center of it, alongside file-sharing clients like Kazaa.

What did the fake Windows alerts cost Bonzi Software?

Bonzi Software bought banner space across the web and styled the ads to look like Windows system dialogs. They ran headers such as "security alert" or "warning", told visitors their IP address was being broadcast, and used close buttons that did not close anything.

A class action was filed against the company on December 4, 2002. ClickZ reported in 2003 that the suit accused Bonzi of a "diabolical scheme" that tricked internet users into clicking through to its site.

The case settled on May 27, 2003. Under the terms, Bonzi Software agreed to put the word "Advertisement" in the header of any online ad that resembled a computer system warning and on the pages those ads led to, and to stop using fake close buttons. ClickZ reported that the company paid the plaintiffs' firm, Lukins & Annis, $170,000 in legal fees, made no damages payment, and settled before the class was certified.

The remedy, in other words, was a label. That was roughly the ceiling of consumer protection for deceptive web advertising in 2003.

Why did the FTC fine Bonzi Software $75,000?

Children. The BonziBUDDY registration form asked for a birth date along with other personal details, and the complaint filed for the FTC on February 17, 2004 in the Central District of California said Bonzi Software had actual knowledge that thousands of children under 13 were registering. Under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, in force since April 2000, that triggered a duty to give parents direct notice and obtain verifiable consent. Bonzi Software did neither.

The company agreed to pay $75,000 in civil penalties. The same FTC announcement carried a $400,000 penalty against UMG Recordings, at that point the largest COPPA civil penalty on record.

The Bonzi case mattered well beyond the money. The FTC called it "the first COPPA case to challenge the information collection practices of an online service in connection with a software product." Enforcement until then had been aimed at websites and their registration forms. The purple gorilla extended the statute to anything you downloaded.

A second action followed that year. On September 1, 2004 Bonzi Software and its two principals settled FTC charges over InternetALERT, a security product sold at $49 for a one-year subscription and advertised as cutting the risk of internet attacks. The FTC said the software "provides only limited protections for computers and the information stored in them," and the consent order required the company to notify subscribers and offer prorated refunds.

Who owns BonziBuddy today?

Joe Bonzi and Jay Bonzi still control the name. Bonzi Software stopped distributing BonziBuddy in 2004, the download lingered on bonzi.com into 2005, and the portal itself went dark later that decade. The brothers moved on to 2KDirect, a San Luis Obispo advertising company that sells online campaigns to small businesses and is still trading in 2026.

The domain came back in February 2022, pointed at an OpenSea page selling BonziBUDDY NFTs credited to the original creators. That is the closest thing to an official BonziBuddy product now. There is no supported download, no company selling the assistant and no vendor to complain to.

What circulates instead is fan work: mirrors of the final 2004 build, a browser chat site called BonziWORLD that volunteers have rebuilt several times, and repackaged "safe" builds on itch.io with the advertising and tracking code stripped out. The original servers have been offline for about two decades, so the program cannot phone home. It is still adware by every antivirus definition, and Microsoft Defender will still quarantine it.

For anyone who wants the character without the risk, a video or one of the browser recreations is the sane route in 2026, the same way people revisit Microsoft Bob without installing it.

What did the BonziBuddy cases change?

Three things, and only one of them was about a gorilla.

First, after February 2004 the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act was no longer a website rule. Any downloadable program that asked a child for a birth date and an email address sat inside the statute, which is why later enforcement reached games and mobile apps rather than sign-up pages.

Second, the September 2004 InternetALERT order put marketers on notice that a security promise is an advertising claim, and that the FTC will ask what the software actually blocks.

Third, the 2003 settlement made an "Advertisement" label the baseline for anything dressed up as a system message. Dark patterns did not disappear. Cookie walls, buried cancellation flows and notification prompts styled like alerts are all descendants. What became legally expensive was the specific trick of a banner pretending to be Windows telling you that you were under attack.

The lasting part is the question BonziBuddy forced into the open and nobody has answered. What does consent mean when the user and the software maker know very different things about what the software is doing? In 1999 the answer was a license agreement nobody read. In 2026 it is a privacy policy nobody reads.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is BonziBuddy a virus?

BonziBuddy is not a virus, because it did not copy itself from machine to machine. It is classified as adware and spyware: it changed the browser homepage to bonzi.com, installed a toolbar, tracked browsing and served ads. Microsoft's malware encyclopedia still lists it as Adware:Win32/BonziBUDDY, and Microsoft Defender removes it in 2026.

Can you still download BonziBuddy in 2026?

There is no official BonziBuddy download in 2026. Bonzi Software discontinued the program in 2004 and the bonzi.com download disappeared in 2005. Copies of the final build circulate on fan mirrors and repackaged versions exist on itch.io, but antivirus software flags the original as adware, so most people use browser recreations such as BonziWORLD instead.

Who made BonziBuddy?

BonziBuddy was made by brothers Joe Bonzi and Jay Bonzi through their California company, Bonzi Software Inc., which released the assistant in 1999 and gave it the purple gorilla character in May 2000. After the 2003 advertising settlement and the 2004 FTC actions, the brothers moved on to 2KDirect, a San Luis Obispo online advertising firm.
