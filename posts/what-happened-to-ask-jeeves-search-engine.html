Does Ask Jeeves Still Exist? What Ask.com Shows Today

By Theo Lindqvist · Updated November 21, 2026 · First published March 27, 2026 · 10 min read

Ask Jeeves no longer exists as a search engine. IAC, which bought the company for $1.85 billion in 2005, closed Ask.com on May 1, 2026, twenty years after it retired the Jeeves butler in February 2006 and sixteen years after it stopped building its own search technology in 2010. The ask.com and askjeeves.com addresses now show only a farewell note.

What does Ask.com show in November 2026?

A goodbye note and nothing else. On November 21, 2026, both ask.com and the old askjeeves.com address load the same single page, which opens with the line "Every great search must come to an end." There is no search box, and old results links such as ask.com/web return a "page not found" error.

The note gives IAC's reason in one sentence: "As IAC continues to sharpen its focus, we have made the decision to discontinue our search business, which includes Ask.com." It signs off with "Jeeves' spirit endures," and the footer still reads IAC Inc. The same page now loads at Reference.com and ConsumerSearch.com, two other Ask Media Group sites listed in IAC's 2025 annual report.

The note has also been quietly edited. When TechCrunch quoted it on May 2, 2026, it said Ask.com had closed "After 25 years of answering the world's questions." The live version now says 30 years. Neither number is exact: Ask Jeeves told investors in 1999 that the site launched in April 1997, which makes the run just over 29 years.

The retreat did not stop at Ask.com. Excite, which Ask Jeeves bought in 2004, was shut down by IAC later in 2026. And the company named on the farewell page no longer goes by that name.

Who owns the Ask Jeeves name now?

IAC does, under a new name. On June 4, 2026, five weeks after Ask.com closed, IAC renamed itself People Incorporated and began trading on Nasdaq as PPLI. Its own description of the business is the publisher People Inc., home of PEOPLE, Food & Wine and Travel + Leisure, plus a minority stake in MGM Resorts International. Search is not in it.

The butler's name has not been let go, though. An application to register ASK JEEVES as a trademark, filed on January 11, 2024 by Ask Media Group LLC, the Oakland, California, unit that ran Ask.com, was still live at the US Patent and Trademark Office on November 21, 2026.

The file kept moving after the shutdown. The examiner sent a non-final office action on July 28, 2026, and on October 28, 2026 the applicant asked for and received more time to respond. The application covers search engine services and online advertising for others. No plan for the name has been announced, and the farewell page does not mention one.

How a $1.85 billion search company shrank to a trademark file and a goodbye note is easiest to see with the dates in a row.

Two owners in 30 years: the company itself, public from 1999, and IAC, which held the brand for the last 21. The odd part is the second row, because in 1997 the thing people typed into was a question box, not a keyword box.

How did Ask Jeeves answer a question in 1997?

With templates and a team of editors. Rival engines in 1997 wanted keywords joined by words like "and" and "or." Ask Jeeves let people type a whole sentence, such as CNNfn's 1999 example, "How do I apply for a student loan?"

The software broke the sentence down by grammar and meaning, then matched it to a stored master question. The company's 1999 prospectus put it plainly: "A question template is a master question to which many natural language questions are matched." Editors read the incoming questions and kept adding templates and answers, so the system worked best on questions it had already seen.

The butler did the selling. The inside cover of that prospectus read "Introducing Jeeves, the world's first Internet butler," and the name came from the valet in P.G. Wodehouse's novels. It worked: Ask Jeeves went from about 3,000 questions a day in its first month to nearly 1 million a day in April 1999, and from 425,000 users in September 1998 to more than 1.9 million in March 1999.

Some rivals paid to borrow it. AltaVista, which the prospectus named as a competitor, licensed the Ask Jeeves knowledge base along with Netscape, and accounted for about 14 percent of 1998 revenue. The company still lost $108,000 in 1996, $448,000 in 1997 and $4.3 million in 1998. Wall Street did not care.

Wall Street's 364 percent day, and the Teoma bet

On July 1, 1999, Ask Jeeves sold 3,000,000 shares at $14 each, raising $42 million before fees. CNNfn reported that the stock closed its first day at 64 15/16, up 364 percent, giving the company a market value of about $1.6 billion after one session.

The template system had a ceiling, though: it answered well only when editors had seen a question coming. In September 2001 Ask Jeeves bought Teoma, a search engine with roots at Rutgers University and offices in Piscataway, New Jersey, for a sum it did not disclose. Teoma, a name InternetNews traced to the Gaelic word for "expert," ranked pages by how many pages on the same subject linked to them. That was much closer to Google's link counting than to the butler's scripts.

In 2004 Ask Jeeves went shopping. On March 4 it agreed to buy Interactive Search Holdings, owner of Excite, iWon and My Way, for $343 million, a deal chief executive Steve Berkowitz said would "double our market share."

Most of the money, even then, came from Google. Nearly three-fourths of Ask Jeeves's 2004 revenue came from a paid-search advertising deal with Google that ran through 2007, Reuters reported the following year. That was the company Barry Diller decided to buy: the number five search network, with Google paying most of its bills.

Why did IAC pay $1.85 billion, then retire the butler?

Diller's IAC announced the deal on March 21, 2005: $1.85 billion in stock, or $28.24 per Ask Jeeves share, a 17 percent premium. "Global search is the gateway to everything," Diller said that day, according to Reuters. By comScore's count Ask Jeeves ranked a distant fifth in search. The deal closed on July 19, 2005. The Associated Press later gave the price as $2.3 billion; $1.85 billion is the figure announced with the deal.

Diller decided to drop Jeeves within a few weeks of closing, the AP reported, and on February 27, 2006 the site relaunched as Ask.com without him. Inside the company the butler had come to stand for an old promise of simple answers to conversational questions that never worked the way its engineers had hoped. "If some people are upset about the butler going away, so be it," chief executive Steve Berkowitz told the AP that day.

Share had crept from 5.3 percent of US searches at the end of 2004 to 6.3 percent in December 2005 by comScore's count, against 40 percent for Google. Jim Lanzone, Ask.com's senior vice president of search at the 2006 relaunch, later became chief executive of Yahoo.

Jeeves came back once. Ask quietly restored him on its UK site in November 2008 and announced it on April 20, 2009, citing "user demand" and research in which 83 percent of people surveyed remembered Jeeves while 72 percent knew what Ask.com was, The Register reported. In the US, the next change was to the engine itself.

The day Ask stopped building its own search engine: November 9, 2010

On November 9, 2010, Ask.com said it would stop developing its own search technology and concentrate on answering questions. It cut 130 engineering jobs in Edison, New Jersey, and Hangzhou, China, and asked 20 of the Edison staff to move to Oakland. The New Jersey cuts landed in the state Teoma had come from nine years earlier. Web results would come from an outside provider it did not name.

By PCWorld's estimate at the time, Ask had about 2 percent of US searches, while Google had about 65 percent. Diller, who in 2005 had said "A group of four or five players is going to be able to thrive in this market," now put it in one line in an email to Bloomberg: "We've realized in the last few years you can't compete head on with Google."

After that Ask.com was a question-and-answer and content site running on someone else's index. By 2025 IAC described it as "a search site with a variety of fresh and contemporary content," with sections on celebrities, travel and general knowledge. What paid for it was, once again, Google.

The Google contract that paid for Ask.com, then ended it

Under a services agreement with Google dated October 26, 2015, Google supplied most of the paid listings on Ask Media Group's pages, and IAC kept a share of the ad money. By the end, that one contract was the business. IAC's Search segment covered the Ask Media Group sites and a shrinking desktop app business, and its annual report for 2025 shows how fast it fell.

The whole Search segment made $10.2 million in operating income that year, down from $17.4 million in 2024.

Then the contract went. On December 10, 2025, Google sent a notice of non-renewal that cancelled the automatic one-year extension that would have run from April 1, 2026 to March 31, 2027. The annual report stated that the agreement "expires by its terms on March 31, 2026," and said talks on new terms had an uncertain outcome. Ask.com closed 31 days after that date.

Typing a full question in plain English and getting an answer back is how millions of people now use AI chatbots. Ask Jeeves sold that idea in 1997 with editors and templates, never owned the index that would have made it pay, and spent its last decade living on a revenue share from the company that did.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Ask Jeeves still exist?

Ask Jeeves no longer exists as a working search engine. In November 2026 both ask.com and askjeeves.com load only IAC's farewell note, with no search box, and old search links return an error. The name survives on paper: IAC's Ask Media Group has a pending application to register ASK JEEVES as a US trademark, filed on January 11, 2024 and still open after the shutdown.

Who owns Ask Jeeves now?

Ask Jeeves belongs to IAC, which bought it for $1.85 billion in a deal completed on July 19, 2005, and which renamed itself People Incorporated on June 4, 2026, trading on Nasdaq as PPLI. People Incorporated's business is now the publisher People Inc. and a minority stake in MGM Resorts International, with no search business left.

Why did Ask Jeeves fail?

Ask Jeeves never built a search business big enough to pay for its own index. comScore had it at 6.3 percent of US searches in December 2005 against 40 percent for Google, and it gave up its own search technology on November 9, 2010. For its last decade Ask.com lived on a Google advertising contract that produced 99 percent of IAC's search revenue in 2025, and it closed on May 1, 2026, a month after that contract expired.
