Why the Sega Nomad Failed: Six AA Batteries, $180

2026-09-22 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

The Sega Nomad was Sega's portable Genesis, released in North America in October 1995 at $180 and discontinued in 1999 after about 1 million units. It failed because six AA batteries lasted only a few hours, the price was double what a Genesis cost, and Sega was already pushing the Saturn. It was the last handheld Sega ever made, and working units now sell for hundreds of dollars.

What exactly was the Sega Nomad?

Sega built the Nomad out of a machine that was never meant for stores. The Mega Jet, sold in Japan from March 10, 1994, was a Mega Drive squeezed into a controller body with no screen of its own, handed to passengers on Japan Air Lines flights so they could plug into the display built into the seat.

Sega of America took that idea, added a 3.25-inch backlit color LCD and a battery compartment, and shipped it in North America in October 1995 as the Sega Nomad. Sega's own materials also called it the Genesis Nomad.

The hardware was a real Genesis, not a cut-down imitation. A Motorola 68000 ran the same code, the screen showed the same 320 by 224 NTSC picture, and any cartridge from a library of more than 500 Genesis games slotted into the top. An A/V output let the Nomad drive a television, and a second controller port on the bottom turned it back into a console when it reached the living room.

The price was $180, about $390 in 2026 dollars. A Genesis on its own sold for $99 at the time, so going portable doubled the cost of the same games.

Tom Kalinske, then president and CEO of Sega of America, put it this way in the 1995 announcement: "Millions of die-hard Genesis fans will now have the convenience of their favorite 16-bit games in the palm of their hands anytime or anyplace."

How long did six AA batteries actually last?

The Sega Nomad ran on six AA batteries, and the sources do not agree on how long they lasted. Wikipedia's entry gives about four hours. Reviewers and retro hardware sites put the real figure at two to three hours, depending on screen brightness and volume, and note that the Nomad drew close to twice the power of the Sega Game Gear, itself notorious for draining cells.

Either number was brutal in 1995. Six alkalines cost real money every few hours of play, and a cross-country flight could burn through two or three sets.

Sega sold a rechargeable battery pack separately for $79, which cost nearly as much as a Genesis console and ran for less time than a fresh set of alkalines. Standard rechargeable AAs were not a fix either, because nickel-cadmium cells supply 1.2 volts where the Nomad expected 1.5.

The screen added its own problem. Mid-1990s reviewers complained about motion blur during fast scrolling, the same flaw that dogged the Game Gear, though the Nomad's larger backlit panel handled it better. The machine meant to free the Genesis from the television worked best plugged into a wall.

Was Sega selling too many machines at once in 1995?

The Nomad arrived at the worst possible moment in Sega's history. In 1995 the company was supporting the Saturn, the Genesis, the Game Gear, the Pico and the Master System, plus the Sega CD and the 32X add-ons. That is five consoles and two bolt-ons competing for the same shelf space, the same marketing dollars and the same developers.

Five months before the Nomad shipped, Sega had already lost the room. At the first E3 on May 11, 1995, Tom Kalinske announced that the Saturn was on sale that day at $399, roughly four months ahead of the September date retailers had been given.

Sony's Steve Race then walked to the microphone, said "two ninety-nine", and walked off. The PlayStation had undercut the Saturn by $100 before a single Saturn game was reviewed.

Kalinske later described the 32X to Sega-16 in 2006 as a machine that "really was designed to be an interim piece and to prolong the life of the 16-bit platform." The Sega Nomad was another bet on that same 16-bit platform, placed in the year Sega's leadership in Japan was winding it down.

Is the Sega Nomad still around in 2026?

No. Sega discontinued the Nomad in 1999 after roughly 1 million units, a number that sounds respectable until you set it beside the tens of millions of Game Boys Nintendo was shifting over the same years.

Blake Snow ranked the Nomad fifth on GamePro's "10 Worst-Selling Handhelds of All Time" in 2007, faulting its timing, its advertising and its battery life. Sega cut the price by $100 during the run, and by 1999 new units were being cleared at around $40, less than a quarter of what they cost at launch.

It was the last handheld Sega ever built. On January 31, 2001, Sega announced that Dreamcast hardware production would end that March and that the company would become a third-party publisher, which closed the door on any Nomad successor.

Nothing has replaced it under the Sega name. There is no Nomad Mini, no official reissue and no Sega handheld of any kind in 2026. Players who want Genesis games on the move use emulation handhelds or modern clone hardware instead, which is a different thing from a real cartridge in a real slot.

Where to find one today

The Sega Nomad is a collector item now, not a bargain bin find. Working handhelds sell in the hundreds rather than the tens: retro sellers have quoted around $300 for a tested unit, and complete boxed sets have been listed near $500. PriceCharting tracks loose, complete and boxed sales if you want to check the trend before you commit.

Condition matters more than usual here. The electrolytic capacitors are three decades old, and a Nomad with dim video, distorted sound or no power at all is usually a recapping job rather than a dead machine. Original screens are dim and blurry even when healthy, and there is no simple drop-in modern panel the way there is for the Game Gear, so IPS upgrades are aftermarket kits fitted by hand.

Budget for the battery problem too. Modern lithium packs replace the six AA compartment and roughly double the runtime. Stone Age Gamer stocks Nomad parts, mods and battery packs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a Sega Nomad worth in 2026?

A working Sega Nomad, the portable Genesis Sega sold in North America from October 1995, generally trades in the hundreds of dollars in 2026. Tested units have been quoted around $300 and complete boxed sets near $500, well above the $180 the handheld cost new in 1995 and the roughly $40 Sega was clearing them for by 1999.

How long does the Sega Nomad battery last?

The Sega Nomad takes six AA batteries and the published figures range from about two hours to about four, depending on the source, the screen brightness and the volume. Sega's own rechargeable pack, sold for $79 after the handheld's October 1995 launch, ran for less time than a fresh set of alkalines, which is why modern lithium replacement packs are the usual fix.

Was the Sega Nomad ever sold outside North America?

No. Sega released the Nomad only in North America, from October 1995 until it was discontinued in 1999. Its Japanese ancestor, the screenless Mega Jet of March 1994, was rented to Japan Air Lines passengers and later sold at retail in Japan, but the battery-powered Nomad with its own 3.25-inch screen never shipped in Japan, Europe or Australia.
