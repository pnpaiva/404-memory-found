Is Clippy Still Around in 2026? Microsoft's Paperclip

2026-04-06 | Updated 2026-09-20 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Clippy, officially named Clippit, has not shipped as a working Microsoft Office feature since Office 2007 stripped out the Office Assistant. The character still exists inside Microsoft software in 2026, as the paperclip emoji in Microsoft 365 since 2021 and as a hidden avatar in the consumer Copilot app since October 2025. It has never come back as an assistant that watches what you type.

Quick facts

Why did Microsoft put a cartoon inside Office 97?

The Office Assistant began with academic research rather than marketing. In the early 1990s the Stanford professors Clifford Nass and Byron Reeves ran experiments showing that people apply social rules to computers without meaning to. Praise from a machine made test subjects rate that machine more favorably. Terse, unhelpful responses made them feel snubbed. They called the effect the media equation and published the book of the same name in 1996.

Microsoft drew a product conclusion from it. If users already treated software as a social actor, the reasoning went, software should have a face. The first attempt was Microsoft Bob, the 1995 release that replaced the Windows desktop with a cartoon house and a guide dog named Rover. Bob was discontinued inside a year.

Alan Cooper, the programmer known as the father of Visual Basic, later argued that the whole concept rested on a "tragic misunderstanding" of the Stanford work. Nass and Reeves had described an unconscious reflex. Microsoft turned it into a specification.

The internal lesson from Bob was not that the premise was wrong but that the scope had been too large. Shrink the character down, put it inside one application instead of the whole desktop, and it might work. That decision produced the Office Assistant, which shipped with Microsoft Office 97 on November 19, 1996.

How did a paperclip beat 250 other characters?

Illustrator Kevan Atteberry was one of the artists hired to draw candidates. "We designed about 250 characters, and I had about 15 or 20 of 'em in there," he told Mental Floss in 2017. The pool ran from animals and robots to household objects and cartoon humans.

The shortlisting was handed to the same discipline that had inspired the project. Social psychologists from Stanford spent six months putting the designs in front of focus groups, asking members of the public which characters felt trustworthy, likeable and funny. The googly-eyed paperclip finished first on trust, engagement and endearment. Some testers in those sessions still flagged the eyes as leering, and the paperclip shipped anyway.

Its official name was Clippit. Almost nobody used it. Within a year of Office 97 reaching desks the character was Clippy to users, to the press and eventually to Microsoft's own advertising.

Atteberry drew the winner on an Apple Macintosh, a detail he has repeated in interviews for years, and which remains the neatest irony in the story.

What made Clippy unbearable once real work started?

Clippy was enabled by default, which is the detail that turned a feature into a grievance. Everyone who installed Office 97 got him whether they wanted him or not. Start typing something that pattern-matched to correspondence and he appeared: "It looks like you're writing a letter. Would you like help?"

The complaint was never that help existed. It was that the help arrived unrequested, animated itself to pull your eye away from the document, and rarely matched the problem you actually had. Dismissing him was temporary. He came back.

The focus groups had measured the wrong thing. A test session lasts minutes, and over minutes the paperclip reads as charming. Office work lasts hours, days and years, and the same twitching character reads as an interruption. The research that picked Clippy captured first impressions, not the fiftieth impression.

By 1999 disabling the Office Assistant had become a standard step in corporate PC setup, the same way turning off BonziBuddy became standard on home machines a couple of years later. Office was the dominant productivity suite on the planet, so hundreds of millions of people had to learn how to switch off a feature Microsoft had spent years researching.

How did Microsoft fire its own mascot in 2001?

On April 11, 2001, Microsoft announced that the Office Assistant would be switched off by default in the next release. Then the company did something stranger than a quiet retirement. It made the firing into the marketing campaign.

At the Office XP launch in New York on May 31, 2001, a performer in a Clippit costume interrupted the presentation, begged for his job back and was hauled off the stage by an oversized magnet. Microsoft put up a promotional site, officeclippy.com, carrying three Flash cartoons about an unemployed paperclip looking for work: "Clippy Gets Clipped", "Clippy Goes Undercover" and "Clippy Faces Facts". There was also a Flash game, X-tract Paperclip. Gilbert Gottfried voiced Clippy for the campaign.

Office XP still contained the Assistant in code, just off unless a user went looking. The final cut came with Office 2007, which removed every Office Assistant character. No version of Microsoft Office since has shipped one.

Where does Clippy still exist in Microsoft software?

Clippy is still around in 2026, but not as anything that helps you write. He survives in two places, both of them decorative.

The first is the emoji set. In July 2021 Microsoft posted a picture of the paperclip with the line, "If this gets 20k likes, we'll replace the paperclip emoji in Microsoft 365 with Clippy." The post cleared 20,000 likes within hours and went on to roughly 175,000, and Microsoft shipped the swap as part of a refresh of about 1,800 emoji across its products. The company later admitted the redesign had already been finished before the vote.

The second is Copilot. In its October 2025 release Microsoft gave the consumer Copilot assistant an animated avatar called Mico, and left Clippy inside it as an easter egg: tap the avatar rapidly, about a dozen times, and it turns into the paperclip. It is a skin over Mico rather than a working assistant, you cannot set it as the default, and it does not appear in Microsoft 365 Copilot, the business version.

Beyond Microsoft, Clippy has had a second life as merchandise and as fan software, which is a better fate than most discontinued features of the Windows 95 era managed.

Why does every AI assistant still get compared to Clippy?

Kevan Atteberry spent years distancing himself from the work. "I would never include Clippy in my portfolio because I was so embarrassed of him," he told The Register in 2023. Ironic nostalgia in the 2010s changed that, and the character that symbolized bad software design became a mascot for retro computing.

The comparison persists because the design problem never went away. Every major software company now ships an assistant that watches your document, anticipates what you want and offers to do it for you. The vocabulary changed from Office Assistant to copilot, and the underlying question from 1996 is unchanged: how does an assistant stay helpful without becoming an interruption?

Clippy answered it by counterexample. Do not turn the assistant on for everyone by default. Do not animate for attention. Do not assume the pattern you matched is the task the user is doing. Modern assistants are better at reading context and much better at staying quiet, and when they get it wrong the insult people reach for is still the name of a paperclip from 1996.

Frequently Asked Questions

When was Clippy removed from Microsoft Office?

Clippy, officially named Clippit, was switched off by default in Office XP after Microsoft announced the change on April 11, 2001, and every Office Assistant character was removed outright in Office 2007. No version of Microsoft Office released since 2007 has shipped the Office Assistant.

Who designed Clippy?

Clippy was drawn by the illustrator Kevan Atteberry, who contributed 15 to 20 of the roughly 250 character designs Microsoft tested for the Office Assistant in Office 97. Atteberry drew the paperclip on an Apple Macintosh, and has said he left Clippy out of his portfolio for years because he was embarrassed by it.

Is Clippy coming back as an AI assistant?

Not as a product. Microsoft's consumer Copilot assistant uses an avatar called Mico, introduced in October 2025, and tapping that avatar repeatedly turns it into an animated Clippy as a hidden easter egg. Clippy has not returned as a working Microsoft Office feature since Office 2007 removed it.
