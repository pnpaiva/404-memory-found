Are Pogs Worth Anything in 2026? What Sets Sell For

2026-10-26 | By Marcus Vale | 6 min read

Pogs are worth very little in 2026. More than 2 billion caps had sold by December 1994, so loose commons carry no premium, and the documented sales sit in complete licensed sets: about $25 for a Star Trek: The Next Generation set, upwards of $69 for Topps Star Wars sets, and $255 for a set of EarthBound pogs in 2009. The POG name itself is active again, bought by Julien Savino in 2019 and making new caps since 2024.

Where the milk cap game came from

The game is older than the fad by about sixty years. Children in Hawaii flipped cardboard milk bottle caps as a playground game in the 1930s, the Seattle Times reported in June 1993, and it faded out when dairies stopped capping glass bottles.

The name arrived later, and it was a juice. Haleakala Dairy on Maui sold a passion fruit, orange and guava drink and printed POG on the caps, which is where the three letters come from, as the Christian Science Monitor explained in December 1994.

What the discs were explains everything that followed. The Seattle Times described them in June 1993 like this: “The poker-chip sized discs are sold, collected, traded, played with like tiddly-winks or used as business cards.” They cost pennies, carried a picture of almost anything, and could be won or lost in a single throw.

A collector market formed before most of the country had heard of the game. That same summer a woman in Shoreline, Washington, known locally as the Pog Lady, ran a basement shop with more than 250 designs spread across her tables, from dinosaurs and snakes to politicians, sports teams and company logos.

How a Hawaii teacher restarted it in 1991

Blossom Galbiso, a teacher at Waialua Elementary School on Oahu, brought the milk cap game back to her playground about two years before the Seattle Times wrote about it, which places the revival in 1991. Her motive was discipline rather than nostalgia: she offered the caps as a nonviolent alternative to sham battle, a rough form of dodgeball her students played at recess.

The timing helped. Haleakala Dairy caps were circulating on Oahu, so the playing pieces were free and already in every kitchen. Children flipped them, kept what they won, and began trading for designs they did not have.

From there the game moved east, reaching the mainland in 1993 with California first. By December 1994 it was a national business with dozens of manufacturers stamping caps, the Christian Science Monitor reported, and prices ran from 9 cents to $2 a cap.

Alan Rypinski, who bought the rights to the POG name from Haleakala Dairy in 1993 and ran the World POG Federation, estimated at the end of 1994 that more than 2 billion caps had sold. His company had also just won a court ruling making POG an exclusive trademark, with millions of dollars at stake in the case.

Why schools banned the game in 1995

The problem was keepsies. Children played for keeps, so a child could arrive with a full tube of caps and walk home with none, and school staff read that as gambling on school property.

By the spring of 1995 the bans were widespread. The Deseret News reported in March 1995 that schools were finding “it's easier to ban pogs ... than spend hours straightening out fights over who won the cap-flipping game,” and it named schools in Windham, New Hampshire, Plano, Texas, and Spokane, Washington, among those that had banned or discouraged the game.

Some schools tried to negotiate before banning. Playing for points instead of for the caps themselves was a common compromise, as was allowing trading at lunch while forbidding the game.

The bans mattered commercially, because a schoolyard game has no other venue. There was no league, no tournament circuit and no reason to keep buying caps once the recess games stopped. The market collapsed within a couple of years, as Fast Company recounted in 2024, and the brand went quiet for roughly two decades. The same arc ran through the toy crazes that followed it, from Tamagotchi to Furby.

What complete sets and slammers actually sell for

The honest answer is almost nothing, with a thin layer of exceptions, and supply is the reason.

More than 2 billion caps had sold by December 1994 on the World POG Federation's own estimate, and dozens of companies were printing them at once. A disc made in those numbers has no scarcity to trade on, and the enormous figures in collector lists are asking prices, not sales.

The documented sales are modest. Complex's survey of rare 1990s pogs records a set of EarthBound pogs that cost about $5 new selling for $255 in 2009. Super Nintendo slammer boards have been offered at upwards of $130, with one selling at $95. Topps Star Wars sets have sold for upwards of $69, and a complete Star Trek: The Next Generation set for about $25.

Value sits in complete licensed sets, metal slammers and printing errors; loose commons do not qualify. It is the same split Beanie Babies collectors learned after their own bubble burst.

Where to find one today: pogs turn up as bulk lots, sealed packs and part-finished sets, and completeness moves the price. A licensed set with its slammer runs roughly $25 to $70 on the documented record. A search for vintage pogs and slammers on Etsy is the place to start.

Who owns the Pog name in 2026

The trademark has changed hands twice. Rypinski bought the POG name from Haleakala Dairy in 1993. After the crash the intellectual property sat mostly dormant until 2019, when Julien Savino, whose father had been the official brand licensee for France, Italy and Spain in the 1990s, acquired it, Fast Company reported in 2024.

The revived World POG Federation went digital before it went physical. POG Digital, cofounded by Kyler Frisbee, launched digital collectibles in June 2022, and the revenue from those paid for new physical production. Frisbee told Fast Company that the digital business scuttled a deal Topps was about to sign for the physical manufacturing rights.

By late 2024 the first new physical sets were in production and reaching buyers. The collector base is small and serious: the artist drawing the new caps, Brent Scotchmer, keeps a personal collection estimated at around 20,000 pieces.

So the name is alive in 2026 and new caps are being made, but as a niche collectible sold direct rather than a cardboard disc stacked by the register at every drugstore. Anyone hoping that a childhood shoebox will pay for a car should read the sales record first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Pogs worth anything in 2026?

Most Pogs are worth very little in 2026, because more than 2 billion caps had already sold by December 1994 and dozens of companies were printing them. The exceptions are complete licensed sets, metal slammers and printing errors: Complex's survey of rare 1990s pogs records a complete Star Trek: The Next Generation set at about $25, Topps Star Wars sets at upwards of $69, and a set of EarthBound pogs that cost about $5 new selling for $255 in 2009.

What were Pogs and how did you play them?

Pogs were cardboard milk bottle caps printed with pictures, named after the passion fruit, orange and guava drink sold by Haleakala Dairy on Maui, whose caps carried the letters POG. Players stacked the caps face down, took turns throwing a heavier disc called a slammer at the stack, and kept every cap that landed face up. The Seattle Times, writing in June 1993, called them poker-chip sized discs that were sold, collected, traded and played with like tiddly-winks.

Who invented Pogs?

Nobody invented Pogs in one go. Children in Hawaii were flipping milk bottle caps in the 1930s, the Seattle Times reported in 1993, and the modern craze began when teacher Blossom Galbiso reintroduced the game at Waialua Elementary School on Oahu in 1991. The commercial version came from Alan Rypinski, who bought the rights to the POG name from Haleakala Dairy in 1993 and ran the World POG Federation.
