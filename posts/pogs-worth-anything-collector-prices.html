Are Pogs Worth Anything in 2026? The Real Prices

By Marcus Vale · Updated November 8, 2026 · First published October 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Pogs are worth very little in 2026. More than 2 billion caps had sold by December 1994, so loose commons carry no premium, and the one well documented sale is a set of EarthBound caps that cost about $5 new and went for $255 at auction in 2009. The POG brand itself still exists, owned by POG Unlimited since Julien Savino acquired it in 2019, and its current Hobby Box sells for $119.99.

Where the milk caps and the POG name came from

The playing pieces were packaging. Haleakala Dairy on Maui printed the letters POG on cardboard bottle caps for its passion fruit, orange and guava juice, which is where the three letters come from, as the Christian Science Monitor explained in December 1994.

Flipping milk bottle caps was a Hawaii playground game long before the 1990s, adapted from the Japanese game menko. Accounts credit a Hawaii schoolteacher, Blossom Galbiso, with bringing it back into her classroom at the start of the decade, and they differ on whether that was 1990 or 1991.

The mechanics explain everything that followed. Players stacked the caps face down, took turns throwing a heavier disc called a slammer at the stack, and kept every cap that landed face up. A cap cost pennies and could carry a picture of anything, from dinosaurs to company logos.

That made the caps a toy and a currency at the same time, which is why a playground game turned into a printing business in about two years.

How the craze reached 2 billion caps

The commercial version started with one buyer. Alan Rypinski bought the rights to the POG name from Haleakala Dairy in 1993 and ran the World POG Federation out of Costa Mesa, California.

By the end of 1994 the game was a national business. The Christian Science Monitor reported in December 1994 that dozens of manufacturers were stamping caps and that prices ran from 9 cents to $2 a cap, depending on the license and the finish.

Rypinski estimated at the end of 1994 that more than 2 billion caps had sold. That figure is a company estimate rather than an audit, and with dozens of printers in the market at once, nobody has a verified total. The direction is what matters: the supply was enormous and most of it survived.

Scale is the whole answer to the value question. Anything made in those quantities and then kept in a shoebox has no scarcity to trade on, which is the same arithmetic that flattened Beanie Babies prices a few years later.

Why schools banned the game in 1995

The problem was keepsies. Children played for keeps, so a child could arrive with a full tube of caps and walk home with none, and school staff read that as gambling on school property.

By the spring of 1995 the bans were widespread. "Schools around the nation are finding it's easier to ban pogs," the Deseret News reported in March 1995, naming schools in Windham, New Hampshire, Plano, Texas, and Spokane, Washington, among those that had banned or discouraged the game.

Some schools tried compromises first. Playing for points instead of for the caps themselves was common, as was allowing trading at lunch while forbidding the game at recess.

The bans mattered commercially, because a schoolyard game has no other venue. There was no league and no tournament circuit, so once the recess games stopped there was no reason to keep buying caps. The legal position had just been settled in the brand's favor and it made little difference: the Deseret News reported that the World POG Federation announced in November 1994 that it had exclusive use of the word pog after a deal with the Universal Pogs Association. The same arc ran through the toy crazes that followed, including Furby.

Who owns the POG brand today?

The trademark has changed hands twice. Rypinski bought the POG name in 1993, and after the crash the intellectual property sat mostly dormant until 2019, when Julien Savino, whose father had been the official brand licensee for France, Italy and Spain in the 1990s, acquired it, Fast Company reported in 2024.

The revived company, POG Unlimited, trades as the World POG Federation and went digital before it went physical. POG Digital sold digital collectibles first, and Fast Company put that digital revenue at about $3 million by 2024. Kyler Frisbee, cofounder and chief executive of POG Digital, told Fast Company in 2024 that "POG Digital is essentially the capital that was able to get the physicals back again."

Frisbee also told Fast Company that Topps had approached his team about buying the physical manufacturing rights, and that a deal was close to signing before the digital business brought in enough money to make it unnecessary.

So the status answer in 2026 is yes, the brand is alive. POG is owned by POG Unlimited, new caps are in production, and they are sold direct to collectors rather than stacked by the register at every drugstore.

What old sets actually sell for

For a childhood shoebox the honest answer is close to nothing. Loose commons from 1994 and 1995 move in bulk lots rather than at single-cap prices, because more than 2 billion caps had sold by December 1994 on the company's own estimate.

The documented exceptions are complete licensed sets, metal slammers and printing errors. Complex's survey of rare 1990s pogs records a set of EarthBound caps, offered through Nintendo Power's Summer 1995 catalog for about $5, selling at online auction for $255 in 2009. That is one sale of one set, not a market price.

Treat the enormous figures in collector guides with care. Most of them are asking prices on open listings rather than completed sales, which is the trap that makes LaserDisc price guides read like a fortune. Condition and completeness do the work: a full numbered set with its slammer sells, a tub of mixed caps does not.

New product also caps the old market from above. The World POG Federation's own store lists the Authentic POG Hobby Box at $119.99 for 12 packs, 96 caps and one KINI slammer, with a 12-box case at $1,200. When the current official product costs that, unsorted 1995 commons have nowhere to go.

Where to find one today: old caps turn up as bulk lots, sealed packs and part-finished sets, and completeness moves the price far more than the design does. A search for vintage pogs and slammers on Etsy is the place to start.
