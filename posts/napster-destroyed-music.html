Did Napster Kill the Music Industry? The 2026 Math

2026-03-17 | Updated 2026-10-22 | By Dana Reyes | 6 min read

Napster did not kill the music industry, but it did end the CD business the industry was built on. US recorded music sales fell from an all-time high of $14.6 billion in 1999, the year Napster launched, to $7.69 billion in 2009. Revenue reached a record $11.5 billion in 2025, with paid subscriptions supplying 55.3% of the total, while the Napster name itself left music streaming in January 2026.

How far did US music revenue actually fall?

US recorded music sales hit an all-time high of $14.6 billion in 1999, the year Napster launched, measured at retail value. A decade later the Recording Industry Association of America's year-end statistics put the total at $7.69 billion, a 12.3% drop from 2008 on its own.

Close to $7 billion a year left a business that had grown almost every year through the 1990s. It did not arrive as one crash. It arrived as ten consecutive losing years, which is harder to fight and much harder to explain to shareholders.

Napster was not the only cause. The CD boom of the 1990s ran partly on listeners rebuying albums they already owned on vinyl and cassette, and that replacement cycle was ending on its own. Broadband, CD burners and cheap hard drives all landed in the same window.

What made the fall structural rather than cyclical was unbundling. A CD sold a dozen songs for one price whether you wanted a dozen or one. Once single tracks could be pulled out of an album, the album stopped being the unit of sale, and the pricing built on it went with it. No court ruling could put that back, because the change was in what listeners expected to buy.

Why did the record labels sue instead of opening a store?

The Recording Industry Association of America sued Napster in December 1999, months after the software appeared. Metallica filed separately in April 2000 and delivered a list of 335,435 Napster user IDs that its lawyers had compiled over a single weekend.

On July 11, 2000, drummer Lars Ulrich took the argument to the Senate Judiciary Committee. "Napster hijacked our music without asking," he testified, as Pollstar reported from the hearing that week. "They never sought our permission."

Ulrich became the public face of the fight, which suited the labels. The quieter argument inside the companies was about margin. A CD at full price funded everything: advances, studios, radio promotion, the retail chains. An authorized download store would have sold the same music for less, and nobody wanted to be the executive who halved revenue per customer.

So they defended the disc. For about five years the most convenient music software in the world was also the illegal kind, and every shutdown produced a replacement: LimeWire, Kazaa, BitTorrent trackers. Suing one network never removed the demand that built it.

What actually shut Napster down in 2001?

A federal court, in stages. In July 2000, US District Judge Marilyn Hall Patel granted the record companies a preliminary injunction ordering Napster to stop the trading of copyrighted recordings on its network. The Ninth Circuit stayed that order, heard the appeal, and on February 12, 2001 largely upheld it in A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc.

The appeals court held that Napster had "knowledge, both actual and constructive, of direct infringement" and could be liable for both contributory and vicarious copyright infringement. It knew specific infringing files sat on the system, it could police them, and it benefited from the traffic they brought. The court also rejected the argument that users swapping complete songs were making fair use.

A revised injunction followed on March 5, 2001, requiring Napster to keep infringing titles out of its index. Filtering a catalogue named by its own users was never going to work, and in July 2001 Napster switched the network off. A partial settlement that September committed the company to pay copyright owners for past unauthorized use, Chapter 11 protection followed in June 2002, and what survived the bankruptcy was a trademark.

Is Napster still around in 2026?

Not as a music service. The original file-sharing network has been gone since July 2001, and the name has changed hands repeatedly since, most recently in March 2025, when a technology company bought the brand and renamed itself Napster Corporation that May.

In the first days of January 2026 that owner shut the Napster streaming app down without warning and pointed subscribers at third-party tools to export their playlists. The notice that replaced the catalogue told users plainly that Napster was no longer a music streaming service, and the company now sells AI software and animated chat personas instead.

So the honest answer to the question people type is that Napster in 2026 is a trademark, not a place to play music. Nothing in the current product line connects to the client Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker released in 1999 beyond the word on the door, and the catalogue the app once carried is gone.

Is the music industry bigger now than before Napster?

In plain dollars it looks that way, and the comparison needs care. US recorded music revenue reached a record $11.5 billion in 2025, up 3.1% on 2024, in the year-end report the Recording Industry Association of America published in March 2026. Paid subscriptions supplied $6.38 billion of that, or 55.3% of every dollar the industry earned, across 106.5 million accounts in the United States. Vinyl passed $1 billion for the first time since 1983.

"The last 20 years have been marked by unprecedented transformation for recorded music," RIAA chairman and chief executive Mitch Glazier said in that report, pointing to streaming and the return of vinyl.

The measuring stick moved, though. The RIAA switched its headline figure from retail value to wholesale value during 2025, so $11.5 billion wholesale is not the same kind of number as $14.6 billion retail in 1999. Its 2023 headline, on the old basis, was $17.12 billion. Inflation pulls the same way across 26 years, which is why the 1999 peak still stands in real terms. The conversion sits in our piece on what a 1999 dollar is worth today.

What did listeners actually take from Napster?

The expectation, not the software. Before 1999, access to music was rationed by what a store stocked and what you could afford that week. Napster replaced that with a search box, and once people had used one, no service could go back to selling scarcity.

Everything that has made money since has had to match that standard first and charge second. The iTunes Store did it by selling songs one at a time. Subscription streaming did it by dropping ownership altogether: pay monthly, get the catalogue, keep nothing.

The trade was real, and artists carry most of it. Album sales paid out in lumps per unit. Streaming pays fractions per play, which rewards deep catalogues and constant releases over one strong record. Touring, merchandise and sync licensing now carry weight they never had to carry in 1999. Vinyl came back as a collector's format in the same stretch, which says something about what people will still pay to own.

The business survived the thing it said would end it, but not in the form it was defending. The labels won in court in 2001 and still lost the product argument, which is the part worth remembering.
